Measure progress. When we start out, our journey from unproven idea to validated business model, in the beginning, we really don't know what's going to work, and we have high uncertainty. As innovators, it's our responsibility to reduce that risk in order to have high certainty that our idea works. But how do you measure whether or not you've reduced that risk? Reducing risk is not a means of counting how many experiments you've run, how many tasks you've completed, how many Trello cards you've moved, or how many interviews you've conducted. Testing is not about measuring velocity. We often mistake velocity, which is a measure for the amount of work a team can tackle during a period of time as a means to measure for progress, meaning how much risk we've reduced. There is no direct correlation between the number of experiments you're running and the amount of risk you've reduced. What matters instead is the amount of evidence you gathered for each of your hypotheses, how strong the evidence is, and what it tells you about your hypothesis. Another way to look at it is this. Each building block of your business model and value proposition canvas represents an area of risk. You will have hypotheses for each area that you need to test in order to validate or invalidate them. Your job is to derisk each building block of your business model to gain high certainty. In order to measure progress, we can break down our business model into four different risk categories, desirability, feasibility, viability, and adaptability. We use these four risk categories to test if a new business model works or not. And each of these four risk categories represents roughly twenty five percent of the total risk, and we can measure progress by reducing the risk of each of these categories. Each of these four risk categories consists of a number of hypotheses. This means the more hypotheses we validate from each risk category, the more we're reducing our risk and the more progress we're making with our business idea towards validated business case. Let's not forget, though, while twenty five percent represents the maximum risk each category can have, it is not an absolute number. And that's because each idea has different starting points. For example, if your idea targets an existing customer segment, you already have some knowledge about this risk category. Your desirability risk will be lower, meaning you've already made some progress towards validated business model. If your idea is based on a new product with new key resources, your feasibility risk will be at its maximum, which is twenty five percent. It means you didn't make any progress yet in your feasibility risk category. Ultimately, the more risk you're reducing in each of the four categories, the more progress you're making.
