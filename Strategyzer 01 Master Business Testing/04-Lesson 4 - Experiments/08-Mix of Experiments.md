Mix of experiments. We often observe teams that are satisfied with running just one experiment for their most important hypothesis, but a single data source is not enough evidence to reduce the risk and uncertainty of a hypothesis that could kill your business idea. And because we recommend that you start with cheap and quick experiments, the challenge here is often that they likely produce evidence that isn't that strong. The farther along you are on your journey and the more resources you're spending, the stronger you want your evidence to be. We also observe that some teams favor specific experiments. For example, some teams only do interviews. The problem here is that interviews only tell you what people say, but not what they will actually do. What customers say might be substantially different from what they actually end up doing. Other teams don't like talking to customers, so they are happier doing AB testing. This provides you with evidence about what people will do, but you won't have any insights into why they do something. You need to have evidence for both. So instead, what you need is a mix of experiments for the same hypothesis, which provide you with stronger evidence over time and with a mix of data points. You will ultimately have more confidence in the success of your hypothesis. Let's look at an example from Life Tree Bank and their business idea of a pop up store concept for generation zed. At the beginning of their journey, they ran fifty customer discovery interviews to test their most important hypothesis. We believe that young adults between eighteen to twenty four will indicate a preference for access to banking anywhere and anytime in comparison to going to traditional bank branches. The evidence from this initial experiment supported that hypothesis, but evidence from interviews isn't strong enough to justify building an actual pop up store just yet. So they paired it up with an AB test to learn how many users prefer a pop up store over a retail concept. This gives more visibility of what people do versus what they say. The evidence from this experiment also supported their hypothesis. And while evidence from an AB test is stronger than evidence from an interview, it's not yet enough to build an actual pop up store. Because the AB test allowed LifeGibank to collect email addresses, they then sent invites to those who signed up to test a real life prototype consisting of a table with a bit of branding in a shopping mall. This type of experiment has the potential to produce strong evidence in favor of Life Tree Banks hypothesis. Evidence from this experiment did not support their most important hypothesis. It means after running a mix of three different experiments that produced different strengths of evidence for the same hypothesis, the innovation team of LifeGibank had to go back to the drawing board to think about why the people who signed up during the AB test did not show up for the prototype in the mall. It doesn't mean that they have to completely drop their idea of a pop up store, but perhaps the location was wrong. Did they select the wrong audience? LifeGree Bank needs to identify the barriers that stopped people from showing up in the mall before continuing testing. Imagine if they had stopped experimenting after running just the interviews and had decided that the evidence is strong enough to justify building a full fledged pop up store in a mall, they would have wasted huge amounts of money. You can avoid that by running a mix of experiments for the same hypothesis that produces increasing strength of evidence.
