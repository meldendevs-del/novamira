Choose the right metrics. After writing a strong hypothesis and designing a strong experiment, you also need to think about what you want to measure during your experiments. You need to make smart decisions here because this data you collect as evidence will be the basis upon which you make decisions. It's all connected. That's why it's important to make sure we're measuring the right things, and this needs to be done before we run an experiment. While the metric itself depends on the experiment, there are a couple of things to watch out for. First, don't use vanity metrics. A vanity metric is the kind of metric that makes you look good. They're exciting because they demonstrate that we appear to be improving. For example, it can be a great ego boost if millions of people follow your brand's Twitter account after a campaign and seem interested in your new business idea. But if those follows don't translate into new sales, that doesn't mean that much. Let's unpack it a bit. Our hypothesis could be we believe that increasing our Twitter followers will result in more sales. To verify that, we will run a Twitter campaign and measure how many people follow us on Twitter. We are right if our campaign results in more than two million followers. We can see how this metric, amount of followers, is not connected to the hypothesis. It doesn't prove that we will have more sales, and therefore, we can't make any decisions and we just wasted our time and money. We're no bit closer to validating the success of our business idea. Instead of vanity metrics, use actionable metrics. Actionable metrics have a clear connection to the hypothesis. In our case here, we might change the metric to measuring an increase in our conversion rate and set the threshold to a one percent increase. Because the metric ties back to the hypothesis, it allows us to drive action at the end of our experiment. It informs us clearly about next steps. Also, this metric allows a comparison between old and new. We can show how something improved or increased over time, which brings us to the next point. Try to avoid absolute metrics and instead use comparative metrics where possible. Absolute metrics alone usually don't prove much. If we go back to our Twitter example, here the numbers just go up from one point five to two point three million followers. But having eight hundred thousand more followers doesn't tell me if that campaign was better than a previous campaign in regards to sales or better than doing anything at all. That's why we want to use comparative metrics to learn if something changes in comparison to something else. In this case, what's critical for our business model is conversion. Did did the campaign result in increase of our conversion rate? If we switch our lens from creating more followers to increasing our conversion rate instead, we generate concrete business results, in this case, more sales. This will likely also impact how you design your Twitter campaign. So, ideally, use ratios for comparative metrics. Typical ratio metrics have percentages to show that something increased over time.
