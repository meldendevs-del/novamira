Presentation best practices. Pitching your business idea to a committee can be a daunting experience. Within only a few minutes, you need to convince a team of strangers to pursue your idea for which you've worked so hard in the past few months. So So we put together a few best practices to help you with that. The first one is a little bit of a repetition, but it's important. Don't just talk about why your business idea is great. This is a mistake that, in particular, beginners make because they don't understand the power of their evidence. What you should do instead is focus on the evidence and how much progress you've made towards validated business model backed up by solid data. Nothing is more convincing. To demonstrate that, don't just list your experiments and the evidence they produced. Your audience will get lost in a maze of experiments not being able to follow your decision making process. Instead, you want to explain the logic of your decision making step by step so your audience can follow how you use the experiments and evidence and insights to develop your idea. When people get nervous or haven't memorized their presentation, they often launch into what we call blah blah blah. It means they end up talking about things that don't matter to the audience in too much detail. We develop the blah blah card for our workshops and presentations and encourage our audience to hold them up when things get boring. Instead, you want to captivate your audience by telling the story of where you started out, where you are today, and what you still need to do. Include an interesting personal story to get the attention of your audience or ask a question that'll get the light bulbs going in their heads. Was there a moment your idea got killed by an invalidated hypothesis? How did you solve that problem? What was the climax of your research? Another one is don't commit cognitive murder. Revealing too much information at once is one of the biggest mistakes we see when people present their ideas. Passionate users of the business model canvas often show their audience an entire canvas all at once and then try to explain it. But brains cannot handle the density of this information and simply shut off. So instead, use short and descriptive words in a big font, like a thirty point font, and repeat them throughout your presentation. They become like hooks for your audience's brains. So be sure to think of a few purposeful and catchy words. Also, create a visual and engaging presentation. Use videos, quotes, pictures of your MVPs, pictures of conversations with your customers. People learn best when the information they hear is supported by visuals. Also, build up your slides over time to reveal information one piece at a time so that what you're saying is always supported by what people see on the screen. One of the worst things you could do is to not practice your pitch. You won't be able to anticipate possible questions your audience will ask you, and you will look less confident if you need to read from the screen. If you come prepared, you will exude confidence, which will have a big impact on how the judging panel thinks about your idea. Videotape yourself presenting the pitch until you can deliver it without hesitation by heart and full of confidence. But most importantly, never forget, the purpose of your presentation is to present evidence that your business idea can work.
