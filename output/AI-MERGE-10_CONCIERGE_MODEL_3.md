## 1. What a Business Model Really Is

### A multi-faceted concept

**What it is.** "Business model" has no single correct definition. The concept adapts to the context it sits in and to the objective of the person using it, and it is fine to have multiple definitions based on each practitioner's goal. A concept carried across several fields will take on different meanings.

**Why it matters.** If you know which meaning someone is using, you avoid talking past them (an investor asking about your "business model" usually means something narrower than a strategist does), and you avoid adopting a narrow meaning that limits your own options.

The main meanings in circulation:

- **Analysts use business models to produce financial analyses.** Business modeling is a technique to dissect any organization and business, used by analysts and business people trying to understand those businesses. Business and financial analysts use it to better understand tech companies, in order to give investment recommendations, financial reviews and investment advice.
- **Academics study business models to classify things.** For academics a business model might be just a holistic way to describe a business. Their purpose can seem more rigorous than an entrepreneur's: the academic has to prove the business has certain features that make it different from or similar to other businesses, and from those features derive classifications. As those classifications become more and more complex, they only live in "theory land". The research does not necessarily have a practical purpose; its goal is uncovering universal classification systems for things in the real world, and as such it can lose practical application.
- **Most people confuse business models with business plans** (see the myths below).
- **Startups confuse business models with monetization strategies** (see the myths below).
- **Entrepreneurs use business modeling as an experimentation mindset** to test assumptions quickly and cheaply (section 3).

### Definitions over time

The concept evolved through a series of landmark statements. Keep them verbatim, since each marks a shift in thinking:

- **Michael Porter, 1979 (strategy era, competitor focus):** "The state of competition in an industry depends on five basic forces... The collective strength of these forces determines the ultimate profit potential of an industry. It ranges from intense in industries like tires, metal cans, and steel, where no company earns spectacular returns on investment, to mild in industries like oil field services and equipment, soft drinks, and toiletries, where there is room for quite high returns." (Harvard Business Review, "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy", March 1979.)
- **HBR, 1993:** business modeling as competitive advantage ("Strategy and the Art of Reinventing Value").
- **Amit and Zott, 2001:** "A business model depicts the content, structure, and governance of transactions designed so as to create value through the exploitation of business opportunities."
- **Marc Andreessen (a16z):** "The only thing that matters is product/market fit." His description of what product/market fit feels like: "The customers are buying the product just as fast as you can make it, or usage is growing just as fast as you can add more servers. Money from customers is piling up in your company checking account. You're hiring sales and customer support staff as fast as you can. Reporters are calling because they've heard about your hot new thing and they want to talk to you about it. You start getting entrepreneur of the year awards from Harvard Business School. Investment bankers are staking out your house. You could eat free for a year at Buck's."
- **Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur, 2010:** "A business model describes the rationale of how an organization creates, delivers, and captures value."
- **Steve Blank, 2013:** "A Lean Startup favors experimentation over elaborate planning, customer feedback over intuition, and iterative design over traditional 'big design up front' development."
- **Fred Wilson, AVC, July 2019:** "I believe business model innovation is more disruptive than technical innovation."
- **Business Model Navigator (BMI Lab):** "A business model is an holistic, repeatable and scalable way to create and capture value." This is the working definition the rest of this reference uses.

The phrase itself barely existed before the second half of the twentieth century. On the Google Books Ngram Viewer, "business model" is flat from 1800 onward, first appears around the 1950s-60s, and then rises almost vertically from the 1990s into the 2000s.

### Where scientists use labs, entrepreneurs use business model experiments

"Where scientists use labs to test their hypotheses through experimentation, entrepreneurs build business model experiments to test their business ideas in the real world." This is the core reframing: a business model is not a document but a testable set of assumptions.

### Product, business model, organization: three nested layers

A company can be pictured as three concentric circles: **Product** at the center, wrapped by the **Business Model**, wrapped by **Organizational Design/Culture**. The product alone is not the business. The business model is the layer that makes the product commercially viable, and the organization and culture are what let the business model be executed and changed.

### Busting the myths

**Myth: a business model is a business plan.**

A business model is not a business plan. For most people who have not studied the topic, "business model" resonates with "business plan". Google shows this: typing "how to ... business model" suggests "how to create a business model", "how to make a business model", "how to write a business model", "how to build a business model", "how to develop a business model", "how to make a business model for a startup", "how to describe a business model", "how to make business model canvas", "how to design a winning business model" and "how to use business model canvas". Clicking the suggested "how to write a business model" returns (about 523,000,000 results) a featured snippet titled "3 rules for writing a business plan" (keep it short, know your audience, don't be intimidated, executive summary, opportunity, execution, company and management summary, financial plan) and a top result "How to Write a Business Plan [Updated for 2019] | Bplans".

Why Google does this: Google is a commercial search engine that adapts to the search behavior of billions of people. If collectively people think a business model is a business plan, Google may make that true in its results. The consequence for an entrepreneur searching for help: either you are lucky and find the right resource, or you end up writing a hundred-page business plan that won't help much with your business. If anything, it will prevent you from starting it, because you start making things more complicated than they should be.

**Myth: a marketing plan is a business model.** In a marketing plan you structure how you make your clients or potential customers perceive you. That is not a business model.

**Myth: a business model is how you make money.**

How you make money is part of your revenue generation strategy, which is one building block of a business model, but it is not the business model.

Startups make this confusion constantly. Example: Airbnb's early pitch deck "Business Model" slide said only "We take a 10% commission on each transaction", then showed 10.6M trips with AB&B (share of market) → $20 average fee ($70/night @ 3 nights) → $200M revenue (2008-2011). Another example: WeWork's pre-IPO filing, under "Our Business Model", describes only revenue generation: "We monetize our global platform through a variety of solutions, mainly by selling memberships but also by providing ancillary value-added products and services to our members and extending our platform beyond offering workspace. Currently, we derive the majority of our revenue from recurring monthly membership fees, which accounted for approximately 83% of total revenue during the six months ended June 30, 2019... Our membership and service revenue also includes revenue from business services purchased by our members, such as conference rooms and printing credits, and from commissions on the sale to our members of additional products and services offered by us or in conjunction with partners, which, together, accounted for approximately 5% of total revenue during the six months ended June 30, 2019. The remaining 12% of total revenue during the six months ended June 30, 2019 represented revenue we generated from additional products and services that we deliver through our suite of We Company offerings. These services, which are dedicated to improving our members' experience, allow us to further connect with our members, driving higher retention, which attracts valuable third-party partners who can utilize our platform to reach our large member base."

This is not wrong in context. Startup pitches and financial forms are often also marketing tools meant to communicate and simplify a concept, so if investors ask about your business model and mean "how do you make money", it is fine to simplify. **The pitfall:** if you as the entrepreneur believe a business model is only how you make money, you limit your options, because you will spend all day thinking about monetization strategies instead of taking a more holistic and strategic approach.

Business modeling is far more than monetization. It is how you make a great product or service so that customers keep coming back; how you make that product or service scalable; how you keep making financial sense of the business over time; and the value proposition you deliver to key partners, which are a crucial ingredient of success. Business model innovation can be about changing how you charge customers, but it can equally be about other critical aspects of the business that let you scale.

**Myth: technological innovation is business model innovation.** Technological innovation is not business model innovation. Technology might help build an innovative business model, but a business model starts from problem-solution fit.

**Myth: the best product wins.** When you go online looking for something you are not sure of, you probably land on a white page with a small search box: Google. You get there because Google is an incredible product that finds anything on the web at super-fast speed. But is it the best product, and how do we define best? Google gives relevant results to any question, but it also benefits from network effects: one reason it is good at intercepting search intent is that billions of people use it every day. It also has drawbacks. In one experiment, an SEO expert tried to rank a site written in Latin (a language used by ancient Romans and no longer in use), and it ranked successfully. Google is the most widely used search engine on earth partly because of its distribution strategy: since its scale-up phase, Google aggressively acquired deals that made it the tech giant it is. Few people realize this, and it is easy, especially in tech, to think the best product wins. **A great product with a lack of distribution strategy won't go far.**

**Myth: technology is what gives a competitive advantage (first-mover advantage).** Conventional thinking says "be the first and you'll probably win over time", which is first-mover advantage. Peter Thiel (former CEO of PayPal) pointed out the opposite paradigm, especially in tech: the **last-mover advantage**. Companies that come later can win over existing organizations even when those were the first movers. Google and Facebook were not the first movers in search and social media, yet they dominated. What happened? Business model innovation.

**Myth: business model innovation is just about how you make money.** When Google came out of the Stanford dormitory where two Ph.D. students had invented it, it was a great search engine that many argue was 10x better than competitors. Yet it was not financially successful until, through a couple of years of trial and error, it designed an innovative business model. Google introduced an auction system for advertising aimed at removing the inefficiencies of how advertising had worked for decades. The auction itself was not the primary innovation: another search engine, Overture, was already doing it successfully. Google's innovation was adding a few critical parameters that decided which ads appeared at the top of its text-based ad results. It was not enough to offer a higher bid on a keyword; Google crossed the bid with other parameters so that the ads shown on top were those most relevant and with a higher click-through rate. It sounds trivial now, since the whole web after Google has been built on click-through rate, but it was not back then, and that innovation was critical to Google's hypergrowth, scale and domination (full case in section 11).

## 2. Why Business Model Innovation Matters Now

### The historic context: from competitor focus to customer focus

**What changed.** For decades strategy looked at competition, with frameworks like Porter's Five Forces. Business model innovation shifted the focus from competition to customers. Today business model innovation has become more important than technical innovation.

**Porter's Five Forces Model.** A model that helps organizations gain a better understanding of their industries and competition, published for the first time by Professor Michael Porter in his book "Competitive Strategy" in the 1980s. It breaks industries and markets down by analyzing them through five forces: competition in the industry (rivalry among existing competitors, at the center); potential of new entrants into the industry (threat of new entrants); power of suppliers (bargaining power of suppliers); power of customers (bargaining power of buyers); and threat of substitute products or services.

**Caveat on the shift.** Saying the focus has moved to customers does not mean you don't need to understand your competition. It means you start from customers and the problems they face, and only after that move to competition and the existing alternatives.

**Why the shift happened: the internet at scale.** A snapshot of internet activity (source: internetlivestats.com) shows the scale that made customer-centric, digital business models possible:

- 1,701,107,315 total number of websites

- 3,794,273,112 Google searches today
- 3,609,056 blog posts written today
- 428,777,573 tweets sent today
- 3,984,038,864 videos viewed today on YouTube
- 46,394,035 photos uploaded today on Instagram
- 77,912,756 Tumblr posts today

The word cloud that represents this new paradigm puts **customer** at the center, surrounded by service, relationship, satisfaction, loyalty, value, support, experience, care, help, process, marketing, information, sales and retention.

### Business model innovation creates competitive moats

**What it is.** Business model innovation is about increasing the success of an organization with existing products and technologies by crafting a compelling value proposition able to propel a new business model to scale up customers and create a lasting competitive advantage. An alternative phrasing: creating a compelling value proposition that allows an organization to scale up customers with a better operating model. It all starts by mastering the key customers.

**Why it works.** As technology becomes a commodity over time, creating a lasting advantage requires business model understanding, experimentation and execution. At its core, business model innovation is a subtle change. Because it is hard to dissect from the outside (in many cases it is detected only after an organization has achieved massive success), it is also hard to copy. In a world where technology has partly become a commodity, that difference is huge. What gives a competitive advantage is no longer technology alone, but technology paired with an innovative business model.

**Pitfall.** A business model is not static. Even though a business model creates a long-term competitive advantage, it requires continuous tweaking to make sure all its pieces come together. How Google's business model looks today will probably have evolved in a few years.

### Why incumbents miss the opportunity

Every new business model starts in a **gap**: between **existing players** and a **new value proposition** sits an **opportunity**, a point of customer dissatisfaction the incumbents are not serving. An incumbent leaves the gap open for one of three reasons:

- It does not see the opportunity.
- It can't see it.
- It does not align with its business model.

**Example: DuckDuckGo versus Google.** Google tracks users. Some users think "I want privacy from Google!" That is where Google's value proposition fails ("I can't give you privacy"). DuckDuckGo built a business model based on privacy, with the value proposition "I can surf the web by protecting my privacy!" It makes money in two ways that don't need tracking:

- **Untracked advertising:** when a user types a search, DuckDuckGo shows an ad based only on that search term. If you type "car", DuckDuckGo shows a car ad. That doesn't involve tracking because it is based on the keyword and not the person.
- **Untracked affiliate marketing:** when a user visits Amazon and eBay through DuckDuckGo and subsequently makes a purchase, DuckDuckGo receives a small commission. This mechanism operates anonymously, and no personally identifiable information is exchanged between DuckDuckGo and Amazon or eBay.

Google cannot easily copy this because privacy does not align with a business model built on tracking-powered advertising.

## 3. Business Model Innovation as Mindset, Framework and Toolset

### Business modeling is an experimentation mindset

**What it is.** Business model design is not about sketching a plan on a piece of paper but a mindset of experimentation. In business modeling you manufacture experiments (business models and business model variations) that let the entrepreneur test the assumptions around the business quickly, cheaply and with minimum effort. Business model innovation is at the same time a mindset, a framework and a set of tools for entrepreneurs to build relevant businesses in today's marketplace.

**Why it works.** Business modeling does three things for an entrepreneur: it gives a better understanding of the businesses around you; it gives insight into how companies work at a holistic level, which makes you a better digital entrepreneur; and it lets you test the assumptions around the business you are trying to build, reducing the time and money spent on a project that was doomed to fail.

**How to do it.**

1. List the assumptions your business depends on.
2. Start testing from the riskiest assumptions (as practitioners like Ash Maurya highlight): those for which the business might not become sustainable over time. Monetization strategy and key customer understanding are among the riskiest and need to be tested quickly.
3. Build one or more business model variations and design experiments against each. The **Business Model Innovation tree** shows the structure: Business Model Innovation branches into Business Model One (Experiment A, Experiment B) and Business Model Two (Experiment C, Experiment D).
4. Use the results to move the product or service in line with the market.

The workshop's map of the discipline puts **Mindset** first, then **Tools** (Business Model Canvas, Lean Canvas), then **Business Model Innovation** with its tree of models and experiments.

### An entrepreneur is not a scientist

An entrepreneur has different goals than a scientist. The scientist tries to uncover more universal truths. The entrepreneur needs business model experimentation to test assumptions, uncover market opportunities, reduce time to market and eventually build a valuable business. An entrepreneur is a market-driven animal: rather than starting from theories and running experiments to see if they are true, an entrepreneur starts from a problem and goes back to theory to understand the underlying assumptions preventing the business from succeeding. Once those assumptions have been streamlined, they can be tested, so the entrepreneur can move on and make the product or service fit the market.

### The questions to ask

There is no single path to business model innovation, but there are critical questions. To innovate a business model, think about how to tweak and redesign your value chain, cost structure, key partners and, in general, what can help you scale:

- How can I design a better value chain?
- Can I improve the existing cost structure?
- What is the distribution channel that can accelerate growth?
- Why is my company experiencing bottlenecks in certain areas?
- Is the organizational structure helping the company to grow as it should?

### Three paths toward business model innovation

Sometimes you can design a business model from your previous experience in an industry; other times you have to figure it out along the way. Three paths:

**Path 1: Engineer an innovative business model from scratch.**

*What it is.* Designing the business model deliberately before launch, using deep prior knowledge of the space. This is the work of a **Business Engineer**: a hybrid between a business administration and technology expert, a person with the business acumen and engineering abilities to understand a complex organization, devise solutions and work as a liaison between commercial and technical teams.

*Why it works.* As Reid Hoffman points out in his book Blitzscaling, business model innovation is a key ingredient of success, especially in digital, where countless companies offer innovative tools. Prior domain knowledge lets you design the model instead of stumbling onto it.

*Example.* Reid Hoffman: "This is what happened when I co-founded LinkedIn. The key business model innovations for LinkedIn, including the two-way nature of the relationships and filling professionals' need for a business-oriented online identity, didn't just happen organically." He used his understanding of the social networking world (he had founded a social network called SocialNet) to design LinkedIn's business model. LinkedIn was acquired in 2016 by Microsoft for $26.2 billion. The two-way nature is visible in LinkedIn's model: it serves **HR Managers** (LinkedIn Hiring) and **Professionals** (Learning & Development) on the same platform.

*Supporting data (LinkedIn profiles with the title "business engineer").* Top companies: IBM 41384, Accenture 33973, Microsoft 31266, Oracle 23065, Cisco 18572, Amazon 18398, Google 14344, Amazon Web Services 8613, Salesforce 7068, Facebook 5334. Top universities: University of Mumbai 38335, Stanford University 22114, University of California, Berkeley 21750, Massachusetts Institute of Technology 19595, Politecnico di Milano 16229, INSEAD 10884, Harvard Business School 10433, Politecnico di Torino 9140, Sapienza Università di Roma 5824. Top skills: engineering, business analysis, requirements analysis, project management, SQL, Java, Scrum, Mendix, Matlab, Microsoft SQL, business intelligence, software development, project engineering, requirements engineering, integration, business engineering, Unified Modeling Language, agile methodologies, lean manufacturing, AutoCAD, MySQL, XML, JavaScript, business process.

*Pitfall.* Designing a business model beforehand isn't always possible. In some cases you need to experiment, reiterate and find it.

**Path 2: Find an innovative business model along the way.**

*What it is.* Discovering the model through trial and error after the product exists.

*Example.* When Google was scaling up, it didn't figure it all out right away. Although it had incredible technology and product, it lagged in business model innovation. When it finally figured it out after a few trials and errors (Google was running out of investment money), it was a massive success. When it listed in 2004 and had to show its numbers, it already made over three billion in revenues, a 155x growth in about four years.

*How to do it.* Try and test many things before you can say you have a business model that scales sustainably and makes financial sense. If you find it, you have created a long-lasting competitive advantage.

**Path 3: Use business model innovation as a survival mechanism.**

*What it is.* An established leader experimenting with new models to protect itself from threats to its current one.

*Example.* Uber has been investing in self-driving cars since 2015. Why would a company dominating an entire space do this? First, if self-driving cars become mass-adopted, Uber would be out of business, so to thrive in the next era it needs to be on top of that game. Second, business model innovation: among its key partners, Uber has drivers across the world, and those drivers also pose a significant threat to Uber's success. Even if Uber pulls the plug on or spins off its self-driving business, the example shows a company that never stops experimenting. Business units like Uber Eats, Express Pool and Freight are all attempts to tweak an existing business model until it allows the company to become financially sustainable at massive scale.

### Is business model innovation for anyone?

In theory, yes. **Small-business example:** a small consulting business in a traditional industry where most competitors charge by the hour stops doing that and instead charges only a small retainer plus a success fee. This model might not be financially viable at the beginning, but it might wipe out competitors over time: with a larger customer base, the retainer becomes an essential revenue base and the success fees become the scalable part of the business.

**Pitfall.** In other cases business model innovation requires massive financial resources. A business that decides to dominate an existing industry by introducing an innovative business model growing at a reckless pace may attract investors looking for high returns, but it may also burn a lot of cash.

**Key takeaway.** Business model innovation is a popular topic and therefore surrounded by misconceptions. Business modeling has a meaning based on why you are using it; it is a dynamic concept, at the apex of its evolution, and can carry different meanings and interpretations.

## 4. Understanding Your Market Type

### The four types of start-ups, based on market type

**What it is.** Steve Blank's classification of start-ups by the kind of market they enter:

1. **Existing Market:** a market with well-defined competitors and customers.
2. **Resegmented Market:** a market dominated by one or a few key players.
3. **New Market:** a market where competitors do not exist and it's very hard to define the customer.
4. **Clone Market:** a market where, due to geographical or cultural barriers, a business model can be cloned and transposed.

### Why it matters to understand the market

**Why it works.** The market type changes the economics of almost everything downstream, so the same product needs a different plan depending on which of the four markets it enters.

**What the market type influences:**

1. **Market.** Depending on the market type, market conditions change: market size, cost of entry, launch type, competitive barriers, positioning.
2. **Sales.** The market type affects sales processes: sales model, margins, sales cycle.
3. **Finance.** The market type influences ongoing capital and time to profitability.

**How to use it.** Classify your idea into one of the four types first, then plan your market sizing, positioning, sales model and funding needs for that type rather than borrowing assumptions from a different one.

## 5. Finding Your Wedge and Your Niche

### The wedge: market opportunity × customer insight × business model

**What it is.** "Once you have a market, then you need to start creating a business around it. Depending on your skills/goals + market needs + customer needs, you can create a different business." The wedge is the point where three circles overlap: **Market Opportunity**, **Customer Insight** and **Business Model**.

**Why it works.** Inference: a market opportunity without customer insight produces products nobody wants; customer insight without a business model produces something loved but unprofitable; a business model without a real opportunity has nothing to monetize. Only the overlap gives you an entry point sharp enough to break in.

**How to do it.**

1. Identify the market opportunity.
2. Gather customer insight (the jobs, pains and gains of the customer).
3. Choose the business model that fits your skills and goals, the market need and the customer need.
4. Find the intersection: that is your wedge.
5. From the wedge, move to testing (section 7): "Once you have the Venn Diagram, you can now create your atomic unit of value (Testing)."

### The Bud Caddell diagram

**What it is.** A Venn diagram that helps you cut through the noise of understanding whether a business idea has potential for you. It lets you assess whether you want to deal with a problem, whether you can do it well, and whether people are willing to pay you for it. Based on a few questions, you can understand whether you're ready to launch a successful business in the long run.

**How to read it.** Three circles:

- **What we do well** ∩ **What we want to do** (but not paid) → **Learn to monetize**.
- **What we do well** ∩ **What we can be paid to do** (but don't want to) → **Learn to say no**.
- **What we want to do** ∩ **What we can be paid to do** (but don't do well) → **Learn to do this better**.
- All three → **Hooray!**

### Niche marketing strategy to scale

**What it is.** A niche marketing strategy looks at a subset of a market to build a business that reaches an audience where there is lower competition, strong demand and higher margins. A niche also lets you build an intimate relationship with that segment where larger brands can't.

**How to do it.** Look for the profitable niche at the intersection of **lower competition**, **strong demand** and **higher margins**. Win it, then expand outward from it (scaling up) into the larger market. Amazon's start in books (section 11) is the model: begin from one relatively smaller market, dominate it, then move on.

## 6. Business Model Types by Value per Customer

### B2B versus B2C, in descending order of value per customer

**What it is.** A ranked list of business model types, from the highest value per customer (top) to the lowest (bottom). "As you get lower down this list, you are less willing to pay for customers and more interested in seeing them naturally jump and get addicted to the product."

| Segment | Model | Pricing model | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| B2B | Enterprise | Single license per company | Docker, Cloudera, FireEye |
| B2B | Usage-Based | Cost per individual action on service | Google Maps API, Twilio, Sendgrid |
| B2B | SaaS | Subscription sold per user | DocuSign, Zapier, Salesforce |
| B2B | Services | Time of employees | TaskUs, Accenture, VaynerMedia |
| B2B/B2C (bridge) | Transactional | % of transactions enabled by service | Stripe, PayPal, Eventbrite |
| B2C | Product (Hardware) | Sell your own product | GoPro, FitBit, Allbirds |
| B2C | Subscription | Subscription for service or software | Tinder, Birchbox, BlueApron |
| B2C | Marketplace | Connect buyers and sellers | Etsy, Kickstarter, AirBnB |
| B2C | (E-)Commerce | Offer a selection of products | Zappos, Honest Company |
| B2C | Ads | Advertisers buy special access | TikTok, Reddit, YouTube |

**Why it matters.** Inference: value per customer dictates how you acquire customers. At the top (enterprise), each customer is worth a lot, so you can afford direct sales and high acquisition cost. At the bottom (ads, commerce), each customer is worth little, so growth has to come from the product itself: organic spread, habit and addiction rather than paid acquisition.

### The riskiest hypotheses for each business model

**What it is.** "There is a common question for almost every company within a certain business model. Your goal is to de-risk these as much as possible before you start." Each model has two riskiest hypotheses, in order:

| Model | Riskiest Hypothesis #1 | Riskiest Hypothesis #2 |
|---|---|---|
| Enterprise | Will they buy? | Will they pay enough for profit? |
| Usage-Based | Will they use? | At what volume? |
| SaaS | Does this meet a need? | Will they keep using? |
| Services | Can I sell? | Can I scale? |
| Transactional | Will they use? | At what volume? |
| Product (Hardware) | Will they buy? | At what volume? |
| Subscription | Will they buy? | Will they churn? |
| Marketplace | Will buyers want sellers' products? | Will buyers repeat purchase? |
| (E-)Commerce | Will they buy? | Will they repeat purchase? |
| Ads | Will they use? | Will they get addicted? |

**How to use it.** Find your model in the table, take hypothesis #1 as the first thing your MVP must prove, and hypothesis #2 as the second. Do not spend effort on anything else until these two are de-risked.

### The rank, learn, test method

**What it is.** "Once you have the Venn Diagram, you can now create your atomic unit of value (Testing). Your goal with this is to test your riskiest assumptions." A three-step loop.

**Why it works.** Inference: you have limited time and money. Ranking forces you to spend them on the assumption that would kill the business if wrong, and focusing the test on one atomic unit of value keeps the test small enough to run fast and clear enough to read.

**How to do it.**

1. **Rank.** Write your highest-impact assumptions in rank order.
2. **Learn.** Learn what you can about each highest-impact assumption and come up with a "test" that enables you to evaluate it and learn more.
3. **Test.** The test should focus on an "atomic unit of value": the most specific spearpoint that provides all of the force necessary towards the value you think users really care about.

**Example.** Inference (applying the method to section 6): for a SaaS idea, the ranked list starts with "Does this meet a need?" then "Will they keep using?" (section 6). The atomic unit of value is the single piece of the product that delivers the core benefit; the test delivers only that piece, by whatever means is cheapest, to see if users care.

## 8. Minimum Viable Products

### What an MVP is

"Minimum Viable Product = the least work you have to do to prove your hypothesis." Your MVP needs to focus on just one thing: the goal. Follow the CHESS model so you don't waste time on everything else.

### MVPs should be CHESS

**What it is.** A five-part checklist for keeping an MVP minimal:

- **C**heap: do not spend money on an MVP.
- **H**aphazard: it is not supposed to look good.
- **E**asy: take 1-2 weeks to build.
- **S**pecific: test 1-2 hypotheses.
- **S**imple: very few features, clear value prop.

**Why it works.** Inference: each letter removes a way founders waste effort: money (cheap), polish (haphazard), build time (easy), scope creep in what is being tested (specific) and scope creep in the product (simple). Whatever does not serve the one or two hypotheses being tested is waste.

**Pitfall.** Inference: if your MVP costs real money, looks finished, takes months, tests many things at once or has many features, it is no longer an MVP; it is a product built on untested assumptions.

### Build, Measure, Learn

**What it is.** "Within each section of the ideation/exploration diagram, you are constantly testing, building and iterating. You try something, you put it out there, then you change it and improve it."

**How to do it.**

1. **Build:** write down a thesis.
2. Create goals that evaluate the thesis.
3. **Measure:** was the thesis right?
4. Ask why or why not.
5. **Learn:** what about the thesis needs to change?
6. Repeat.

Each turn of the loop narrows the gap between your thesis and reality.

### Different types of MVPs

1. Marketing Test
2. Build a user base first
3. Wizard of Oz
4. Concierge Model
5. No-code product

After any of them comes **MVP Feedback**: collecting and acting on what the MVP taught you. (The sources name the first three and the fifth without explaining them; the Concierge Model is covered in full in section 9.)

## 9. The Concierge Model (Stripe and Sprig)

### What it is

Inference (the sources define it only through the Stripe and Sprig examples): a concierge MVP delivers the value proposition to the customer by hand, doing the work for them, instead of building the technology that will eventually automate it. The customer gets the real outcome; the founder gets to learn exactly what needs building before building it.

### Why it works

The concierge model de-risks the riskiest hypothesis "What do I build?" (Stripe slide). Inference for the rest of this paragraph: When the product is technically complex, the biggest danger is spending months building the wrong technology. Doing the job manually for real customers shows you which parts of the product actually matter and which are irrelevant, at almost no engineering cost. It also tests the riskiest business hypotheses (will they use it, can we deliver at a profitable price) directly with paying customers.

### Case 1: Stripe, "do it for the customer"

**Situation.** Stripe was a payments processing startup taking on an extremely complex B2B problem. It had to build a lot of technology to make it work. Its biggest concern was building the wrong tech. So it had a simple challenge: how do we de-risk our time and make sure we build the right thing?

**Riskiest hypothesis:** What do I build?

**What they did.** "To solve their problem, they did the most ghetto MVP I've heard of. They literally went to startups' offices and actually implemented Stripe for them, instead of building the implementation themselves." At the time, the product was presented as **/dev/payments**, with a simple landing page: "Payment Processing Done Right.", three feature blocks ("Sane, simple, open-source APIs.", "Immediate sign-up", "Powerful analytics") and a "Try it now" button with "No sign-up required."

**Caveat.** The slide adds: "Obviously this can prevent you from learning what people" (the sentence is cut off in the source). Inference: doing the integration for customers can hide how hard it would be for them to do it themselves, so what you learn about self-serve usage is limited.

### Case 2: Sprig, a full concierge example

**Product thesis.** "Using GPS-managed drivers, Sprig will provide healthy meals to customers for $15 in 15 minutes."

**The question the MVP must answer.** "Will this work? Can we deliver food at a profitable price point?"

**Features that matter (test these):**

- Delivery model (can good food be delivered in 15 minutes)
- Costs (need to find out what the underlying cost structure is)
- Quality (how will the food look when it arrives?)

**Features that don't matter (skip these in the MVP):**

- App for ordering
- Delivery algorithm
- Full kitchen setup
- Inventory and order management software
- Driver dispatch / GPS (didn't even use "Find My iPhone")
- Text message / order confirmation system

**Lesson.** Inference: every feature that makes the eventual product scale (apps, algorithms, dispatch, confirmations) was removed. Only the three things that decide whether the business works (speed, cost, quality) were tested, and they were tested by humans doing the work manually.

### How to run a concierge MVP

Inference: these steps generalize the structure of the Sprig slide.

1. Write your product thesis in one sentence (who, what, price, speed).
2. Rewrite it as the question that decides whether the business works (usually "can we deliver this at a profitable price?").
3. Split your feature list into "features that matter" (they answer that question) and "features that don't matter" (they only make it scale).
4. Deliver the value manually for real customers, covering only the features that matter.
5. Measure the delivery, cost and quality results, then decide what to build (Build, Measure, Learn).

### Pitfalls

- Inference: the manual delivery does not scale; it is a learning tool, not the business.
- Because you do the work for the customer, you may not learn what they would do on their own (the Stripe caveat above).

## 10. Tools and Frameworks

### The Business Model Canvas, the Value Proposition Canvas and the Lean Canvas together

The toolkit combines three canvases: **The Business Model Canvas** (Strategyzer), the **Value Proposition Canvas** used for **Customer Insights** (Strategyzer), and the **Lean Canvas** (Lean Canvas is adapted from The Business Model Canvas and is licensed under the Creative Commons Attribution-Share Alike 3.0 Un-ported License).

### Lean Canvas: the order in which to fill it

**What it is.** A one-page business model for startups (source: blog.leanstack.com). Each box, with its prompt:

- **Problem:** list your customers' top 3 problems.
- **Existing Alternatives:** list how these problems are solved today.
- **Solution:** outline a possible solution for each problem.
- **Key Metrics:** list key numbers telling how your business is doing today.
- **Unique Value Proposition:** single, clear, compelling message that turns an unaware visitor into an interested prospect.
- **High-Level Concept:** list your X for Y analogy (e.g., YouTube = Flickr for videos).
- **Unfair Advantage:** something that can't be easily copied or bought.
- **Channels:** list your path to customers.
- **Customer Segments:** list your target customers and users.
- **Early Adopters:** list characteristics of your ideal customer.
- **Cost Structure:** list your fixed and variable costs.
- **Revenue Streams:** list your sources of revenue.

**How to fill it (numbered order):**

1. Customer Segments
2. Problem
3. Unique Value Proposition
4. Solution
5. (Channels is not numbered in the source image. Unverified: it is step 5 in the standard Lean Canvas order.)
6. Revenue Streams
7. Cost Structure
8. Key Metrics
9. Unfair Advantage

**Start with problem and customer segments.** Fill these two boxes first, as a pair, using four questions:

- Who is the customer?
- What are your early adopter's top 3 problems?
- How do they solve the problem today? (existing alternatives)
- How will you qualify/identify early adopters?

### Value Proposition Canvas: two ways to start

**What it is.** The Value Proposition Canvas has a value map (products and services, gain creators, pain relievers) on the left and a customer profile (customer jobs, gains, pains) on the right. You can start from either side (source: Strategyzer, "Starting with the customer: value proposition design").

**Start from customer insights.** Begin on the customer side: collect jobs, pains and gains (sticky notes labeled Job, Pain, Gain) and feed them into the customer profile, then design the value map to fit.

**Start from an asset or opportunity.** Begin on the value map side: start from something you already have, a **tech**, a **capability** or a **patent**, and push it into the products and services, then look for customers whose jobs, pains and gains it fits.

### Blitzscaling Business Model Innovation Canvas

**What it is.** A canvas that checks whether a business model can scale extremely fast, organized around four key growth factors and two key growth limiters.

**The 4 key growth factors:**

- **Market size:** How large is the market you're targeting when Blitzscaling? Is it really reachable?
- **Distribution:** Are there existing networks you can leverage on? What viral loops can you create to spread your product/service quickly and at scale?
- **High gross margins:** When you grow your revenues do you generate larger amounts of cash available to finance growth?
- **Network effects:** Is each additional user joining in bringing a positive effect to the whole platform?

**The 2 key growth limiters:** the first is **lack of product/market fit**: Is the market satisfied with your product/service? If not, what's missing? (The second limiter is not legible in the source.)

### Pirate Metrics: the AARRR funnel

**What it is.** Venture capitalist Dave McClure coined the acronym AARRR, a simplified model that shows which metrics and channels to look at at each stage of the users' path toward becoming customers and referrers of a brand.

| Stage | Question | Channels / tools |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition | How do potential customers get to know us? | SEO, SEM, social, blog, email, PR, affiliates, BizDev, apps and widgets, TV and more |
| Activation | How do potential customers get to try our product? | Homepage, landing page, product features |
| Retention | How many potential customers will stick around? | Emails and alerts, system events and time-based features, blogs, content |
| Revenue | How many will actually become customers? | Ads, lead gen, BizDev, subscriptions and more |
| Referral | Will those customers invite other potential customers? | Campaigns, contests, emails and widgets |

Referral loops back to acquisition.

### Eric Ries' three engines of growth

**What it is.** In The Lean Startup, Eric Ries defined the engine of growth as "the mechanism that startups use to achieve sustainable growth." He described sustainable growth with a simple rule: "new customers come from the actions of past customers." Each engine is measured and tracked by a few key metrics, and together they drive Lean Startup growth:

- **Sticky engine:** customer acquisition rate, churn rate, customer retention rate.
- **Viral engine:** viral coefficient.
- **Paid engine:** customer lifetime value versus cost per acquisition.

### Ramen profitability

**What it is.** Serial entrepreneur and venture capitalist Paul Graham popularized the term. "Ramen profitable means a startup makes just enough to pay the founders' living expenses." It is a different form of profitability from what startups have traditionally aimed for: traditional profitability means a big bet is finally paying off, whereas the main importance of ramen profitability is that it buys you time.

**Why it works.** Founders can finally pay their living expenses through the startup's revenue generation. This lets founders buy time to keep scaling their startup, and it also lets them leverage their found profitability to scout a better deal from investors, allowing the startup to move to the next stage of growth.

### Bootstrapping

**What it is.** The general concept of bootstrapping is "a self-starting process that is supposed to proceed without external input." In business, bootstrapping means financing the growth of the company from the available cash flows produced by a viable business model.

**How the cycle works (numbered as in the source):**

1. Customers
2. Organic growth
3. Cash flows
4. Reinvested in the business
5. Viable business model
6. Better customers
7. Bootstrapping (the loop repeats, now with better customers)

### Freemium: does it make sense? (decision flowchart)

A freemium pattern can also be used as a growth tool (to the extent the company uses it to amplify its brand) and as a marketing funnel where enough non-paying customers can be converted into customers. Use this workflow to decide whether to implement it:

1. **Do we have extra resources to sustain a free offering?**
   - No → focus on mastering your key customers.
   - Yes → go to 2.
2. **Will the freemium offering cannibalize the existing key customers?**
   - Yes → focus on mastering your key customers.
   - No → go to 3.
3. **Is the freemium offering in line with my business model?**
   - Yes → define a solid freemium offering → measure impact on existing key customers → if not affected and getting viral growth → **Go!**
   - No → go to 4.
4. **Do we have alternative growth tools?**
   - Yes → develop alternative growth tools.
   - No → consider a free offering with complementary features.

### Business model patterns

**What it is.** A business model pattern is a blueprint for creating new ideas and achieving business model innovation as a means to create a lasting competitive advantage. The BMI Lab's Business Model Navigator identifies 60 business model patterns, from the research of Oliver Gassmann, Karolin Frankenberger and Michaela Csik.

**Caveat.** There isn't a single way to define business model patterns, and in some cases a pattern can also be a growth tool or a revenue strategy (Freemium is the example above). Without getting bogged down in definitions and classifications, what matters is knowing how to use validated business systems and heuristics that help grow your business.

### Netflix

**Business model.** Netflix is a subscription-based business model making money with three simple plans (basic, standard and premium, from $7.99 to $15.99) giving access to stream series, movies and shows. The company is profitable, yet it runs on negative cash flows due to upfront cash paid for content licensing and original content production.

**The innovation.** People hated Blockbuster's late fees. Netflix could not compete with the established Blockbuster head-on, so it rethought how it delivered the service: for a fixed monthly fee, people could rent as many movies as they wanted, with no late fees. It initially found the perfect medium for this new subscription model in DVDs. The new DVD technology let it deliver a better service, but Netflix had to wait more than a decade before streaming picked up: its founder and CEO set out to wait for Moore's law to make streaming possible. Today streaming is Netflix's key delivery channel. Netflix also moved the needle of "culture creation" from Hollywood to Silicon Valley for the first time, and today invests billions in original content, which is what makes it so successful.

- Free cash flow ($ billion): 2016: -1.65; 2017: -2; 2018: -3.

### Amazon

**Business model.** Amazon has a diversified business model. In 2018 it posted over $232 billion in revenues and over $10 billion in net profits. Online stores contributed nearly 52% of revenues, followed by physical stores, third-party seller services, Amazon AWS, subscription services and advertising revenues. It started in 1994 as a bookstore and soon became "the everything store". Its physical stores already generated over five billion dollars in revenues in 2017. Amazon Prime (a subscription service) plays a crucial role, as it makes customers spend more and become more loyal to the platform. AWS is a world leader and a high-margin business, and Amazon also has an advertising business worth a few billion dollars, so the mix looks like many companies in one. Amazon measures its success through customer experience obsession, lowering prices, stable tech infrastructure and free cash flow generation.

**The innovation: start from a niche, then the cash machine.**

1. Rather than trying to sell everything to everyone (that came later), Jeff Bezos started from one niche, books: form one relatively smaller market, dominate it, then move on.
2. Amazon invested in infrastructure and technology from its early years, but it also turned the conventional business model for publishing upside down. It offered an extensive collection of books at a lower price than physical bookstores; the initial value proposition was convenience.
3. It cut its margins and set up a **cash machine business model**: a model where the company lowers its profitability but generates a lot of short-term cash flow in return. Customers paid for a book right away, Amazon delivered, and it paid its vendors later on.
4. The model worked on all sides: customers got any book at a lower price; vendors got higher exposure and visibility through the Amazon store; Amazon used the additional short-term cash to invest in growth.
5. Repeated many times over for more than two decades, this let Amazon expand to sell anything and create other businesses (AWS, Prime and physical stores) that run at high profitability compared to the online store.

Once again it all starts with business model innovation. Technology is an enhancer of the process, not necessarily what makes the real difference in the outcome.

- AWS sales ($ billion): 2015: 7.88; 2016: 12.22; 2017: 17.46.

### Apple

**The innovation.** It is easy to assume Apple is solely a product company selling beautifully crafted devices. But there would have been no Mac success without its operating system, no iPod success without iTunes, and no iPhone success without the App Store. When Steve Jobs went back to Apple, the company needed to be rebuilt from its ashes: sales were plummeting and too many unsuccessful products had been introduced. Jobs cut and simplified the product offering, but most importantly he started creating the infrastructure that would make the products successful: iTunes for the iPod and the App Store for the iPhone. The devices are great, but they would not have succeeded without an environment of developers willing to create applications for the iPhone, or if people had to pay for entire albums to listen to on the iPod. Apple changed how music was consumed and created a whole new industry with its smartphone. Sales did not take off because of great devices powered by suitable software alone; the business model crafted by Jobs and his team created the commercial viability of those products and a massive amount of traction.

### Google

**Business model: hidden revenue generation.** Users don't pay for the service. Google's revenue streams come from businesses bidding on keywords searched by users. Three sides, each with its own value proposition:

- **Users** (free search engine): "I can find an answer to anything."
- **Businesses** (AdWords): "I get more sales through targeted ads."
- **Publishers** (AdSense): "Easily monetize my content."

The flows: users provide better data for businesses to monetize keywords; publishers provide free and quality content for Google's users; businesses bid on keywords, and those fees pay publishers' effort to produce content.

**The innovation.** When Google launched, its search algorithm proved quite good compared to other search engines: it didn't look spammy with all the ads other engines showed, and it was fast. The tool was free, which made it scale quickly, but revenues did not take off until Google figured out a new business model for advertising. It combined its technology with a model first crafted by a competitor, Overture, which had introduced a system of auctions for advertising that proved quite successful. Google took that model and improved on it with its seamless advertising machine (Google AdWords, now Google Ads): auction-based ads plus a **quality score**, a 1/10 scale rating made of components like expected clickthrough rate, ad relevance and landing page experience. Ads were judged not only on the price paid but also on relevance. In 2017 Google generated over a hundred billion dollars in revenue from advertising. A great technological product without a proper business model would have been doomed.

When Google started in the late 1990s, it wasn't the first search engine. It was a better product than many others on the market, yet it wasn't technological innovation alone that made it successful. Many argue that business model innovation is what makes the difference in a company's success, even though many believe product or technology is the most critical competitive advantage; from a business standpoint, business modeling has proved to create a real edge, especially since digital businesses took over the business world, and Google is no exception. It was when Google finally found its business model that revenues took off. When it went public in 2004 and opened its financials for the first time, everyone was astounded. Google was among the first companies to prove the internet was not just a cool new thing but what would give rise to new, disruptive business models.

### Facebook

**Business model.** Facebook is an attention-based business model. Its algorithms condense the attention of over 2.4 billion users as of June 2019. Facebook advertising revenues accounted for $31.9 billion, or 98.66% of total revenues. Facebook Inc. has a product portfolio of Instagram, Messenger, WhatsApp and Oculus. (The figures conflict across sources: one gives $31.9 billion and 98.66% of revenue in the June 2019 context, another says the feed made "over $39 billion in 2017 (Facebook + Instagram feeds)" and that over 98% of 2017 revenue came from targeted advertising; the periods differ and the sources do not reconcile them.)

**Hidden revenue generation.** Users get the service for free; the platform owns the data, which gets sold in the form of targeted advertising. The flows: users provide free data to power up Facebook's algorithm; users' data is sold as targeted ads in the feed; businesses pay for Facebook ads based on impressions, likes, clicks, shares and so on; users get an unlimited source of entertainment, because the feed is an infinite scroll. **The Feed** is Facebook's most precious asset.

**The innovation.** When Mark Zuckerberg came up with Facebook in his dorm room, it wasn't the first social network. It grew exponentially thanks to investment and an initially free model, then transformed the social network into a targeted-ads money-making machine. It figured out how to monetize "social currency": companies and marketers pay Facebook to gain visibility, sold as impressions, clicks or other actions people can take on a targeted ad. It has built a strong brand and remains among the most profitable companies and most popular apps, while apps like TikTok try to disrupt it. Facebook's hidden revenue model has proved the only worthy competitor to Google's advertising business.

### LinkedIn

A two-sided model serving **HR managers** (LinkedIn Hiring) and **professionals** (Learning & Development). Its key innovations, the two-way nature of relationships and meeting professionals' need for a business-oriented online identity, were engineered deliberately by Reid Hoffman (section 3). Acquired by Microsoft in 2016 for $26.2 billion.

### Quora

**What it is.** "The most popular 'social Q&A'". Quora is a question-and-answer site that allows anyone to ask anything and get answers from real people. It grew thanks to several rounds of investment from venture capital firms, among them Founders Fund (Peter Thiel) and Y Combinator (Paul Graham). It has over 190 million monthly users and has built an engaged community over the last decade; people on Quora like to call themselves "Quorans".

**Value matrix (Quorans):** writers build their audience while offering value to the community.

- **Users:** free knowledge from real people; ask any question; ask questions to my favorite authors.
- **Writers:** community building.
- **Top writers:** PR, visibility.

**Money matrix:** publishers/online businesses and investors want to maximize their ROI.

- **Publishers (online businesses):** ROI through contextual advertising.
- **Investors:** ROI through exit or IPO.

**Status.** Quora is still transitioning and experimenting with monetization. Although it now generates revenue through contextual advertising, it is hard to say whether it is profitable. Over time the goal is to replace the financial resources coming from investors with those coming from publishers and online businesses willing to spend money on contextual advertising.

**How sticky is Quora? (SimilarWeb, February 2018):** global rank 140; rank in the United States 82; rank in Reference > Dictionaries and Encyclopedias 2; estimated visits 491.9M; time on site 00:02:34; page views 2.15; bounce rate 65.79%.

### Spotify

**Business model.** Spotify is a two-sided marketplace where artists and music fans meet on a single platform. Founded in 2008 on the belief that music should be universally accessible with a seamless experience based on streaming audio and video. It generated over €4 billion in 2017, almost 90% from premium memberships and 10% from a free, ad-supported service. It recorded an operating loss of €378 million in 2017.

- **Premium service:** premium subscribers get unlimited online and offline high-quality streaming access to the Spotify catalog. Premium membership revenues: 89.83%, €3.6 billion.
- **Ad-supported service:** no subscription fees; ad-supported users get limited on-demand online access to the catalog. Ad-supported revenues: 10.17%, €416 million.
- 71 million premium subscribers (2017).
- Each user spent over 40 hours consuming audio and video content on average in a year.
- Revenue grew to over €4 billion in 2017 from €1.9 billion in 2015 (€ billion: 2015: 1.9; 2016: 2.9; 2017: 4).
- Average revenue per user was over €5 in 2017, a metric critical to Spotify's future financial success (€: 2015: 7.06; 2016: 6; 2017: 5.24).

### The AI value chain

The AI ecosystem has generated a multi-billion dollar industry, and it all starts from data. Going up the value chain:

1. **Data.**
2. **Chips** (GPUs) that allow the physical storing of big data; a dominant player is NVIDIA. Other players: Qualcomm, Google, Samsung, Intel, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, AMD, IBM, Graphcore.
3. **Platform and infrastructure:** big data must be stored on platforms and infrastructure SMEs can't afford, which is where Google Cloud, Amazon AWS, IBM Cloud and Microsoft Azure come to the rescue.
4. **Models and algorithms** (Microsoft, Amazon, IBM, Google, Facebook): conversational agents, speech, NLP and semantics, core algorithms, vision.
5. **Enterprise solutions:** customer management (Oracle, Salesforce, IBM, SAP), HR and talent, marketing and sales, RPA and others, intelligence and analytics, cybersecurity, tools.
6. **Corporates:** healthcare, finance and insurance, tech and telco, agriculture, automotive, legal and compliance, industrials, retail, media and other.

### Blockchain economics 101

**Where value is captured.** According to Joel Monegro, a former analyst at USV (a venture capital firm), the blockchain implies value creation in its protocols. The web allowed value to be captured at the **applications layer** (Facebook, Twitter, Google, Uber, TikTok, Netflix and many others) on top of a thin protocol layer: the **web-based economy**. In a **blockchain economy**, value might be captured by the **protocols** at the base of the blockchain (for instance Bitcoin and Ethereum), with a thin applications layer on top.

**The counter-view.** According to blockchain investor Paivinen, due to ease of forking, incentives to compete, and improved interoperability and interchangeability, protocols might get thinner in a blockchain-based economy too. A forking, interoperable and interchangeable blockchain economy stacks governance protocol, processing protocol, file storage protocol and other protocols beneath the applications layer. The marginal value of scale might be lower than in a web-based economy, where massive scale created an economic advantage. The success of blockchain will depend on its commercial viability.

- **The good:** transparency, distributed ledgers, decentralized systems.
- **The bad:** security failures, fake transactions, no commercial application yet.
- **Potential applications:** publishing, ride-sharing, internet advertising, voting, banking.

"I am excited about the move to crypto based business models supporting decentralized apps for this very reason. I think it opens up the possibility that some very large new companies will be created that innovate largely on entirely new business models." (Fred Wilson, AVC, 2019.)

### Industries blockchain could disrupt (CB Insights)

- **Banking:** as a digitized, secure and tamper-proof ledger, blockchain could serve the same function, injecting enhanced accuracy and information-sharing into the financial services ecosystem.
- **Hedge funds:** backed by names including First Round Capital and Union Square Ventures, Numerai is taking the hedge fund model, employing a bunch of traders and quants, and decentralizing it.
- **Messaging apps:** Telegram is still developing its blockchain-based platform called the TON (Telegram Open Network), which extends Telegram's services into payments, file storage and censorship-proof browsing for its 200M users.
- **Voting:** in the future, blockchain tools could serve as a foundational infrastructure for casting, tracking and counting votes, potentially eliminating the need for recounts by taking voter fraud and foul play off the table.
