## 1. Growth Foundations: Traction, the Gap and the Real Obstacle

### The miracle of business is growth, not the start

**What it is**: The miracle of business is not that you can start one; it is whether you can grow one. Anybody can have an entrepreneurial seizure and come up with a great idea. The question is not "Can you come up with an idea?" but "Can you come up with an idea and get traction?" That is what growth is really about.

**Why it matters**: The more traction you get, the better and more popular the business becomes. Traction does not automatically mean you make money, but it begins to solve a lot of problems, because it means you have created a scratch for your target market's itch. Many entrepreneurs are a scratch looking for an itch: there is a market out there with an itch, and if you can find a scratch that solves their problem, you have an opportunity to grow.

The growth question is really two questions in one: how do I grow a business, and how do I create a business that is **sustainably** successful?

### Everybody has a gap: where they are vs where they want to be

In twenty-five years of teaching business owners, nobody has ever come in wanting to reduce the size of their business. Everybody says some version of "Here's where I am and here's where I'm trying to get to." There is always a gap somewhere between where they are and what they want.

**The key to growth is understanding the obstacle that sits in that gap.** Everyone has an obstacle preventing them from getting from where they are to what they want, and understanding and resolving that obstacle is the key to growth.

### The Is line and the Ought line

**What it is**: A way to picture the gap.

- The **Is line** is reality: the way it actually looks right now. Your circumstances, the team you actually have, the environment you are in, your competition.
- The **Ought line** is the way you would like it to look: your vision, your dream.

**Why the Is line is hard**: Most of us have a hard time being truthful with ourselves about where we actually are. We sugarcoat. We tell ourselves a story, or make up an excuse about why it looks the way it does. It takes a lot of work to say, "This is the reality of where I am."

Over the doors of the main library building (the Tower) at the University of Texas in Austin are the words: "You shall know the truth, and the truth shall set you free." A student can walk past that inscription every day for four or five years without understanding it. The meaning: **once you tell yourself the truth, you have an opportunity to make a choice. Until you tell yourself the truth, you are stuck.**

**The problem with the goal-setting crowd**: Goal setters focus heavily on "What do you want?" and rarely on "Where are you?" That is like saying "I want to go to Paris, France" without knowing where you are starting. The route and the time it takes are completely different if you start in Austin, Texas than if you start in Munich, Germany. It is one thing to know what you want; it is another to know where you are and what obstacles are in the way.

### The gap is not the problem; the gap is a symptom

**What it is**: The most common mistake in thinking about growth is identifying the gap between where you are and what you want as "the problem."

**The exercise**: Write down one of the three biggest problems you have in your business right now. Stop. Notice how fast you identified it. Whatever you wrote down is almost certainly not the core root problem. It is simply a description of the gap.

- "I don't have enough sales." That is not a problem; it is a symptom.
- "I've got an employee problem." Symptom.
- "The culture of my business is kind of wonky." Symptom.
- "I'm running short on cash." Symptom. Something else happened that caused that symptom to show up.

If you don't have enough sales or revenue, that is the result of something else not happening that should have happened.

**Why it matters**: If you solve for the symptom, you are solving **the problem that isn't**, and you waste an enormous amount of time and resources. The gap is what sits between where you are and what you want; the obstacle is what is actually preventing forward movement. You do want clarity on the gap (if there is no gap, there is no obstacle, and you are "fat and happy"). But once there is a gap, what you need is clarity on the obstacle. Once you identify the obstacle, you can begin chipping away at it and move the needle closer to what you want.

**Ordinary things consistently done produce extraordinary results.** The question that follows is: how can I improve my situation?

### Lurching for tactics

**What it is**: When we see a gap, the first thing most of us do is lurch for a tactic: "What do I need to do?" The result is a search engine optimization strategy, a digital media strategy, Facebook, websites, salespeople and brochures. Every one of these could be a good idea, and all of them are tactical.

**Example: the bathroom scale.** You step on the scale and weigh five pounds too much. The first thought is "What do I need to do?" Buy one of Oprah's diet books (and there are something like three thousand of them). Go on the diet for a couple of weeks. It doesn't really work. So join a gym. Still not in shape. Buy a new pair of sneakers so you can walk comfortably. Doesn't work. Buy a Peloton. Build a massive home gym. Still not in shape. Then it's a StairMaster. Then, because you travel all the time and can't get to the Peloton, a jump rope you can pack. Tactic, tactic, tactic. You never understood the actual problem preventing you from getting in shape.

**Another example**: You meet someone at the gym who rebuilds websites and think, "The law of attraction! I needed a website. Let me hire you, that'll solve my problem." That is a tactic, not a diagnosis.

**The hardest work in growth**: The hardest work any business owner does on growth is figuring out the actual obstacle in the way, the real problem. When you are busy tactically throwing solutions at a problem, the problem often doesn't get solved, so you reach for another tactic, and that cycle itself prevents the growth you want.

**Growth is not a bathrobe**: Business is not a bathrobe; one size does not fit all. Ask business owners "Why isn't your business two times bigger than it currently is?" and you hear: "Not enough people know about us." "I need to increase the size of my funnel." "If I just had a good social media strategy." Tactic, tactic, tactic. "Not enough people know about us" is not the problem; it is a symptom. Why don't enough people know about you? Is the real problem really the lack of a social media strategy? It could be, but we react far too quickly when asked what our problem is.

### A growth plan is a business plan

A growth plan is another word for a business plan. It asks: What are the obstacles? How are you going to grow this thing and get traction? What are all the pieces required not only for growth to occur, but for it to be sustainable?

**The "then a miracle occurs" cartoon**: Two old men stand at a chalkboard with complex formulas on the left and complex formulas on the right. In the middle it says "and then a miracle occurs." One says to the other, "I think you should be more explicit here in step number two." We tend to rely on miracles and tactics to get from point A to point B instead of asking, "What is the challenge? What is the obstacle preventing my forward progress?"

### Symptom stacking: diagnosing "inadequate sales"

**What it is**: Symptoms often sit on top of other symptoms. Finding an underlying symptom can feel like finding the problem when it is really a secondary effect of the original symptom.

**Example**: "My problem is inadequate sales." Possible reasons, each a different problem and each actually another symptom:

- Customers are not buying enough.
- Customers are buying from the competition.
- You don't have enough prospects.
- You're not closing enough.
- The sales cycle is too long.
- There is a lack of referrals.
- Repeat business is broken.
- Your market isn't large enough.
- There is a market concentration issue.
- You are unable to communicate **the difference that makes the difference** (what is the promise you are making?).
- You don't have a niche; you are trying to be broad instead of narrow.

"Not closing enough" is itself another symptom, with a problem underneath it. If you stop at it, you still haven't reached the real problem.

Apply the same thinking to "I'm not growing enough." Is it because not enough people know about you? Or they know about you and don't see the difference that makes the difference? Or they know about you, tried you, and you didn't do anything to cause them to come back for a second helping, so every time you add one customer you lose one?

**Example: the speaking problem.** A business owner who runs a four-day business course wanted to run it more often. The course had always been marketed the same way: he speaks for other organizations, people get a taste, and some of them enroll. His logical conclusion: "I need to give more speeches." His partner pointed out that he was already speaking to about thirty or forty thousand people a year, so it was unlikely that the problem was not being in front of enough people. The real problem: he couldn't sell from the stage. He didn't want to sell, because he believes that if he has to stand up and convince you, he cares more about your dreams than you do. The easy answer ("speak more") was a line drawn from the visible gap. The real obstacle was something else entirely, and it explained why the course was not running every other week.

**Pitfall**: Everyone draws lines and makes assumptions about the problem based on the gap they see. If you actually sit with "What is the real problem?", you may find that inadequate sales comes from a lack of referrals, broken repeat business, a market that isn't large enough, market concentration, an inability to communicate the difference that makes the difference, or no niche.

### The tension between keeping and getting

**What it is**: Every leadership team faces the same tension: operations and delivery (today) versus growth (the future). You are constantly allocating resources one way or the other.

**The CEO's primary job**: **Set priorities, and based on those priorities, allocate resources.** Without clear priorities, or without a clear understanding of the obstacle in the way, resources get wasted on tactics or spent solving the problem that isn't.

Based on that allocation, resources go to one of two things:

- **Resources to get new customers**: here you are optimizing to **get people to notice what you do**.
- **Resources to keep what you've got**: here you are trying to **do something people will notice**.

Those are two different things. Most owners spend the majority of their time putting out fires and reacting to the problem du jour. When there is time to think, the thinking is "How do I grow this thing?" rather than "How do I fix this thing so that I get more of the customers I've got coming back for a second helping?" Getting customers to keep coming back for another helping is called **addiction**, and a business full of addicted customers is in a great place.

**The key question**: **How big would my business be if I still had every customer that ever tried me?** The answer should be somewhat disconcerting. Many customers gave you a shot, and somehow what you did or how you did it did not meet their expectations. There was a boo-boo, a miss, and you did not take the opportunity to get them addicted.

**Pitfall**: The vast majority of business owners are really good at attracting and not so good at keeping. Therein may lie a fundamental reason why growth doesn't happen: every time you add one, they try you once and bounce off. You haven't built a machine that resolves the tension between what needs to happen to keep them and what needs to happen to attract them.

### The genius is in the question, because the answers keep changing

**What it is**: The genius in growth is not finding the answer; it is getting to the right question.

**Example: Einstein's exams.** After developing the theory of relativity, Albert Einstein was a professor at Princeton for twenty years. At the end of his first three semesters, fellow professors told him, "They're on to you. You keep giving the same questions every semester on the final exam, and the students pass them along to the next group. You have to change it up." Einstein refused. Asked why, he said: **"Because the answers keep on changing."**

**Why it works**: A good idea last week could be a horrible idea today because the environment has shifted. A great idea in February 2020 might be a horrible idea in April 2020, not because of the idea but because of the environment. A horrible idea today could be a great idea next year. We would all like to come up with the answer once and never think about it again, but change is constantly happening in your competition, the economy, politics, the environment, your employees and your customers, and what they want keeps changing. So the answers keep changing, and the genius lies in being able to come up with the question.

## 2. Thinking Time: The Most Powerful Tool for Growth

### Business is an intellectual sport

Business is an intellectual sport. The classic book on the subject is called *Think* (sold as *Think and Grow Rich*): it has sold eighty million copies, is published in thirty-five languages, and is seventy-five to eighty years old. What you are thinking about is: What is the problem, and what questions do I need to ask that will help me get clarity either on the obstacle or on possible solutions, the yellow brick road that gets me from Munchkinville to Oz?

If growth depends on figuring out the real obstacles and real problems, and on planning instead of reacting, playing whack-a-mole and dealing with dumpster fires all day, then setting aside time to think is essential.

### The Mike Milken story: scheduling time to think

**What it is**: The practice of putting dedicated, recurring thinking time on your calendar.

**The story**: A business owner was raising money to acquire the cable television system in Atlanta, Georgia, roughly a $150 million deal. The financing source was Drexel Burnham, at the time the king of Wall Street (roughly what Goldman Sachs is today), whose superstar was Mike Milken, the man who dreamed up junk bonds. The bank was going to put up almost all of the money, all except maybe a million dollars. The whole deal was structured and a week from closing when the investment banker called: "There's one more hurdle. You have to go have a meeting with Mr. Milken."

The instructions were precise. Fly to Los Angeles. Seventeen-story building, thirteenth floor, all conference rooms, twenty of them. Each room has a table with five chairs: one for you, three for the bankers, and one empty chair next to you. The pitch lasts about an hour and a half. At some point the door will open and Mr. Milken will walk in and sit in the empty chair. He'll stay ten or twelve minutes and leave. **If he stays longer than ten or twelve minutes, the deal has problems.** Don't talk to him, don't greet him, don't look at him when he leaves; just keep pitching.

Twenty minutes into the pitch, the door opened and Milken sat down, took out his day-timer, made a note, and listened without a word. At about eleven or twelve minutes he closed his book and pushed back from the table. Right then there was a knock. A man at the door said: "Mr. Milken, I'm sorry to interrupt. We have to have a decision on the takeover of Gulf Oil." (A deal with one or two more zeros than the cable deal.) Milken said "Give me one second," opened his day-timer, turned a page ("no"), turned another ("no"), wrote something down, closed it, and said to the man at the door:

**"The next time I'm scheduled to think is between 3 AM and 5 AM on Thursday morning. You'll have an answer at 5 AM."**

The business owner said out loud, "You must be kidding me." Setting aside time to think had never dawned on him. Three to five in the morning is impressive, but the concept itself was the revelation.

**Why it works**: If business is an intellectual sport and the job is to figure out the real obstacles and real problems, scheduled thinking time is how you stop reacting and start planning. It is what lets you be **strategic with your business as opposed to tactical with your ideas**, and that leap in itself creates growth.

**Pitfall: learning it and forgetting to use it.** He wrote "schedule time to think" on his tablet and then forgot to use it. A few years later came personal and economic drama: he lost all his money, and the recovery took eighteen months (he took a sabbatical, grew a ponytail, got his ear pierced, read psychology and theology, and rebooted himself). During that time he remembered the Milken meeting and checked his calendar for the previous twelve months to see whether he had set aside even thirty minutes to think. He hadn't. So he sat down with paper and pen in a chair and took a nap. He tried the next day and took another nap. "I just don't know how to think."

**The follow-up call**: He tracked Milken down through the New York banker and, within about a week, got him on the phone. "You don't remember me." "You're right." He explained he had been in the room when Milken was interrupted, had tried this thinking thing, and was not good at it. Milken's reply: "Of all the people that have seen me, you're the first person who's ever reached out and followed up on what I'm doing. **It's the most powerful tool I have.**"

### The key to good thinking: start with a powerful question

**What it is**: Thinking time starts with a powerful question, not a blank page.

The examples Milken gave:

- **What don't I see?** (Your assumptions, the story you are creating.)
- **What could go wrong, and how do I reduce the probability of that happening?**
- **How would I run my business if one hundred percent of my future growth was by referral only?**
- **How would I have to run my business so that customers would say "I'd have to be crazy to use somebody else"?**

He said he had hundreds of such questions and refused to share them: "You need to go away and think."

**How good ideas happen**: Asked "How do you have a good idea?", Milken answered: **"I just have a lot of ideas and then pick the best one."** The whole point of thinking time is to create a lot of ideas, instead of settling for the idea right in front of you that seems most obvious.

### How to do thinking time

1. **Schedule it.** For about thirty-five years the practice has been two sessions a week of about an hour each, on the calendar.
2. **Ritualize it.** Close the door. Turn off the machines, all the electronics and technology. Set an alarm. Get totally still in your body. Use a dedicated thinking chair that you sit in only when you are going to think, a thinking pen you pick up only for thinking, and a thinking journal.
3. **Start with one powerful question.** Some come from a question bank, some occur to you in the course of your business, your life or your economics.
4. **Write.** Thinking time is not about finding the right idea; it is about finding a bunch of ideas. Create many possibilities.
5. **Always keep an empty dot.** Every time you write down a possible answer, immediately draw the next empty bullet. It tells you there may be one more thing you need to think about.
6. **Stay long enough to reach the third third.** The first third of the ideas you come up with will be the most obvious and the least likely to be the best. The second third will rhyme with the first set. The third third is often where the juice is. Staying with the question long enough to get there is critical.
7. **Then cull.** When thinking is over, review the list, get rid of the lower-value ideas and identify the high-value ones.
8. **Capture the high-value ideas on a follow-up page**, called the VIP (very important points). The follow-up might be another thinking time, implementing something, or redesigning part of your machine to move the thing forward.

**Why it works**: Over time you get clearer and clearer about what the problem really is and what the possible solutions are for addressing that obstacle. The majority of good thinking time is spent making sure you have clarity on the obstacle so that you don't wind up solving the problem that isn't. The insights come not from being told something but from being asked something and taking the time to be thoughtful about your possibilities. **The key to accessing better answers is having better questions.**

**How long**: Some questions deserve far more than one session. The five simplifying growth questions (section 7) deserve at least ten hours of thinking time; anything less probably doesn't do them justice. A first session of twenty, thirty or forty minutes of sitting quietly with a question is a good start; there are no brownie points for finishing early.

**Pitfall**: The first attempts often end in a nap. That is a sign you haven't started with a powerful question, not a sign that you can't think.

### Powerful questions for thinking time

*The Road Less Stupid* is built for thinking time: chapters of two or three pages, each followed by a list of questions, 730 questions in all, delivered in small chunks. These are examples of powerful questions:

1. **If you sold your business and the buyer forgot to include a covenant not to compete and a non-solicitation agreement on hiring your former employees, and you decided to restart your business and compete with your old business: (a) What would you do to compete? (b) Who would you hire (or rehire) from your old business?** A useful variation: who would you for sure *not* hire from your old business? That list might be kind of chubby. The point is not that you are going to sell; it is to get clarity on part of why you are not growing, and on whether part of the problem is some of the people you have.
2. **How many of your current problems would disappear if one hundred percent of your team was an "A" player?** Or said another way: make a list of the problems you would still have if one hundred percent of your team was an "A" player. For most people, an all-A team would resolve well over fifty percent of their problems, often seventy-five or eighty percent. You can make it specific: what problems do I know I have because Bob, Sue and Joe are not "A" players?
3. **What would your message or promise need to be so that you could double the number of leads and conversions with your existing marketing and sales budget?**
4. **If I could ______ really, really well, I could close the gap or solve this problem.** (A skills question.)
5. **If I had ______, I could close the gap or solve this problem.** (A resources question.)
6. **Where have we tolerated mediocrity, lowered our standards, or allowed inconsistent execution to become an acceptable performance criteria?** Businesses often fail to grow not because of a lack of marketing strategy but because somewhere in the organization some level of mediocrity has been tolerated. This question ties directly to consistent execution as a competitive advantage.
7. **Competitively, why do customers buy from us? Why isn't this strength sufficient to cause more potential buyers to stop doing business with our competition and to start doing business with us?**
8. **What could I do to make this problem worse?**
9. **What can be done today (this week, this month) to improve this situation?**
10. **If my Board of Directors fired me and hired a new management team, what decisions or answers would my replacement have?**

Any one of these questions might contain the clue to why growth isn't what you would like, or, if it is, a possibility for accelerating it.

### The Andy Grove question: fire yourself and rehire yourself

**What it is**: Question 10 above, which comes from Andy Grove, one of the founders of Intel and its chairman and CEO for years.

**The story**: Grove and his partner Gordon Moore (who came up with Moore's law) sat in a conference room on a Saturday trying to decide whether to migrate Intel from making one kind of chip to another. The new chip would cannibalize the old one, and the industry had not yet decided which direction it would go. It was a bet-the-ranch decision: get it wrong and it was game over for Intel. They had wrestled with it for months. Finally Grove asked Moore: "What if our board of directors fired us and hired somebody new to come in and make this decision? What would the new people do?" Moore said, "That's easy. They'd migrate to the new chip." Grove said: "Okay. Let's fire ourselves, walk out the door, rehire ourselves, walk in the door, and make the right decision."

**Why it works**: Often there is a decision you kind of know is right, but it carries consequences, and because you are the legacy, embedded management team, you are afraid to make it. Someone new would know exactly what to do. The question gives that decision mass and often produces enormous clarity.

### Running a "Clarity of the Problem" thinking session

**How to do it**:

1. Set aside forty minutes for the ten questions above.
2. Before starting, review all ten and decide which one or two are of higher value to you right now. You can answer one, two or as many as you like, in whatever order is best for you.
3. You are doing this for yourself: nobody collects it, and there is no grade.
4. Keep an empty dot after every answer, and push for the third third.
5. When time is up, stretch, get back in your body, then take a couple of minutes to read what you wrote, find the high-value ideas and possibilities worth following up on, and transfer them to your VIP follow-up page.

Almost everyone who does this for the first time gets at least one insight or distinction to follow up on, and many get two or more, and most wish they had more time. The questions are a reusable tool: you can go back and revisit them, or any of the other question banks, again and again.

## 3. The Growth Achievement Process (G.A.P.)

### What it is

The Growth Achievement Process ("G.A.P.") is a flowchart for thinking about your business (and equally your life, a relationship or your money). It is one of the most powerful tools in your arsenal because it forces you to get clarity on your Problem and then to design a Machine that will actually solve the Problem identified. **The only thing standing between you and your Goal is an unresolved Problem, and sadly, the unresolved Problem is also usually unidentified.**

### The flowchart

The chart reads left to right and loops back:

- **GOAL / STANDARD (Point "B")**, which must be **Non-negotiable**
- → **REALITY (Point "A")**, which requires **Candor / Honesty**
- → **PROBLEM or CHALLENGE**, which may be a **Predicament**, a **Crisis**, or a **Symptom (visible)** that leads to **Root Cause / Diagnosis (Why?)** and must become **Intolerable**
- → **MACHINE**, built from three parts:
  - **Strategy / Plan** (Options, Choices, Consequences), shaped by: Obstacle, Guardrails, Leverageable Assets, Price/Cost, Tasks, Resources, Time/Effort, Environment, Priorities, Core Competence
  - **+ People**: Skills, Abilities, Attitude, Training, Culture, "A" Players
  - **+ Execution**: Ownership, Intensity, Alignment, Accountability, Consequences, Critical Drivers, Org. Structure
- → **OUTPUT / RESULT**
- → **MEASURE PROGRESS (Critical Drivers & KPIs)**, compared against the Goal/Standard
- → **CORRECT / IMPROVE**, which feeds back into the Problem and the Machine.

The Wizard of Oz version: you start in Munchkinville (Point A) and want to get to Oz (Point B). The obstacle is that you don't know the route. So you build a yellow brick road (the machine). There might be some flying monkeys. Three people plus a little dog named Toto get on the road and execute, and you measure your progress toward Oz with KPIs and critical drivers.

### Step 1: Goal or Standard (Point B)

**What it is**: Clarity on the intended goal or outcome, the "ought": the way you would like it to be.

**How to do it**: Get clear on the outcome, then make its achievement **non-negotiable**. If the goal is really a wish or a hope, find something else to work on. Wishful thinking is daydreaming and is never a useful strategy for goal achievement or success.

**Why standards beat goals**: Most people are already good at Point B; it is what the goal-setting courses teach (SMART goals, bring it closer, push it further away, turn it into colors, slow it down, add words and music). A goal is often wishful thinking; a standard is a different level because it is non-negotiable. **We need fewer goals and more standards. Raising your standard is a far superior strategy than setting new goals.** Regardless of what you call it, clarity on the destination is critical.

### Standards vs aspirational goals: milestones and "kick the can"

**The question**: If you want growth (more revenue, profit, effectiveness, efficiency, "twenty percent more of this"), how do you turn that into a non-negotiable standard rather than an aspirational goal, while still fostering growth and excellence?

**What it is**: Break the aspiration into milestones, and make each **next milestone** non-negotiable.

**The old car radio**: Old car radios had two dials, one for volume and one for tuning. To get a station you rolled the tuning dial gradually, passing through every point on the way, and fine-tuned at the end. Modern radios have buttons that jump straight to a station. Growth works like the old dial: you roll through milestones on your path from where you are to what you want.

**How to do it**:

1. Allow the aspirational vision ("world domination," whatever it is). Vision is fine as long as you can reverse engineer it into milestones on the yellow brick road.
2. Measure the gap honestly. Example: you are doing $100,000 a year of revenue; world domination is a long way off.
3. Create the milestone that is a step in front of you: get from $100,000 a year of revenue to $500,000 a year. Now you have a metric and a milestone, and you make its achievement non-negotiable, with a plan, a yellow brick road and resources.
4. Chunk it further. If you don't know how much you can get done over the next year, decide on **three things you will do over the next quarter** to move the needle. Maybe none will work, maybe one will; commit to doing the work consistently on your hypothesis of what moves the needle.
5. Keep kicking the can.

**Why it works: the people you admire did not start with a giant aspiration.**

- Michael Dell has said that when he started, he wanted to figure out how to make a quarter million dollars a year.
- The Google founders tried to sell Google for one million dollars (with an "m," not a "b"); the Yahoo founders turned them down.
- Bill Gates had a deal to sell Microsoft for twenty million dollars to H. Ross Perot; the only reason it didn't happen is that Perot changed his mind.
- Steve Jobs offered the founder of Atari a third of Apple Computer for fifty thousand dollars. Had Jobs started with an aspirational goal of creating a $250 billion-a-year company, he would not have been offering a third of it for fifty thousand dollars.

This is not an argument against aspirational goals; it is an argument in favor of the milestone in front of you.

**Example: kicking the can.** Walking outside their hut (a bure) at Tony Robbins' resort in Fiji, a man found a Coke can and kicked it. It tumbled two or three feet. He walked over and kicked it again. After two kicks his wife said "Leave that can alone," but he kept going and kicked it a full mile. The can did not travel a mile because of a big aspiration, or because he laced up his shoes, took a running start and kicked it really hard once. It got a mile down the road because he kept kicking it. Sometimes it squirted sideways, sometimes it tumbled, sometimes it stalled or went backwards a little. Have all the aspirations you want, then kick the can.

**Pitfall**: Without the interim step (the chunk, the intermediate thing to move toward), people get paralyzed and don't know what to do.

### Step 2: Reality (Point A)

**What it is**: Clarity on where you actually are. This requires candor and honesty.

**Why it matters**: We tend to be experts at goal setting and believe that if we just get clear on what we want, we will somehow achieve (or "attract") it. Knowing what you want matters, but it does no good to know where you want to go if you don't have a clue where you are, because then you don't know what machine needs to be built to get you there. This is the "Point A" problem. **Getting clarity on the Reality "That Is" is the only way you stand a chance of solving the Problem "That Is."**

**How to do it**: Be brutally candid. You typically need the support of a third-party coach, advisor or friend to accurately assess your current status.

### Step 3: The Problem or Challenge

**What it is**: Based on Point B and Point A, identify the problem or challenge. First decide what *kind* of problem it is.

**Type 1: a predicament.** A predicament is a statement of environment that you cannot change. You want a picnic and it's raining. The price of oil. Interest rates. "I'm seventy-one years old." There is nothing you can do about these, so a predicament requires you to **think differently about the problem**, not to attack it.

**Type 2: a crisis.** A crisis is an emergency room. Emergency rooms require lots of tactics and strong, massive action. In a true crisis, the symptom must be dealt with first. When someone is admitted with an apparent heart attack, you don't start a lecture about smoking and obesity at admission; you stabilize the patient and get them off life support and out of crisis. Only when the patient is not in critical condition is the best course of action to identify the core problem and diagnose the root cause.

**Vertical time vs horizontal time**: There are two kinds of time. Vertical time is twenty-four hours, a day. Horizontal time is a lifetime.

**Example: the dam.** Your house sits at the base of a three-hundred-foot dam in your backyard. One morning, coffee in hand, you see a crack with water seeping through. The repair crew says the fix takes twenty-four hours: you can have it all at once, or thirty minutes a day for the next forty-eight days. It's a crisis, so you choose all at once. Now, with time on your hands, you decide to get in shape the same way: you join a gym and spend the next twenty-four straight hours there, lifting weights, sucking down vitamins, jumping rope, doing push-ups and running two marathons. At the end of twenty-four hours the dam is fixed, and you are sore.

**Why it matters**: Anything that requires a massive amount of time at once is a crisis, and crisis is when you take very strong action. But most of what we want in life requires horizontal time: ordinary things consistently done, the consistent repetition of going to the gym, eating right, and doing what we need to for our relationships and our money. Massive action works really well in a crisis and is problematic if you want to get in shape. Being clear about the type of problem tells you what kind of time to allocate to it.

**Type 3: a symptom with a hidden root cause.** If the challenge is neither a predicament nor a crisis, what you can see is probably only the symptom. **The symptom is visible; the problem is usually invisible.** You can tell that you feel bad; you can't see the tumor that is making you feel bad.

**How to separate symptom from root cause**:

1. Recognize that the symptom is never the real problem nor the root cause. We gravitate toward fixing what is visible and therefore make no progress. Addressing the symptom makes us feel better in the moment ("At least I'm doing something"), but **confusing activity with productivity** is a major saboteur of businesses and dreams. Being busy on the wrong thing is like **running the wrong direction enthusiastically**: a complete waste of time and effort. What you want is sustainable forward progress ("kicking the can"), which is only possible when you identify exactly where the can is that needs to be kicked.
2. Ask "Why?" repeatedly. A problem is simply an unanswered question. Why is this a problem? How did this get to be a problem to begin with? Why isn't this problem already solved? Once you run out of answers to the "Why?" questions, you are probably close to the core problem and the root cause.
3. Get peer input and brutal honesty. This is frankly the most difficult part of the G.A.P.
4. Don't over-analyze. Too often people get a headache (the symptom) and repeatedly take an aspirin until aspirin-taking becomes a habit, instead of figuring out the root cause and stopping the "hammer to the head" activity that causes the headache. You can take time to figure out why you are hitting yourself in the head with a hammer, but **if that takes longer than about fifteen minutes, move on**. At the end of the day you need to decide to stop the hammering regardless of why you are doing it. All the understanding in the world does not change the fact that if something needs to change, **the decision to change it is the key to the change** ("Just stop it!").

### Make the problem intolerable

**What it is**: Whatever you identify as the problem must become **intolerable** to you, and solving it must become non-negotiable.

**Why it works**: A resistance to making the problem intolerable is simply a resistance to solving it, a way of staying in your comfort zone, and no one has ever been successful by embracing their comfort zone. Everything we want lies outside our comfort zone, and everything we want demands a price.

**Procrastination is the son of a lack of commitment to making the problem intolerable.** Without that level of commitment and decision-making, you will ultimately fail. A hint: the things you have been procrastinating on are the things holding you back and retarding your progress. Making the problem intolerable is another way of saying you must elevate your commitment from **"Best You Can" to "Whatever It Takes."**

### Step 4: Build the Machine

**What it is**: Once you have clarity on what you want, where you are, and the obstacle (not the symptom), ask: what is the machine (the solution) that must be built to produce an output that moves me toward the desired outcome and overcomes the obstacle?

**Every machine has three parts**:

1. **A plan or strategy** (the design), with well-thought-out possible consequences in the event of success as well as failure. It must take into account the resources available to you, the price or cost of the endeavor, the environment you are operating in (a good idea yesterday might be a horrible idea today) and the core competence you have to actually pull this machine off.
2. **People** to design, build, run and monitor the machine. People don't operate in a vacuum; they operate within a culture. **Given a choice between great people in a lousy culture and mediocre people in a great culture, take the great culture every time.**
3. **Execution**: what the people are supposed to do. It must be consistent and intense, with rewards for success and consequences for failure.

### Step 5: Output, Measurement and Correction

**What it is**: The machine produces outputs or results. Measure the progress of those results against the goal or standard you originally set: is the machine moving you closer to your outcome?

**How to do it**: Use the measurements to decide what improvements or corrections need to be made to the machine to enhance its output and accelerate progress. The machine can always be tweaked and refined, but **refinements are dictated by the measurements**. If something is broken, check first whether you are still clear on the problem; if you are, tweak the machine. It is a loop.

**Why it works**: **Measurement and correction are THE keys to sustainability.** The only constant in business is change, and ignoring change is a prescription for disaster. **Change is not visible without measuring and comparing.**

### Critical drivers

**What they are**: The measures that are **predictive** of results. Great critical drivers are predictive.

**Example**: If you are given the exact exercise and diet plan and you execute on it, that is a predictive indicator that you will weigh the right amount. Whether you brush your teeth or take a shower first thing in the morning is not predictive of how much you weigh. So brushing your teeth and taking a shower are not critical drivers of your weight. The job is to find the true critical drivers.

### Using the G.A.P. to find the weak link

**How to do it**: In a thinking-time session, draw the G.A.P. on a sheet of paper and ask: **Where's the weak link? Where is this somehow broken?**

- Have I lost the vision (Point B)?
- Have I lost clarity on my reality (Point A)?
- Has the problem or obstacle shifted?
- Has my machine gotten a little wonky? If so, is it the plan, strategy and design? The people? Or an inconsistency of execution?
- Is the output of my machine less strong than it used to be, so that I'm no longer making progress? Do I need to do a better job of measuring so I can see what needs correcting?

Then connect the dots: which part of this applies to me, how do I prioritize it, and have I been too tactical instead of getting clarity on the obstacle?

### The Options Analysis Matrix: choosing between priorities with similar impact

**What it is**: A one-page tool for thinking through the various options that could support the achievement of an outcome, deliverable or priority, when you have too many options or several with similar impact and results.

**How to do it**:

1. At the top, write down **what you are optimizing for**.
2. List your options in columns (three, five, however many). Examples: hire two new salespeople, boost Facebook spending, create a new product.
3. For each option, fill in:
   - **Time**: the estimated length of time between now and when the deliverable is available (one month, three days, one year).
   - **Effort**: how much of *your* effort is required (twenty percent of your time, two hours of your time, fifty hours total).
   - **Money**: the cost (five dollars; four hundred thousand dollars; a thousand dollars). An option might take only two hours of your time and three days but cost four hundred thousand dollars.
   - **Price tag**: what you have to say **no** to in order to say yes to this. You can't keep piling things on your plate. Nobody is sitting around with nothing to do on Tuesdays, so starting something new means something current takes a back seat or gets paused. (The price tag is essentially the opportunity cost.)
   - **Competence**: on a scale of 1 to 10, do I know what I'm doing, have I done this before, can I pull it off? (One might be a 2, another a 9, another a 4.)
   - **Risk**: on a scale of 1 to 10 (10 being very risky), what could go wrong, and what is the probability of this actually working?
   - **Consequences**: on a scale of 1 to 10, if it works, does it get me closer to what I want, and is it better than the others?
   - **Equals**: the result it is expected to produce, and by when (for example, a certain amount of money by 12/31/2021).
4. Look at the whole page and rank the choices.

**Why it works**: The point is to get everything out of your head and onto paper so you can evaluate one alternative against another. Your brain is not good at remembering; it is good at evaluating and creating, and it can do neither while it is busy holding everything. On paper you can see all of it at once, which makes the decision more objective.

**Pitfall**: There is no point system. Don't add the scores up ("this one totals forty, that one sixty"). Look at the whole matrix and use judgment to rank.

## 4. Growth Grows in Stages

### Growth is a staircase, not a ramp

**What it is**: Growth rarely happens as a ramp; it happens as a series of steps.

**Why it works**: Steps are a good thing. After each step you get an opportunity to set a foundation, collect your resources and stabilize. A staircase in a building typically has a landing halfway up so you can catch your breath, marshal your energy, and establish "I've made it this far" before turning the corner. Builders could make stairs that just keep going with no landing, but the stair-step process is more effective than a straight ramp.

**What got you here won't get you there.** The problems (and solutions) you had a year or two ago, or when you first started, are very different from the problems you have today. At the beginning the problem was getting traction; once you have traction, the problem looks different. Different stages require different strategies, and, bizarrely, when it comes to growth, experience is rarely your friend. We keep doing the same thing over and over, and when it doesn't work, we do it louder, harder, stronger and faster. It still doesn't work; it just makes us tired.

### Normal, abnormal and life-threatening problems

The management thinker Ichak Adizes classified problems into three types:

1. **Normal**
2. **Abnormal**
3. **Life-threatening**

**Example**: A newborn can't feed itself: normal. A ninety-seven-year-old can't feed themselves: also normal. A forty-year-old who can't feed themselves has an abnormal problem that could morph into a life-threatening one. Different problems belong to different stages. The question to ask about any problem is whether it is normal for your stage.

### The growth chart

**What it is**: A chart with **size of organization** (small to large) on the vertical axis and **age of organization** (young to mature) on the horizontal axis. The age axis is hypothetical: a "stage" is not a fixed number of years and might be a matter of months. As a business grows, different things happen and different problems appear. Growth rises as a staircase, and **a crisis typically happens between the steps**.

The seven stages and the crisis that tends to follow each:

| Step | Stage of growth | Crisis that follows |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Creativity | Traction |
| 2 | Niche | Exhaustion |
| 3 | Leverage | Abdication |
| 4 | Delegate | Control |
| 5 | Systems | Red Tape |
| 6 | Culture | Entitlement |
| 7 | Entrepreneurship / Flexibility | |

Across the bottom of the chart, stage, leadership style and main issue:

| Stage number | 1 | 2 | 3-4 | 5-6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stage | Existence | Survival | Disengage-Resources | Growth | ROI |
| Leadership | Crisis | Control | Delegate | Systems | Drive Events |
| Issue | Cash & Customers | Breakeven: Salary-Job | Passive-Growth | Complexity, Strategy & Execution | Innovation & Flexibility, Entrepreneurial |

### Stage by stage

**Stage 1: Creativity → the Traction crisis.** The first thing that happens with a business is the seizure: creativity. This is when you tend to be most excited, because it is your passion and your idea. You are the mother of your idea, and mamas love their babies, so no wonder you are passionate. The biggest problem, the crisis, is **how do we get traction?** At the beginning you throw a lot of ideas against the wall to see what sticks, because you don't yet know who your target audience is or what the message needs to be to hit a niche. You are working on the product, the marketing, and the "bullhorn" (how loud to shout to let people know).

**Stage 2: Niche → the Exhaustion crisis.** You start to get a little traction, usually in one segment, and suddenly you have a niche. **Most businesses die at the niche stage because the owner dies of exhaustion.** You are a one-person army with no leverage: fulfilling for customers, billing, answering the phone, fixing problems, chasing people who don't pay. You become overloaded; your hair is on fire all the time. To keep growing, you need leverage.

**Stage 3: Leverage → the Abdication crisis.** So you hire people, thinking that's all it takes. Then you discover you are working harder than ever, because you did a sorry job of hiring: you hired people who are three feet tall and asked them to dunk the ball, or you have a broken training system. What is generally broken here is **abdication**.

*Example*: A speaker hands his microphone and clicker to a team member who has heard the course many times, says "Come take over," and walks off the stage. She knows the material, but he never trained her to deliver it the way he does, and he doesn't stay engaged. He has abdicated. Owners in this crisis say "Shoot, this doesn't work," go back to doing it all themselves at the niche stage, get exhausted again, hire again, abdicate again, and bounce back and forth, never reaching the next stage. They don't understand that it's not just hiring people; it's hiring "A" players and staying engaged with them.

**Stage 4: Delegate → the Control crisis.** Getting from stage three to four has nothing to do with what it took to get traction. It requires not only people, but learning to delegate (see section 5). You cannot grow past a certain point without leverage, and that leverage must be delegated leverage, from "A" and "B" players, not people three feet tall. Once you finally delegate, the next crisis appears: you have no control. There is no process and no system.

**Stage 5: Systems → the Red Tape crisis.** So you get busy systematizing, standardizing and making sure things are done the same way every time. Pretty soon you have created an enormous amount of red tape (see section 6).

**Stage 6: Culture → the Entitlement crisis.** To fight red tape, you decide what you need is culture, and you make the mistake of substituting perks for culture. That creates entitlement (see section 6).

**Stage 7: Entrepreneurship and Flexibility.** If a company is going to continue to be successful, it tends to revert to ownership, flexibility and entrepreneurship.

### Pitfall: the stages blur, so design ahead

The chart is not a rule that says "if you reached this level you're past systems problems." In practice nearly every business owner slides up and down the scale all the time. They've hired people, tried a training program or two, put jelly beans in the kitchen, run some kind of culture initiative, tried delegating, and are still throwing things against the wall to see what sticks.

**How to use the chart**: Treat it as a template of the issues you are likely to encounter: traction, exhaustion, abdication, control, red tape, entitlement. If you know that over-systematizing creates red tape, and that skipping training and delegation creates ineffective leverage, then instead of hitting each obstacle in sequence you can, from wherever you are now, begin **designing a machine that dodges these crises**. If you don't design that machine, you will hit every one of those obstacles, and not necessarily in sequence; you may hit them all at the same time. Ask: knowing these problems are out there, how can I design the niche, the leverage, the delegation, the systems, the culture and the entrepreneurship and flexibility so that I grow without the crisis?

Then take a few minutes: how does this chart apply to me? What part of it might be the obstacle hindering my growth? Put the answer in your VIP follow-up notes.

### The ceiling of complexity

When you hit what Dan Sullivan calls the **ceiling of complexity**, the point where what you used to do doesn't work anymore, it is an opportunity to **simplify**. The five simplifying questions in section 7 are the tool for that.

## 5. Delegation Is Not Abdication

### Why delegation decides growth

Delegation is exceptionally powerful, and failing at it is one of the things that sabotages people on the growth staircase. It isn't just that you need bodies; you need **the right people doing the right job delivering the right outcomes**. If you abdicate instead of delegate, you will have a problem: it sabotages the effectiveness of your team and therefore your growth.

**Two rules**:

- **Delegate everything that anyone else can do at a lower hourly rate than you earn.**
- **Free up every minute possible for doing those things that only you can do.**

### The rules of delegation

**How to do it**:

1. **Define the task and output clearly.** What results do you expect? Agree on clear targets, goals and objectives. Define the specific outcome.
2. **Delegate to a person with demonstrated competence.** Ask about their experience. If they don't know what to do and haven't done it before, they are a trainee, not an "A" player. **"A" players produce results.**
3. **Set the rules: parameters and guidelines.** Explain the framework, the constraints and the rules.
4. **Explain the resources available**: the people, money, materials and tools.
5. **Give a schedule or deadline**: the timetable and time to completion.
6. **Invite questions and feedback. Even better, ask them to repeat it back to you.**
7. **Define what you will measure and how often.** Inspect what you expect. What gets measured is what gets done. What gets measured is what gets managed. You can't change it if you can't measure it.
8. **Define the consequences**, positive and negative, for performance or nonperformance: payment, rewards, incentives.

### Example: the room evacuation

A hypothetical delegation, done live with a volunteer:

**The task**: Get everybody in this room out of this room in fifteen minutes. You can't use the windows or the doors. The only resources available are **a rope and a ladder**.

1. **Check competence.** "Do you have any experience in emergency evacuations? Tell me about a couple." She describes evacuations she ran while serving in the Iraq war. (Had she said no, she would be a trainee, not an "A" player.)
2. **State the outcome, rules, resources and deadline.** Everybody out, fifteen minutes, no windows or doors, rope and ladder, and you'll lead the team.
3. **Don't accept "I understand."** Asked "Do you understand?", she answers "I hear you loud and clear." That proves nothing. So: "Could you do me a favor and repeat back to me what it is we're trying to do?" She repeats: "Our goal is to evacuate this room in the next fifteen minutes; we cannot use windows or doors; we have a ladder and a rope." Now you know she understands. Had she missed something, she might still have said she understood, and you'd have had a miss.
4. **Notice how resources dictate strategy.** With a rope and a ladder, the strategy is through the ceiling tiles. Change the resources to **a stick of dynamite and a shovel**, and the whole strategy changes: blow a hole in the wall. Change them to **a jackhammer and a shovel**, and it changes again: go under the wall. If you don't specify the resources and you happen to have a demolitions expert, you come back in fifteen minutes to find everyone out and a huge hole in your wall you weren't anticipating. Be clear about the resources.
5. **Pre-frame the check-ins with measurable standards.** "I'll be back to check your progress at eight minutes, and I'd anticipate thirty percent of the room has been evacuated. I'll be back at thirteen minutes, when I'd anticipate ninety percent." Without the pre-frame, when you show up at eight minutes she'll feel micromanaged. With it, you're not micromanaging; you're measuring progress you agreed on in advance.
6. **Require early warning.** "Should any problems come up or you get behind schedule, I need to know right away. I don't want to read about it in tomorrow's newspaper. This is critical."
7. **State the consequence.** "If we are successful, you get a gold star."

**Common mistakes this avoids**: not getting clarity on a person's expertise or execution intelligence; explaining a task without putting it into context; doing a poor job of checking back in, or of having the person report out.

### Reporting out vs checking in: transferring ownership

**What it is**: A better version of step 5: instead of *you* checking in at eight and thirteen minutes, *she* reports out. "At the end of eight minutes I need a report from you on how we're doing, against the standard of thirty percent evacuated. At the end of thirteen minutes I need another report showing ninety percent evacuated."

**Why it works**: **If they report out, you have transferred ownership of the job to them. If you have to check in, it is still your job.** Checking in is what moms do: "Did you make your bed? Did you brush your teeth? Did you pack your lunch? Did you do your homework?" At some point a mother stops asking about her child's teeth (typically around age seven or eight) and transfers ownership. A ninety-six-year-old mother on a thirty-minute call with her adult son never once asks whether he brushed his teeth; they are his teeth.

The same goes for accountability. "I need to figure out a way to hold people accountable" means you are still the mom: if you have to hold people accountable, you have to check in. **If they report out, they are accountable; they have ownership.** Your job becomes coaching and training so they have an opportunity to be successful.

### The Rules of Delegation worksheet

For any task you are delegating, fill in:

1. **Define the specific task or outcome** (a, b, c, d).
2. **Is there demonstrated competence?** (a, b)
3. **What resources are required?** (a, b, c, d)
4. **Define the time to completion** (a time for each milestone).
5. **Have they repeated the outcome or task back to you verbally?** Is help needed? Is this the right person for the task?
6. **Define specifically what to measure and how often** (measure / when, for each).
7. **Define the consequences**: positive and negative.

### Productivity techniques

1. Plan your day before it starts.
2. Read and write your outcomes (goals and promises) daily.
3. Develop a regular schedule.
4. Analyze your activities to identify and minimize time wasters.
5. Analyze your business and employees to identify and minimize time wasters.
6. Prioritize! Prioritize! Prioritize!
7. Use your day-timer system.
8. Write it down and think on paper.
9. Manage interruptions.
10. Work every day towards an outcome.
11. Control the telephone and email.
12. Learn how to terminate discussions.
13. Delegate (following the rules of delegation above).

### The eight steps to simplification

1. **Focus** on higher-value, more important outcomes and priorities.
2. **Concentrate** on key tasks, and stay at them until one hundred percent complete.
3. **Improve**: get better at your key tasks.
4. **Delegate** all lower-value tasks that can be done by someone at a lower income or salary.
5. **Consolidate**: do several tasks together.
6. **Outsource** all possible activities to specialty companies.
7. **Eliminate**: discontinue or abandon certain activities altogether.
8. **Delay**: defer tasks or actions that are not urgent or important today.

### The three rules for bringing you a problem

Require all peers and subordinates who have questions or problems for your attention to communicate with these three rules:

1. **Write the problem down.**
2. **What are the best three solutions or suggestions?**
3. **What do you recommend?**

## 6. Systems, Red Tape, Culture and Entitlement

### Systems and the red tape crisis

**What it is**: After delegation, the crisis is lack of control, so the emphasis shifts to systems and standardizing ("If we just had systems in place, that would solve most of our problems"). Taken too far, the result is an enormous amount of red tape.

**Why it happens**: **You can't deliver exceptional service unless you're willing to make exceptions, and exceptions are the enemy of the system you've created.** Systems are a wonderful tool, but overdo them and you commoditize or normalize what you do until nothing exceptional is going on. There are so many rules that it is almost impossible for customers to feel special, because the rules prevent the special from being delivered. Most larger organizations are filled with red tape, which prevents flexibility with their customers.

**Example: the missing passport.** A couple had to fly from Austin to Singapore on the day of a speech to about five thousand people: the flights had to be on time because there were only two hours between landing and being on stage. At the airline counter, with a long train of luggage, the agent asked for his passport. It was in his briefcase on the kitchen counter at home, the only item not in the luggage. With about an hour and a half before departure, they sent their driver back: thirty minutes home, thirty minutes back, in plenty of time for the flight. The problem was the luggage: if it wasn't checked in before the cutoff thirty minutes before departure, it wouldn't make the flight. He showed his driver's license; he was in their system as an elite customer; the agent could see him and his picture. "Sir, I cannot check you in without a passport." Mercifully, that agent went on break and another took over. He asked her: **"Do you feel like doing a miracle today?"** "As a matter of fact, I do." He explained the whole problem, with the driver about twenty minutes away on the phone. Her fingers started going and she said, "Put that luggage up here." She made an exception. That airline does lots of things wrong, but that was an instance where it took care of a customer. Everyone always wants to be the exception: "I know you've got rules, but I'm me."

### Arms and legs vs heads and hearts

**The rule**: **If you only need your employees' arms and legs, emphasize systems. If you need their heads and their hearts, be gentle with systems. Heads and hearts don't do well with systems.**

McDonald's is the king of systems and does a great job with what it does. But systems are really good at two things: **baking out passion and baking out flexibility**. If your business can get by without much passion and flexibility, systems will be your friend. If it needs more of either, be thoughtful about how much to dose them up.

**How to do it: systematize selectively.** At a live training event, everything about the stage setup is systematized: where everything is, what is in each place, how it looks every single time, with a checklist; the team knows exactly what the presenter likes. What is *not* systematized is the presenter's interaction with the audience. That part stays fluid, within guardrails, because giving people what they need requires flexibility. Make the lane too narrow and you take away part of their experience. In "operator mode" the presenter is often also in "creating mode," weaving in material he has never presented before because it fits the conversation. Rigid systems would have prevented that. So be tough on processes and systems in some places and allow more flexibility in others, in whatever way supports the customer.

### Pre-framing exceptions so they don't snowball

**The problem**: Exceptions can turn into snowballs and set a precedent, especially in a growing business.

**How to do it**: Pre-frame the exception so you set an expectation that it is a one-time event: "I understand the problem. I'm willing to make an exception, but that is what it is: an exception. We won't do this every time, but we'll do it this time to get everybody over the hump. That's not typically the way we do it." Without the pre-frame, the exception becomes the norm and snowballs.

**Example**: The norm at an airport is that you show up with your passport. Everyone knew that was the norm, and someone decided to make an exception. Since then, the passenger has always arrived with his passport; he never developed an expectation that he could check in for an international flight without one.

### Culture is not perks

**What it is**: Once red tape builds up, owners decide "I need culture," and most make the mistake of **substituting perks for culture**: paid time off, bigger bonuses, paying for health insurance, jelly beans in the kitchen, bringing dogs to work.

**Example: the nap rooms.** At a two-day event in Sweden with around seven thousand people in the audience, a very famous speaker two slots earlier described building culture by building two nap rooms for employees, because employees sometimes get tired. A speaker sitting in the front row (and slightly deaf, so unaware of how loudly he talks) said out loud, "You must be kidding me." She heard him. When he got on stage he threw out his planned talk and spoke about the difference between perks and culture.

**The distinction**: **Perks are the goodie bag. Culture is how we treat each other.**

- Culture is behavior.
- Culture is accountability.
- Culture is ownership.
- Culture is doing what we said we are going to do.
- Culture is measuring.
- Culture is please, thank you and you're welcome.
- Culture is caring about people, acknowledging people and valuing people.
- Culture is not jelly beans in the kitchen. Those are perks.

**Why perks backfire: entitlement.** When you approach culture through perks, the next problem you create is entitlement. You end up with a team of spoiled brats with their hands out, because you have been trying to be nice to them under the theory that it's culture. You have built a reward system where, like Pavlov's dog, people expect more goodies in the goodie bag in order to be happy. Nearly every business owner recognizes this. It happens because culture was never defined the right way.

**The fix**: Successful companies revert to the basics of ownership, flexibility and entrepreneurship: the seventh stage.

## 7. Simplify: The Five Questions

**What it is**: When you hit the ceiling of complexity and what you used to do stops working, simplify your thinking about growth and the next stage with five questions:

1. **Who do I want to buy from me?**
2. **What MUST happen to cause them to buy?**
3. **What MUST happen to keep them buying** (keep them coming back)?
4. **What could cause them NOT to buy?**
5. **Why aren't they buying from me now?**

**Why it works**: We get so attracted to, or absorbed in, taking action that we lurch for tactics instead of sorting out the underlying problem. Sometimes the problem is complex, and these questions are designed to simplify the issue of growth. They are astonishingly simple and powerful, and they lay the foundation for everything in the rest of this document (target market, the reasons people say no, keeping customers coming back).

**How to do it**: Use them as thinking-time questions and mark them for repeated future sessions. **If you spend less than ten hours of thinking time on these five questions, you probably haven't done them justice.**

## 8. The Twelve Pillars of Growth

### A platform built on pillars

If growth is the platform, what are the pillars that support it? The pillars below are a way to think about growth strategically, not tactically. There are twelve. You might think of one or two more, or want to drop one or two; the point is to think strategically. None of them is a magic bullet; each is "here's something else to think about" that can accelerate growth. After each one, ask: **How does this apply to me?**

### Pillar 1: Find out what they want (FOWTW, GAGI, GITT)

**What it is**: A success strategy in three steps:

- **FOWTW: Find Out What They Want.**
- **GAGI: Go And Get It.**
- **GITT: Give It To Them.**

**The story**: In the mid-1980s, at the height of a first fortune made in real estate and other industries, a business owner's secretary told him his two o'clock appointment had arrived. There was no two o'clock in his day-timer. A woman who owned (or sold for) a magazine had been calling every week for about six months to sell him advertising, and the secretary couldn't get rid of her. The plan: bring her in, and two minutes into the meeting he would get an "emergency phone call," and that would be the end of it.

He sat at his enormous desk (rumored to be made from wood from the Mayflower) pretending to think, and made a great show of not looking up. When he finally looked, a woman stood at the door, and he said: "I don't know what you're selling, but I'm buying all of it. Come on in here. You're by far the prettiest woman I've ever seen." When the fake emergency call came, he refused to take it. About forty-five minutes later she had told him about her advertising and said it didn't sound like a good fit. He asked how much she needed him to buy, and bought it for the next issue, though he didn't need it. For the next six, seven or eight months she came by every month, and he bought advertising he didn't need.

Then, in the late 1980s, the bottom dropped out of the economy. He had to take bankruptcy; his identity had been wrapped up in being successful in business, and he lost it. His relationship unraveled and he divorced. He rebuilt by taking turnaround jobs on the deal Lee Iacocca struck when he left Ford for Chrysler: pay me a dollar a year and give me fifteen or twenty percent of the stock; if I don't fix it, it only cost you a buck, and if I do, we all get rich. He fixed one company, then another, then another, and rebuilt his net worth.

In December 2000, about fifteen years after last seeing her, and about ten years after his divorce, he was at a Christmas party. A friend asked if he was dating anyone and suggested: "How about Sandy?" It was the woman at the door. He called her from his brick-sized car phone and asked her to dinner Friday at seven, offering to pick her up. "How about if I meet you there at seven?" (Not a good sign, but better than nothing.) They had a spectacular first date. At about ten o'clock he said: "I'm about to ask you a question I've never asked another woman in my whole life. What do you want? What do you want in a man? What do you want in a relationship?"

"That's easy. I want to be loved and adored."

He called the waiter over for a pen and paper and wrote: **"Sandy, January 4, 2001, loved and adored."** Then: "Now tell me every single thing that needs to happen for you to feel loved and adored." She talked for an hour and a half, and he wrote it all down. At one point she said, "And I want a note every day." He protested that was a lot of pressure. "What if I forget?" "I'll remind you." And she does: at three in the afternoon with no note, a text arrives: "Did the mailman break his leg?" Sometimes the notes are short ("I love you"), sometimes a poem, sometimes a little naughty, but she gets her note every day (the couple calls them "oh baby" notes). She finished with: "And I reserve the right to add to this list."

Then she took the pen and asked him what he wanted. His answer: to create **spectacular**, to live his life so that if he had one last breath and one last thought, it would be "Whoa, that was spectacular; I would gladly live it again." She asked him everything that needs to happen for him to create spectacular. There were only three things.

**Why it works**: For fifty years the mistake had been starting with step two: "What do I want to give you?" rather than "What do you want to get?" That is why his relationships weren't working. Business makes the same mistake: we try to give customers what *we* want to give them, because we are in love with our product, service or idea, instead of asking whether what they want matches what we've got.

**How to do it**:

1. Find out what they want (ask; write it down; ask "what needs to happen for you to feel that?").
2. Check whether what they want matches what you've got and want to give; go and get it.
3. Give it to them, **consistently**. If delivery and execution are inconsistent, they don't get what they want and the whole thing doesn't work.

**When they don't know what they want**: Many people who try this report back, "I asked what he wants, and he said he didn't know." If someone can't tell you, this pillar won't be very useful as asked. Maybe they know what they *don't* want. Reframe the question: **"When you think about the person who's currently supplying you with this product or service, if you could wave a magic wand and change any two things, what would you change?"** By telling you what they don't want, they give you a peek into what they do want.

**Pitfalls**: Asking customers what they want works poorly for truly disruptive products (see section 9 on innovation). And it doesn't mean giving people whatever they ask for when it won't solve their problem (see section 9 on wants vs needs).

### Pillar 2: The Gap (unmet frustration and white space)

**What it is**: The gap between what customers want and what the competition is doing. Is there unmet frustration, friction, pain or need? Is there white space where customers are frustrated and the competition is doing nothing about it?

**Example: Accor.** Accor, a French company, decided to enter the hospitality business and build hotels. Knowing nothing about hotels, they sent a team of young MBAs to hotels all over the world for five or six months to measure everything: whether there was a bellman outside, the carpet, which side check-in was on, how many check-in stations, the chandeliers, room service and the restaurant, how the bellmen were dressed, the elevators, thread counts on the sheets, the TV and cable system, amenity packages, one sink or two.

At the big meeting in Paris (starting at 10 AM), the CEO found himself in the men's room at the lunch break next to one of the MBAs, who didn't recognize him and let out a huge yawn. "Am I keeping you up?" The MBA apologized: he had just flown in from the United States, landed at about 5 AM, and gone to his hotel, where he was told check-in wasn't until four that afternoon, so he'd had no chance to freshen up after international travel.

The CEO said: "That's the gap." It isn't thread count. International travelers arrive in a city in the morning and want to check in and freshen up, but the room isn't ready, and what dictates whether the room is ready is the lowest-paid person on the staff, the maid. So Accor built hotels in destination cities with the promise that **your room will be available when you show up**, even at 7 AM. They charge a bit of a premium; they are not competing on price but on a very specific target market with a very specific pain. They can be average on thread counts, sinks, carpet and chandeliers, because what differentiates them is that they found a group of frustrated customers the competition was ignoring, filled that gap, and charged more for it. They made a great deal of money.

**How to do it**: Take thirty seconds and ask: Is there a group of frustrated customers who aren't getting their problem solved or their frustration alleviated by my competition?

### Pillar 3: Pain (is it an aspirin or a vitamin?)

**What it is**: Is your product, service or solution an **aspirin** or a **vitamin**?

**Why it works**: Aspirins sell themselves. When someone is in pain, they will be proactive about getting out of it. Vitamins you have to convince people to take: "This will be good for you," "You feel okay, but this will make you feel okay plus a little," "You have a deficiency, and this fixes it." But nothing is really wrong. Vitamin businesses can grow, but they are tough: they take more effort, more explaining, free samples, and constant motivation to keep people coming back. If you have an aspirin, you don't need much advertising; you just need to let people in pain know what you can do about it.

**How to do it**:

1. **If you have an aspirin, remind customers of the pain.** Many people with an aspirin don't do a good enough job of reminding customers of the pain they have, which the product alleviates. Help the customer understand the pain of their present situation, or the pain they might create if they don't choose you.
2. **If you have a vitamin, build in some pain communication.** Is there pain, frustration, irritation or inefficiency that your product alleviates or addresses? Message it so that, in the customer's mind, the product transitions from purely a vitamin to something that still has vitamin characteristics but also some aspirin: "We're going to fix something that's wrong, and you'll do better as a result."
3. Give this a thinking time or two. If you leave a vitamin as a vitamin, you will work much harder than if you spend the time on the messaging: the aspirin parts of what you do, how you do it, and the benefit of using your service or buying your product.

**Why businesses struggle here**: Some haven't identified what their niche target market wants. Some haven't defined the gap where frustrated customers are ignored. Some haven't thought through the language, messaging and promise that repositions their vitamin to carry aspirin characteristics.

### Pillar 4: Flexibility

**What it is**: The owner's or founder's flexibility to adjust what they offer to what the market wants. It is tied directly to pillar 1.

**Why it matters**: We often start with "go and get it" and fall so in love with our product that we can't see where it needs adjusting for the target market to see it as a solution. **A lack of growth is often a result of a lack of flexibility on the part of the owner.**

**Example**: You create something with the ear here and the eye up there. You take it to customers and they say, "No, we'd like the ear here and the eye there." You created it, you think it's beautiful the way it is, and you argue with them: "No, I want to give you the one with the ear up here." That argument is the growth problem.

**How to do it**: Engage with the audience and ask, "What do you want? What does it need to look like?" That covers not only the product, but how you need to message it, describe it and promise it, and the mechanics of how you deliver it. A talk can have the right message and the right words and still fail if the speaker delivers it from another room to an empty stage: something is missing because it doesn't meet expectations.

### Pillar 5: What your product offers vs what it does

**What it is**: The distinction, from Peter Drucker's work (start with his book *Managing for Results*), between what your product **does** and what it **offers**. It is another way of asking, "What business are you in?"

**Why it works**: We tend to communicate what the product does and forget the emotion, the feeling, what it really offers. What it offers is where both the pricing power and the ideas for serving customers come from.

**Example: the car wash.** A car wash business had unusually high revenue and profit averages, not because of its location or higher prices (competitors charged comparable prices for the same service). The owner realized that **people don't wash their car because it's dirty; they wash it because they want to feel better about themselves**, the same reason people shine their shoes. What the product *does* is get bugs off windshields; anybody can do that, including you with a bucket, a sponge and a hose. What it *offers* is **self-esteem**. "I'm in the self-esteem business."

If you are in the self-esteem business, the question becomes: how do we make people feel really good about themselves while they are here?

- **Recognition.** Repeat customers like to be recognized, called by name, and not sold what they were sold the week before. At Parleole Romanissimo's, a small cubbyhole Italian restaurant in New York, a regular who hadn't been in for three months would walk in to the staff announcing him by name, and they would rearrange things, remembering his drink and his favorite table; he felt important. The car wash borrowed the handheld "brick" that rental car attendants at Avis and Hertz use to punch in your license plate. Staff entered each plate as a car pulled in, which showed the owner's name, what they had bought the last ten times, how frequently they came and whether they were due for a detail. They greeted customers by name at the window ("Hello, Mr. [name], welcome back"), again at the cashier, and again when the attendant waved them off.
- **Free shoeshines.** The shoeshine man, Lawrence (with the business twenty-five years; a third-grade education and one tooth, and very smart), charged five or six dollars a pair. A shine took about ten minutes and he was there eight hours a day, so in theory he could shine forty, fifty or sixty pairs, but he was doing about ten: roughly fifty dollars a day, about a thousand dollars a month. His average tip was already four or five dollars a pair. The proposal: give shines away free and work for tips. Lawrence refused: "A man has to be paid for his work. You cannot give away your work. If this is such a good idea, why don't you give away car washes?" The owner guaranteed he would make at least a thousand a month for a two- or three-month trial. Lawrence countered: he had been paying a hundred dollars a month in rent, and if he agreed, he would never have to pay it again, no matter what. Done. With free shines he went from six to ten pairs a day to seventy-five to a hundred, and from a thousand dollars a month to four thousand dollars a month in tips.
- **Massage chair.** A free massage chair where customers could choose to tip.
- **Charity bar.** People feel better about themselves when they help other people. So the car wash installed a charity bar with free soft-serve ice cream, Cokes, gourmet coffee and popcorn, with a "charity of the month" box and a sign saying one hundred percent of proceeds go to that charity. It has given away hundreds of thousands of dollars.

**The economics**: If you compete on "we get bugs off windshields," revenue per car is about **$14**. If you compete on "we'll make you feel better about yourself," customers pay **$66**.

**How to do it**: Ask, "What does my product offer versus what does it do?" Then ask what else you could do to deliver what it offers.

### Pillar 6: Target market (go narrow and deep)

**What it is**: Clarity on who your ideal target customer is.

**Example 1: the business educator and EO.** Wanting more students, a business educator decided to tap organizations already attracting his ideal target market, smaller business owners (he had done a day with Procter & Gamble's senior leadership and it was unsatisfying for both sides: "they had a different size gun, and I was a different size bullet"). The Entrepreneurs' Organization (formerly the Young Entrepreneurs' Organization) had twenty-five thousand business owners worldwide. He worked hard on it for eighteen months: chapter speeches, relationships with senior leadership, speaking to thousands of members. Cumulative students from EO over eighteen months: **zero**.

The real target market was not young, relatively new or smaller businesses (under twenty or thirty million a year in sales). It was **people who are used to paying for their continuing education and who also happen to have a business**: people for whom this is not their first rodeo and who are willing to pay to accelerate their progress. EO members care about education, but the social aspect of the organization dwarfs it for many, and that is not what he offers. They may need what he has, but they won't pay for it.

**Example 2: the car wash.** At first the target market was "anybody with a car within three miles." Then "anybody with a dirty car within three miles." In the film *Beverly Hills Cop*, Eddie Murphy pulls up to the Bel Air Hotel in a junky, scratched, dirty car with a dented bumper and a crooked wiper, tosses the valet the keys and says, "Be careful. Look what happened last time I was here." If all you want is dirty cars, you get people with junky cars who want to spend a dollar, not sixty. The real target: **people with new cars**, because they are anxious about their car and want it to keep that showroom feel.

That narrowing told the business exactly where to go: auto dealerships selling the kind of car its customers drive, within a two- or three-mile radius. The pitch to dealers: "Next time you sell a car, to anyone, whether they live in my radius or not, hand your customer this coupon for unlimited services for the next thirty days." In theory someone could come in daily for a three-hundred-dollar detail, but they'd never be driving their car. In the history of the business, nobody abused it. People came two or three times, and the owner's view was: **if I can't get you to start paying me after I've given it to you two or three times, something's wrong with me, not you.**

**Why it works**: As soon as you define the target market, you know where to go, what to say, what message to communicate, which channels to use, and how often to communicate. The business educator has spent zero on Facebook advertising, because people get a feel for him by hearing him somewhere or being referred by someone who has. Most students are on their second, third, fourth or eighth course, and few have been to his website (which he admits "needs help"), because improving it won't sell more tickets.

**Pitfall: broad instead of deep.** If you consult with dentists, the entire universe of dentists is not your target market. Try to communicate to all of them and you end up with a watered-down message that doesn't convince even the people you'd most like to work with. The mistake is going broad instead of going deep. (For how narrow is too narrow, see pillar 12.)

### Pillar 7: Competition (a reason to discriminate in your favor)

**What it is**: Clarity on who your competition is, and what you do, offer or message that they don't.

**Why it works**: Growth means asking customers to **discriminate in your favor**, and you must give them a reason to. You can't communicate a reason if you don't understand what you do differently from everyone else, and you can't pinpoint that difference without a fairly deep understanding of what the competition does.

**How to do it**: Be able to say to yourself, and to a customer:

> "Here's what I do. Here's what they do. If you want that, you should buy from them. But if you want this, I'm your only choice. I think you want this."

Your job is to define **"this"**: the thing you do that they can't get somewhere else.

**Pitfalls**:

- "This" can't be customer service. "We do a great job of taking care of our clients" is pathetic, because everybody says it.
- The competition is not all bad. If it were, it wouldn't have any customers; it is doing some things right.
- Sometimes you are after de novo customers just entering the space, but often growth means causing someone who does business with somebody else to stop and start doing business with you. And once they do, you need them to come back for a second and third helping. **Getting someone to try you is easy. Getting them to come back for seconds is where the tough is.** That also requires messaging that differentiates you, which requires understanding the competition.

### Pillar 8: Certainty of success

**What it is**: Can you give the customer certainty of success? There are two parts: **success** and **certainty**.

**Part 1: Success is defined by the customer.** The customer is the final arbiter of what success looks like; theirs is the only opinion that matters. You can influence it through the expectations you set, and this is where businesses trying to grow often hurt themselves: they announce unachievable expectations, the customer tries them, the expectations are missed, and the customer is unhappy because they didn't get what was promised.

*Example: fancy restaurants vs McDonald's.* Most people have more upsets with fancy restaurants than with McDonald's. At a nice restaurant, something irritates you perhaps sixty percent of the time. Not the food, which is great, but a waiter who tries to impress a table of ten by memorizing everybody's order (what would impress is bringing what was ordered, so go get a pencil and paper), a waiter who interrupts in the middle of a story, or a steak that comes out overcooked and goes back to the kitchen while everyone else eats. McDonald's, by contrast, has set your expectations so precisely (reheated food wrapped in paper that tastes bad and isn't good for you, though the French fries are astonishing) that it never disappoints you. **Upsets come from expectations that were set and not delivered.** So understand the customer's definition of success, then build and deliver something that comes close to meeting it.

**Part 2: Certainty.** The customer is about to give you money for a product or service and may hesitate because they aren't sure you can actually do what they are paying for. Certainty removes that doubt. It can be a guarantee, money back, or a testimonial. Sometimes certainty is a **feeling**, not a commitment.

*Example: the airline promise.* On every flight, what you are paying for is getting from point A to point B safely **with your luggage**. When the luggage doesn't arrive with you, no airline has ever offered a partial refund. An airline could promise: "You and your luggage will arrive at point B simultaneously, and if that doesn't happen, your flight is free." Charge a little more, give away a few free flights, and attract a whole group of customers for whom it matters that their things arrive when they do.

*Example: the testimonial.* A father planning an annual guys' trip with his grown sons wanted to fish the Amazon in Bolivia for a week but couldn't find the right outfitter. Then he found one whose website carried a testimonial, with a photo, from Jerry Jones, owner of the Dallas Cowboys. Instantly: "If it's good enough for Jerry, it's good enough for me." He didn't know Jones personally, only of him, but a big name who wouldn't put up with bad service took him out of confusion and gave him certainty.

**How to do it**: Ask how to communicate so customers say, "I feel good about this. I'm confident that if I give them my money, I'll get what I think I want." Money back is lots of certainty (certainty they won't waste their money); if you can't do that, what can you do to eliminate some of the doubt?

**Pitfall: promises that create grief.** A car wash knew its customers' biggest problem was time: they pay money instead of washing their own car because it saves time. So it promised: **"In and out in twenty minutes, or it's free."** (Free *this* wash, not the next one: a restaurant that serves you something bad and offers a free dessert *next* time has it backwards; it screwed up this time.) The promise also set a guardrail that forced employees to tighten processes. Twenty-one or twenty-two minutes bothered nobody, thirty made people antsy. But soon a rash of other car washes' customers arrived with stopwatches: "Twenty minutes and three seconds. It's free." Someone brought in a car a raccoon and a litter of kittens had been living in, with fur and mess everywhere, which could take four days to clean, and the car behind it then couldn't be done in twenty minutes either, so two customers were about to be unhappy. The business had to unwind the promise gently, and for a year afterwards people still showed up saying, "It's been twenty-two minutes, this one's free." Staff gave those people one free wash, logged them in the license-plate system, and when they returned about three weeks later for another free one, helped them find somebody else to wash their car.

**Take thirty seconds**: Are there expectations we have set that we can't deliver?

### Pillar 9: Control (growth and control work inversely)

**What it is**: **Growth and control work inversely. The more control you have to have, the less growth you will have.**

**Why it happens**: Look up "control" in the dictionary and most business owners will see their own picture. It is how we had to start: at the beginning everything was an emergency room, and emergency rooms are command and control. There is zero time for consensus and no vote; someone directs traffic. As the organization gains mass and adds people, we still want to be in control, primarily because we haven't hired "A" players and haven't learned to delegate effectively. We have given the job to someone who is incompetent, can't do it, doesn't want to do it, or doesn't understand what the job is. We try to hold them accountable (still being the mom) instead of having them report out. So we stay in control, and the need to stay in control stunts growth.

**The only three reasons people don't do their job**: they don't know what to do, they don't know how to do it, or they don't care.

**How to do it**:

1. Onboard race horses, or at least not donkeys.
2. Onboard, train, engage, delegate to, coach and mentor someone who can support you in reaching the outcomes.
3. Design the role and articulate the outcomes, so someone else can do this while you do that.
4. Put it into a structure, stay engaged, and provide ongoing training, a guide, a mentor or a coach.

**The CEO's real role**: To make sure the people who work for you are as effective as possible at getting their jobs done and producing their outcomes: to coach, mentor and engage. At the CEO level you are not doing much of the work. One owner of several businesses didn't even have a key to get into some of them; he didn't need one. His job was not to run the operations; it was to run the business: to work with the leadership team and make sure they were effective, efficient, productive and trained, and to say, "If you have problems, raise your hand, because my job is to make sure you're successful. I'll coach you, guide you and encourage you, but I'm not going to do your job."

### Pillar 10: Execution (consistency is the competitive advantage)

**What it is**: Consistent execution, which is a rarity.

**Example: the lost strategic plan.** The CEO of Bank of America left the bank's strategic plan for the coming year in the seat pocket of an airplane. The next passenger found it and within twenty-four hours had contacted the CEO, who thanked him and invited him to drop it off. The finder remarked that the CEO didn't seem very concerned. The CEO said he was totally unconcerned: "You could publish it tomorrow on the front page of the Wall Street Journal, and I wouldn't bat an eye. The key is not whether we can create a plan; it's whether we can consistently execute on the plan we've created." His problems had little to do with strategic planning (he had a team for that); they came when a teller or loan officer didn't do it the way the bank wanted and customers got upset.

**Why it works**: In the book *Execution*, Larry Bossidy (vice chairman of GE, later chairman of Allied) argues that **the biggest competitive advantage a business can have is consistency of execution**. A miss in consistency makes customers nervous about whether, if they pay you, they will get what they got last time. McDonald's has this advantage: a hamburger flipper who innovates gets fired, because the burger must be made exactly the same way every time, and the result is a product that tastes and looks the same every time, whether you like it or not.

**How to do it**: If you have a McDonald's kind of business, amping up systems is a great idea. If you need your employees' heads and hearts as well as their arms and legs, too many systems will kill passion (systems bake out passion and flexibility), so dose them thoughtfully. Execution also involves compensation, "A" players, culture, designing the org chart, and critical drivers. Growth is often sabotaged not by bad plans or bad strategy but by problems with the consistency of execution.

### Pillar 11: How you do it, not what you do

**What it is**: Most of us spend the majority of our time figuring out **what** to do and very little figuring out **how** we do it. The how is what separates you from everyone else.

**Example**: Anybody can get the bugs off your windshield. Lots of people can teach business. The difference is not what they do but how they do it. Southwest Airlines puts butts in seats and moves them safely from point A to point B. So does American Airlines. So does United. Yet one of them has made all the money. Was it because of what they did, or how they did it?

**How to do it**: Ask whether your differentiator lies on the "how you do it" side rather than the "what you do" side, or both. Your product may well need tweaking, updating, making more useful or longer lasting. But also be thoughtful about how you deliver it, how you message it, the promise you make, the certainty you provide, the white space you fill, the team you have and your need for control, and whether your target market needs refining.

### Pillar 12: The real estate you control in the customer's mind (your niche)

**What it is**: The real estate you control in the consumer's mind. Put another way: what is the niche you are known for? Where are you the expert? You can't be the expert everywhere.

**Why it works**: The more narrowly you define the niche where you are the expert, the less you have to spend on marketing and advertising. The broader you are, the more money you must spend to announce yourself and become the expert.

**Example: the Grand Canyon.** The Colorado River carved the Grand Canyon: water carved through rock. What allowed that was the boundaries on the river. The closer the boundaries, the more power the river had. Had the boundaries been three hundred miles apart, you'd have a mud puddle instead of the Grand Canyon, because the volume of water would be spread over three hundred miles. Narrow the boundaries, create the niche, and the force of the water becomes great enough to carve through rock.

**Example: car brands.** Say Rolls-Royce and you can picture the type and wealth of person who owns one. BMW: the ultimate driving machine. Mercedes: engineering. Ford Pinto: fire. Prius: green. Lamborghini and Ferrari each trigger a thought. Volvo: safety. What is the likelihood Volvo is the safest automobile on the planet? Zero. Volvos are safe (and look like a box on wheels), but they bought the story and they control that space. Say General Motors and you've got nothing. GM is big, but broad, and controls no real estate.

**Warren Buffett**: "Do you know the difference between very rich people and very, very rich people? **Very, very rich people say no to almost everything.**" The richer you get, the more opportunities you are presented with, and the more you must ask whether each is in or out of your lane.

**How to do it**:

1. Define your lane: your target customer, your real estate, where you are the expert.
2. Set guardrails, and say no to what is outside your lane.
3. Plant a flag: "I'm really, really good at this, probably one of the best that's ever lived. If you've got this problem, I can help you." Customers may have other problems you can also help with, but you plant the flag in one place and build toward it.

**Pitfall: widening your boundaries.** Businesses stop growing when they widen their boundaries and try to be all things to all people. That commoditizes the product and service and pollutes the brand: "You've got this problem? We solve that. And that one? We solve that too." Maybe you can solve all those problems, but that is not what you are known for. This is not a message that you can never broaden; it is a message that a major engine of growth is clarity on the real estate you control.

### Pillar 12 in depth: aren't niche markets too small to scale?

**The objection**: Niche markets are smaller and have lower economies of scale, so don't they limit growth potential?

**The answer**: You certainly can create a niche so small that there isn't enough audience to give you mass, and you want to avoid that.

*Example: Omarama.* Driving without a map on New Zealand's South Island, a couple passed a sign for the town of Omarama (a gas station and little else). About two hundred yards past the gas station, a hand-lettered sign lying against a fence read "Omarama Golf and Country Club." Down a gravel road, through a stream, a couple thousand yards on: three parking places, a clubhouse shack that looked like a hot dog stand, a nine-hole course, sheep everywhere, and one golfer trudging along with three clubs in his hand and his pockets stuffed with balls. You can make a lot of money in golf, but there weren't enough people there for that idea to get traction; unless they teach sheep to play golf, it won't work.

*The too-narrow example*: "I'll only work with white males between twenty-five and twenty-five-and-a-half with between $3.1 and $3.2 million of revenue, exactly fifteen employees, living in Austin, Texas, who own their own house and aren't living in their parents' basement." That is so tight there isn't a minimally viable audience.

**But the real problem most people have is the opposite**: they believe that because they want to be big, they must start with a big target audience.

*Force = mass × acceleration.* A great big battering ram has so much mass it needs little acceleration: pull it back a little, let go, and it knocks down the castle doors. If you are relatively small and want to penetrate a market, a battering ram is not your friend. Instead, **sharpen the tip of the spear**. Most successful business people will tell you that one of the keys at the beginning is sharpening the tip of the spear. You have heard of a minimally viable product; there is also a **minimally viable market**. Instead of trying to conquer the world on day one, get traction, get a foothold, penetrate, and become the expert. Don't get too narrow, but don't get too wide.

**Why guardrails accelerate growth instead of limiting it**: Guardrails create real estate in the consumer's mind, and that accelerates growth. They don't cap it, because at any time you can decide to widen a little, add a feature or address a different market. What they ask is whether you are prepared to say no to things outside the tip of your spear. If you are already a billion-dollar company, you are certainly looking for new markets to conquer. But if you are a two-million-dollar company that wants to be a fifty-million-dollar company, one of the keys is making sure the tip of your spear is really, really sharp. The advice from anyone acting as a million-dollar investor in your company would be the same: you can get too restrictive, and you can get too wide; find the place where you can penetrate, establish a reputation and a market, and be the expert. **Penetration is the key. Get a foothold.**

**The shiny penny problem**: Staying in your lane is hard. You have clarity on where you are and where you're going, and partway there, out of the corner of your eye, you see something really cool. You race over, pick it up, play with it, drop it ("nah, that wasn't quite it"), head back toward your line, and spot another one. If you're not careful, you spend all your time zigzagging. Everyone has this problem for one reason: **smart people are curious** (a sloth is not). The fix is not a frontal lobotomy; it is a **learning day** or a **shiny penny file** where you park all the cool things you are interested in so curiosity doesn't sabotage your progress or pull you wide.

## 9. Wants vs Needs, Innovation and Expectations: Pillar Questions Answered

### When what they want conflicts with what they need

**The question**: "Find out what they want" can conflict with what they actually need to solve their problem. One approach is to poke the pain harder, driving into the problems they're having, and redirecting after the sale toward solving the actual problem without crossing your guardrails. How should you think about wants vs needs?

**Example: the cheesecake.** You run a restaurant. A customer who weighs four hundred pounds comes in and orders a slice of cheesecake. The last thing they need is cheesecake; what they need is a cup of spinach. You sell both. Telling them "I know you want cheesecake, but I'm going to give you spinach" won't work; it will irritate the customer and destroy the sale.

**How to think about it**:

1. **Think in terms of gains they want to get and pains they want to avoid.** You may serve the cheesecake not because they need it but because they want it.
2. **Educate at the right moment.** The opportunity is not at the time of the order, but when you introduce what you do: educate people on the aspirin (or the vitamin) you offer. Cheesecake can be the initial transaction, and later conversations can make them aware that you also have spinach.
3. **From a business perspective, the key is what they want to pay for, not what you want to give them.** If what they want to pay for is cheesecake, and cheesecake is what you want to serve, make a really good cheesecake and figure out how to keep them coming back.
4. **Redirect as the expert when the request won't work.** People often tell you what they want, and you can redirect: "I can do that for you, but not in the time frame you're requiring," or "it's not sustainable," or "it's not in my sweet spot, because I've found it doesn't solve the underlying problem." Then reframe the conversation around what you, as the expert, think they need rather than what they think they want.

**Script**:

> "Look, I know that's what you want, and certainly we can do that. The problem is that when we're finished, you're going to be disappointed with the sustainability or the outcome or the results, because that's what's happened to most people who've told me that's what they want. Instead, you can get to the outcome you want, but there's a different way to do it. That's what I'm offering: a solution that will get you to your outcome. I can help you be more successful; I just can't do it in the next fifteen minutes. This is a journey, a process, and a lot of it depends on the work you decide to do to support the outcomes and the solution we provide."

**The core principle: align on the outcome.** As long as you and the customer are aligned on the outcome they are striving for, *how* they get there is the part you can help redirect. Once you're aligned on the outcome, it's your job to design the machine that delivers it. Contrast the seminar promoters who promise you can get rich sitting in your bathrobe fifteen minutes a day: they promise outrageous things because that is what people want, and it rarely works, including for the person selling it.

**Qualifying questions instead of walking away**: You don't have to walk away. Ask qualifying questions to find out what they really want. If they want cheesecake because they're hungry and want to be full for the next five hours, you can go to the spinach. The want is often on the surface and the need is deeper. Stay in an engaged conversation, help them see that what they want is a certain outcome, that there are several ways to get there, and that "our way has been proven to be incredibly effective; we have a lot of customers who are very happy with their results."

**When to walk away**: If a customer wants something you don't supply, that customer is probably not your target, and you should probably walk away. Otherwise you pollute and dilute what you are trying to do for your real target audience by trying to do something where you don't have much confidence it will work.

### Everyone is lazy, greedy and impatient

Ask anyone: all other things being equal, would you rather have something sooner or later? Easier or harder to get? Cheaper or more expensive? Sooner, easier, cheaper. **All of humanity is lazy, greedy and impatient.** But that doesn't mean the world works that way. Sometimes the solution will look different than the customer imagined, cost a little more, or take a little longer, but the outcome they want is there. Your work is helping them understand the outcome.

### The triangle of death: price, time, quality

**What it is**: The machine you design cannot optimize for three things simultaneously: **price, time and quality**. As a leadership team, and as a customer, **you get to pick two**.

**Why it matters**: If you try to optimize for all three at once (the lowest price, the fastest time and the highest quality), there is a **one hundred percent probability that you will die**. You will be hard-pressed to find a company that has successfully pulled it off.

**Example**: Building a financial software product, a business owner wanted it done at a low price (lower than he ended up paying), really quickly, and at high quality. The developers kept telling him: "You can only pick two. Whichever ones you pick, we have to let go of the other one. You can have high quality in a short time, but we have to jack up the price." Everyone wants what isn't there, and you can't give it to them and be successful as a business.

### Customers who don't know they have the problem

**Example: the R&D tax credits.** A technical company filed for R&D tax credits every year and believed it was getting everything it could. A firm pursued them for more than a year, kept telling them how it was helping companies like theirs, and kept referring them to people who had gotten the same benefit. When they finally listened, they recovered **hundreds of thousands of dollars**, going back to 2017, that they would never otherwise have had.

**The Challenger Sale approach**: It takes a really good salesperson (the top five percent). Find an industry (or industries) that share a problem they don't know they have. Go in and educate the customer about the problem, convince them they have it, and, without ever talking about the solution, show them the hole they are in. When they finally say, "Boy, I have it. What do I do about that?", answer: "I've got that for you." It is a very effective way to sell, but very difficult.

**Three situations to recognize**:

- Sometimes people don't even know they have the problem they have.
- Sometimes people come to you with a problem, but the problem they think they have is not the real problem; it's something else.
- In both cases you need the ability to redirect the conversation, remind them, or educate them: "Here's an opportunity, here's a problem, and **if you see the problem like we do, here's a possible solution**." Part of the job is helping them understand the problem they actually have, the guardrails they face and the outcomes they want to achieve.

### Innovation: "they would have said a faster horse"

**The question**: Henry Ford supposedly said that if he had asked people what they wanted, they would have said a faster horse. Where do innovation and vision fit with "find out what they want"?

**The answer**: Ford was disrupting with an entirely new technology. If Steve Jobs had asked people what they wanted, they probably wouldn't have come up with the iPhone; he had to intuit it. (Interestingly, Jobs fought against the iPhone internally for four years while his team kept telling him there was a way to do it.) **When a business is truly disruptive, asking customers what they want is not that useful.** But most businesses are not disruptive. Most provide a service that **rhymes** with a service someone else provides (cosmetics and Botox, helping dentists, car washes and hundreds of others), and the job is to get traction, grow and build. For those, finding out what customers want works.

**Innovation still matters for everyone.** Innovation is critical regardless of industry.

**Example: Lenox China.** In the late 1900s, Lenox was one of hundreds of china makers and wasn't getting traction. Its primary audience was young brides, the people who wanted china, and young brides didn't have any money, so sales languished. The owner's breakthrough, borrowed from an entirely different industry, was the **bridal registry**: if the bride can't afford it, maybe all her friends can. One friend buys a cup, another a saucer, and with enough friends she has place settings for twelve. Had he asked brides what they wanted, they would have said "twelve place settings of china," which gives no clue to the answer. He had to do other thinking: the person who wants it can't afford it, and the people who can afford it aren't the ones getting married, so how do I put something together? That is the result of thinking time.

### Using thinking time on your own specific problem

**Example: the dentist's dilemma.** A dentist in Paris who does surgery, bone grafts and implants bills by the hour. Routine checkups bring in only about twenty-three euros each, and over the year there are too many of them. How can he make patients feel special without spending so much time that he destroys his hourly rate?

**How to work it** (rather than waiting for someone to hand you the answer):

1. Keep the economics in mind. Delivering exceptional service or making the business addictive must not turn the economics upside down so that you lose money.
2. Write the question down and take it into thinking time, using quality questions.
3. Research: are there other people who have had the same problem, or one that rhymes with it, and how did they solve it?
4. Identify your guardrails. Do you have flexibility on price, or is it set by the state, the government or insurance? That changes everything.
5. Look for a way to deliver exceptional service in an amount of time appropriate to your economics.

## 10. Strategic Growth: Internal Strengths + External Needs = Opportunity

### The formula

**What it is**: A template for thinking strategically rather than tactically about growth:

**Internal Strengths + External Needs = Opportunity**

There are internal strengths you have and external needs (pains) in the market. If you can marry your internal strengths with external needs, you may find an attractive opportunity, meaning a money-making opportunity.

**Why it works**: Growth may have more to do with strategy than with adding Facebook marketing. Facebook isn't the issue; the issue is the **sequence** in which you think about it: strategy first, tactics after.

### Internal strengths: leverageable or under-utilized assets

**How to find them**: **Think, analyze, question.**

The list of possible leverageable or under-utilized assets:

1. Skills
2. Expertise
3. Customer base
4. Knowledge
5. Network
6. Testimonials
7. Bell cow
8. Access to influence
9. Alliances
10. Referrals
11. Capacity
12. Core competence
13. Brand / reputation
14. Excess resources
15. Competitive strength

**The bell cow**: On a cattle ranch, you drive feed out to the herd every day, and not every cow hears the truck. So you find the cow that always hears the truck and tie a bell around her neck. When she hears the truck, she waddles toward it because she knows there's hay on the back, and all the cows that can't hear the truck hear the bell and follow her. In business, a bell cow is a customer, alliance or relationship that others follow. If you know Oprah, you're set. If Warren Buffett has agreed to commit a million dollars to your deal, people don't even need to hear about the deal: "Warren's in? I'm in." A bell cow is extremely powerful, and having one is an internal strength.

### External needs: the environment, competition and market

**How to find them**: **Ask, probe, listen.** Switch hats and look outward at the competitive environment. The market has pains it wants to avoid and gains it wants to get.

1. Pain to avoid
2. Gain to get

**Opportunity without structure is chaos.** What is the "structure" I need to take advantage of the opportunity I see? An opportunity requires thinking through:

**Customers you have vs customers you want**: Many businesses are stuck because the customers they want don't rhyme with the customers they have. Getting the customers they want may require reformulating the machine, which may cause breakage with the customers they have. It is almost an Intel-style problem (see the Andy Grove question): migrating to the new thing may mean abandoning what you have, which is a very dangerous proposition, but sometimes it is required because the market has shifted.

**Pitfall**: Sometimes the opportunity is simply enhancing what you currently do; sometimes it is in an entirely different market. Either way, it will require structure and resources.

## 11. Why People Say No (and Why Buyers Say Yes)

### The better question than "how do I find more customers?"

**What it is**: When business owners want to grow, they ask "How do I find more customers?" and pivot to tactics: SEO, social media, more leads, a bigger funnel, without clarity on the real problem. The strategic question is:

**Why do the people who already know about me, and who need what I've got, say no?**

**Why it works**: We keep trying to find a bigger mountain with a bigger bullhorn and scream a little louder to reach more potential customers, when there is already a whole group of potential customers who have heard of us, know about us, and said "not a fit." **We are presenting our product or service to far more people than are buying.** Finding out why people *don't* buy from us is probably **THE** most important question we can ever get answered. Then we either know what to change to turn the "no" or "maybe" into a "yes," or we find out these people are not our target. **Either way, it is a win.**

**Pitfall**: The mistake most business owners make is to keep expanding their mailing lists, look for new markets to flog their product to, read books on techniques to increase their closing percentage, and launch social media and search engine optimization programs to boost sales, instead of finding out why the people they are already talking to aren't buying. Dumb! Until the underlying reasons for the "no" have been identified, you are a scratch looking for an itch, doomed to brute-force and whack-a-mole marketing, both of which are prescriptions for being **tired instead of rich**.

### The four reasons people say no

The "No" sits in the middle of four quadrants: **Risk, Friction, Success and Difference.** Resolve them, and the "Yes!" gear turns, with price and convenience as the smaller gears alongside it.

**Reason 1: They perceive a risk.** Something could happen that they won't be happy about. It may not be a real risk, only a perceived one, but they think your solution isn't a fit. They know the devil they're dancing with, and dancing with you is a risk they may not want to take. They may be uncertain that if they pay the money they'll get the result. Maybe your software will interfere with their other software. Maybe they'll buy it and it won't get used.

**Reason 2: They perceive friction.** Switching carries friction, and friction creates inertia. Almost anyone who has considered changing accounting systems has run into "Oh, that's going to create a lot of problems," and those who did it experienced them. Adopting a new CRM carries huge friction; if you're on Salesforce, the friction of unwinding it is monumental. So people say, "I'm not that happy with what I've got, but the friction of starting over is breathtaking." Changing doctors carries gigantic friction; the "brain damage" of switching primary physicians is huge.

*Example: the email gate.* A voracious reader (two to three books a week for thirty-five years, with a library full of unread material, and who would spell growth "L-E-A-R-N") is constantly sent articles and websites to check out. More often than not he goes, and the first thing many sites do is demand an email address and contact information. He clicks out. "If you've got something you want to tell me, let me look at it; if it's good enough, I'll subscribe." Demanding contact information before he knows whether the content is any good creates friction that makes him bail before they ever had a chance to make him a customer. Collecting information up front can be a great approach, and if it works for you, great; just be aware of the friction it may cause, because friction makes potential customers who need what you've got say no.

**Reason 3: They don't perceive a difference that makes a difference.** Your messaging must contain some kind of promise: "Here's what we do. Here's what they do. If you want that, buy it from them. If you want this, I'm kind of your only choice." It has to be clear, different and meaningful. Often we message features of the product instead of the difference that makes the difference between us and someone else. When your target market knows about you but is confused, or doesn't see the difference, they are unlikely to buy.

*Pitfall: discounting instead.* When we haven't been clear about the difference that makes the difference, we put our stuff on sale. We are essentially saying, "I'm not creative enough to come up with a reason you should choose me other than a discount." Years ago, during four or five months with nothing to do, one man got messages almost every day from AT&T: switch to us and get three months of free service and free installation. He had been an AT&T customer for twenty years and they had never offered him anything. He called: "I'm an existing customer, and I want the discount." "No, the discount is only for new customers. We realize you've been paying our bills for twenty years, but you can't have a discount." "Then I quit." "Oh, if you're going to quit, now you can have the discount." There has to be a better way. Sometimes a discount is right, and if you need to offer one, do it, but it should feel like a last resort. It usually means you haven't done enough thinking about the real difference, or you aren't creative or innovative enough, and you've resorted to the lowest common denominator, lowering the perceived value of what you do.

**Reason 4: They lack certainty of success.** A two-part conversation: first, how do they define success? Second, can you reduce or eliminate the friction or perceived risk that is stopping them from buying?

**Why it works**: Most people, if they had certainty of success, were introduced to your product or service, and saw a meaningful difference that made the difference, would buy. Something is in the way, and part of the CEO's and leadership team's job is to get clarity on those friction points and risks, even risks that aren't real.

*Example: enrolling in a course.* Everyone who enrolls in an intensive four-day course feels some risk: it's a lot of money, it's four days away, and an online participant in Australia may have to stay up all night. Somehow that got resolved for those who enrolled. Many others heard about it and need it, but hit a speed bump: "I'm not sure. Maybe I'll wait and see. I've got enough going on. Besides, I don't even know if he's any good. The safest thing would be to do nothing." More people chose to do nothing than enrolled.

**Understanding why people say no is a huge strategic competitive advantage**, because it lets you rework your messaging, your promise or the structure of what you do to alleviate the friction, the risk, the lack of certainty, or the lack of clarity on the difference that makes the difference.

### No, YES! and "yes?": segment your answers

You have managed to convert some portion of the audience from a "No" into a "YES!" (or maybe a "yes?"). Business is not a bathrobe: one size doesn't fit all, and different market segments have different considerations. Answer these questions (for each segment):

**Unsold buyers (NO)**:

1. What **risks** does the unsold buyer perceive that are preventing them from making the switch? (How can you give them "certainty of success"?)
2. What **friction** points does the unsold buyer have that are preventing them from buying?
3. What is the **DIFFERENCE that makes the DIFFERENCE** to the unsold buyer?
4. How, specifically, does the unsold buyer define **"success"**?

**Buyers (YES)**:

1. Why did these people say yes? (What must happen for this target to say "YES!"?)
2. What must happen to keep them coming back for seconds?
3. What must happen for them to become addicted and refer?

### If the marketing is done right, selling becomes unnecessary

Peter Drucker: **"If the marketing is done right, selling becomes unnecessary."** Drucker defined marketing as figuring out the red-hot, searing pain and offering a product or service that alleviates the pain and solves the problem. Aspirins sell themselves.

## 12. The Success Proposition Canvas

### What it is

A one-page picture of how a business should talk to its market.

- **The market (right side, the target market)** is outward facing. Customers are saying, "We have jobs and tasks, problems and needs." But underneath those, every target customer has **gains to get** (hope) and **pains to avoid** (fear).
- **The business (left side)** is also outward facing: "We have products and services we want to sell." Most businesses take those products and services, build features and benefits around them, put them in a nice package and market them.
- **The bridge in the middle**: instead of features and benefits, the business presents **gain creators** and **pain relievers**, packaged together ("gain + pain") and delivered through marketing. Two kinds of arrows connect the sides: **Ask / Listen** (flowing from the market's gains and pains back to the business) and **Educate** (flowing from the business's gain creators and pain relievers out to the market).
- The bumper sticker on the canvas: **"Instead of falling in love with our products, we should fall in love with our customer's outcomes."**

### Why it works

Customers buy products and services to **create a gain or avoid a pain**. The only reason your product is meaningful to a customer is that it alleviates a pain or creates a gain. Nobody walks into Best Buy and says, "Give me fifty dollars' worth of technology." You pay for the technology, but you want it to solve a problem or create an opportunity.

Typically we think of customers as having problems or tasks, so we market by describing features and benefits. When we aren't selling enough, we get seduced into thinking we need to tweak and modify the product. As business owners we rarely ask what pains or gains customers are dealing with, and even rarer is the customer who can accurately and precisely articulate them. But if you can discern the **real** pain and gain conversations customers are having in their heads, you can "package" and communicate your products and services so they meet the customer's **emotional needs**: customers have a **fear** about some pain or a **hope** around some gain.

**Raving fans and addicted customers are not created by product enhancement**, but by meeting their emotional needs of gain creation (hope) and pain avoidance (fear).

**Strategic vs tactical**: Starting the conversation with gains and pains is a strategic approach to growth. Spending your time discussing features and benefits is the tactical, selling-heavy approach. You can get there that way, but it is harder.

**Marketing done right**: Instead of trying to convince customers, start by asking what gains they want and what pains they want to avoid: let them educate you. That is market research. Drucker said selling becomes unnecessary if the marketing is done right; at minimum, selling becomes less central, because you have understood the gains and pains the market has. Once you have asked and listened, you don't talk about features and benefits; you talk about gain creators and pain relievers, because that is what customers want to talk about.

**The definition of a target market**: A target market exists when you have accurately found people who have gains and pains and you are able to position what you do as a gain creator or pain reliever for them. Then you communicate, educating them on how your product's gain creators and pain relievers match the gains they want and the pains they want to avoid.

### Success proposition, not value proposition

Marketing professors and gurus talk about the **value proposition**. What customers want is **success**: to leave the transaction saying, "Yes, that was it. That's what I wanted." That is a **success proposition**, and it is very different from a value proposition. The only way to deliver on a success proposition is to be clear on the gains and pains.

Instead of falling in love with your product, fall in love with your customer. If you're going to fall in love, at least fall in love with something that can love you back. Your business doesn't love you as much as you love it, and your product doesn't love you, but your customers can.

### How to do it

1. **Be a detective.**
2. **Ask customers lots of questions.**
3. **Listen closely to the answers and to the reasoning and explanations.** Listen for fears and hopes.
4. **Develop a communication strategy that wraps your product or service in a "package"** that addresses the customer's hopes and fears.
5. **Train and retrain sales and marketing people on the process of finding gains and pains.** That is far more critical than extreme product knowledge.

**The bumper sticker**: Instead of falling in love with our products, we should fall in love with our customer's outcomes and emotional needs.

**The thinking-time question**: How might I create a value proposition that focuses on a gain creator or pain reliever so that my target market has **less risk**, **less friction**, is **clear on the difference that makes the difference**, and **meets their definition of success**?

## 14. The Ultimate Blueprint: 24 Strategies for an Insanely Successful Business

### The silent saboteur: "How do I get big so I can get rich?"

That one question is the silent saboteur of small businesses. A better question: **"What did the big guys do that caused the profits and cash flow that allowed them to get big?"**

The answer is not just one thing. Business is complex, and anyone who says it isn't is either stupid or trying to sell you their flavor-of-the-month Kool-Aid. Business owners struggle financially because they are too busy "growing and operating" the business and not running the business end of the business. It's not that they don't want to; it's that no one ever taught them how.

Warren Buffett: "If you can't read the scoreboard, you don't know the score. If you don't know the score, you can't tell the winners from the losers." You cannot create and sustain business success without business skills and tools. When you learn the tools that work, you get the results that are possible. If you master this information, your chances of business and financial success increase dramatically; if you don't, your chances of frustration and failure are guaranteed.

On a bumper sticker: **Great operators get tired. Great business owners get rich!**

The 24 strategies below are a few of the critical drivers of an insanely successful business. Each ends with a question for thinking time.

### Management, thinking and measurement

1. **There are very few lousy businesses, but there are a ton of lousy business owners.** Look at your industry and count the competitors knocking the cover off the ball: same industry, similar product, different management. *Where are your business skills lacking?*

2. **Thinking Time and Measuring Time are the two highest forms of leverage.** Look for problems before they arise, be skeptical about what could go right, face reality by consistently measuring, find the root cause that produced the poor results, and build a machine that will change it.

3. **Business is an intellectual sport.** The book is called *THINK and Grow Rich*. The only constant in business is change; if you do what you've always done, you will fail. *When is the last time you scheduled some serious Thinking Time?*

4. **Thinking Time is a core leverage tool you must add to your business tool belt.** The key to getting rich is to avoid doing stupid things. All stupid mistakes are the result of faulty assumptions, and faulty assumptions are just another way of saying "it was a good idea at the time." All problems start out as a good idea. *How much money would you have right now if you hadn't thrown it away on bad investments and ill-conceived, looking-good, impulsive, instant-gratification expenditures (all good ideas at the time)?*

5. **What gets measured is what gets done. What gets measured is what gets managed. What gets measured and reported improves exponentially.** *What are you measuring, and how are you being held accountable?*

6. **The formula for creating the results you desire**:
   - Get clear (measure) on where you are.
   - Decide what is non-negotiable (the goal).
   - Identify the root problem (cause) blocking this progress, and make it intolerable.
   - Build a machine that addresses the problem (execute the change) and measure your progress.

   *Are you using this formula to create the results you want?*

7. **The best in the world at what they do are measuring freaks.** Warren Buffett, like all masters, understands that the slightest numerical variation can mean the difference between stupendous success and catastrophic failure. *Do you know what to measure? Do you know what to change when you do measure?*

8. **Management makes decisions. Decisions get converted into activities. Activities become results.** In other words: **Lousy Management = Bad Results.** *What new business skills and tools do you need to learn?*

### Profit and sustainability over size

9. **Gorging yourself on growth rarely solves financial problems.** Worshipping at the altar of getting big as fast as possible is little more than business pornography: exciting to think about, but when you're done looking, the results rarely equal the stimulation. The goal is to get rich, not get big, and the way to get rich and stay rich is to maximize the profits and cash flow your business generates. **Getting big is the result of success. Success is not the result of getting big.** *Do you know what to do to maximize profits and cash flow?*

10. **The goal of business is profitable sustainability, not size.** No one wants to be big (or rich) for a week. You can't sustain something unless you know what is working and what isn't, and the only way to know for sure is to: measure your progress and results; identify, correct and improve the activities causing those results; measure your progress and results again; identify, correct and improve again; and so on. An addiction to big, luck and "hopium" are not strategies. *What are your addictions?*

### Financial analysis: the language and the scoreboard

11. **Business and financial analysis are to business owners what blood tests and MRIs are to doctors.** Sustainability requires looking under the hood to locate the silent but deadly killers that will sabotage you every time. *How much is slipping through the cracks in your business?*

12. **Business, like every other activity, has a language, and the language of business is accounting.** Not the debits and credits, but what's going on with the three accounting report cards, how they fit together, what they mean, and how to use them to figure out what needs to change. If you can't speak the language, you can't play the game. *Do you speak the language of business?*

13. **Accounting is the process of converting activities into numbers** and placing them on a financial report card. **Business and financial analysis is the process of converting the numbers on the report card back into activities.** Change the activities, change the numbers. *Can you look at your numbers and know which activities must change?*

14. **Change the activities, change the results.** Financial analysis is about reverse engineering the numbers to find the activities causing the offending results. You cannot change the numbers without first changing the activities that caused them. *Do you know how to drive your profits and cash flow?*

15. **Business is a game.** Like any game there are winners and losers, fierce competition and a set of rules; bad moves, good moves and better moves. Strategy is critical, execution is paramount, and there are scoreboards. Name one competitive sport where you play hoping to win and never glance at the scoreboard. Imagine a Super Bowl where the scoreboard stops working moments before kickoff and the announcer says, "Don't worry about it, no one looks at it anyway." Yet that is how most people play the game of business. No wonder 90 percent of all businesses fail and the vast majority of the rest struggle to survive. *Can you read your scoreboard? Who's winning?*

16. **It does no good to get the numbers and then not know**: what they mean; what needs to be measured; how to measure; which numbers are important; how to change the numbers; which levers to pull to improve your results. Mastering the numbers is a competitive advantage (and can be learned in as little as two days).

17. **Controlling expenses is always the low-hanging fruit** for driving and maximizing the profits and cash flow of any business. *Do you know what to do, and where to look, to increase your profits by 50% in the next 90 days?*

18. **A single column of numbers on an income statement is meaningless.** Business and financial intelligence starts with multiple points of comparison over numerous time periods, and those comparisons MUST be on a single piece of paper or spreadsheet to be useful. This month versus last month is a pretty good comparison. Even better is this month versus last month versus the month before. Better still is this month versus last month and versus each of the **six** months before that. *How many points of comparison do you have?*

19. **Present your detailed income statement (for multiple time periods) in four formats**:
    1. Your currency.
    2. Averages of your prior annual results, so they are comparable with your current monthly results.
    3. "Common size," with the income statement expressed as a percentage of sales.
    4. Recast as "dollars" per unit sold or serviced.

    *How many of these tools are you using?*

### The blueprint and the cash levers

20. **The Ultimate Blueprint for an Insanely Successful Business**: **Assets → Sales → Profit → Cash Flow.**
    - *Effectively* convert assets (physical and/or people) into sales.
    - *Efficiently* convert sales into profits.
    - *Productively* convert profits into cash flow.

    A hint: not all cash is created equal. *Do you know what to measure to make sure you optimize your financial performance?*

21. **Overspending on assets is fatal.** The instant you make this mistake, you set yourself up for failure. You cannot create a sustainable business or optimize financial performance by "pimping" your assets. *How much pimping have you done?*

22. **If you are unhappy with your cash flow, you have a total of five levers to pull.** Your **income statement** drives the first two: (1) sell more; (2) spend less. Your **balance sheet** drives the last three: (3) reduce accounts receivable; (4) reduce inventory; (5) increase accounts payable. *Do you know exactly what to do to maximize your cash flow every month, every quarter, every year?*

### Keeping honest books

23. **Have one set of books that is an accurate reflection of the true business results**, without a bunch of personal "ya-ya" stuff padded in, and another set of books for the taxman. Minimize the commingling of personal expenses with your business's financial results during the year. *How can you measure and see the true financial progress of your business if a ton of personal expenses are buried in the numbers? You wouldn't fly an airplane with faulty dials, would you?*

24. **You have two options for your accounting method, and one is right and the other is dead wrong.** Cash accounting is a HUGE mistake (and sadly, the one most commonly used by small business owners). **Accrual accounting is the correct and only way to keep score** if you expect to measure your true progress.

### Closing principles

- If you are done wasting time and money lurching from shiny pennies to magic bullets to fast-buck schemes, commit to doing whatever it takes.
- As business owners we tend to be astonishingly optimistic in our thinking and planning. We rarely question our assumptions, and we rarely have experienced advisers watching our progress and giving us feedback. The result is the most fatal of mistakes: **running in the wrong direction enthusiastically**.
- **Invest in yourself**, which is the key to growth. **Learn the critical distinctions** so you can make better choices, which is the key to a better life. **Do whatever it takes to create excellence, mastery and contribution**, which are the keys to fulfillment.
