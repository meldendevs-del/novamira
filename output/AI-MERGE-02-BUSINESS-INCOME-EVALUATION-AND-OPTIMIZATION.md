## 1. The Financial Cockpit: Why You Need Dials

### The cockpit metaphor

**What it is**: Running a business is flying a plane. The owner, the leadership team, management and the founder are all somewhere in the cockpit. The job is to decide which dials the cockpit needs, what standards each dial should hold, and where there are blinking yellow or red lights that have to be addressed to improve financial performance.

**Why it matters**: If you have those optics, you have an opportunity to make more money. Without them there is only one lever: grow, grow, grow. "How do I get bigger?" is the most fun lever and there is nothing wrong with getting bigger, but the goal is getting rich, and that takes optics.

On a bumper sticker: **You can get lucky and get rich. You cannot get lucky and stay rich.** Plenty of people got lucky and made a tremendous amount of money but could not keep it. It is impossible to keep it without a cockpit, or without somebody who knows the cockpit flying your plane.

The failure mode is flying blind: "you're not gonna hit a mountain going a hundred and eighty miles an hour" is the whole point of building the cockpit. Flying a business on cash accounting, or with personal spending mixed into operating expenses, is exactly how owners hit that mountain.

### What gets measured gets managed

**What it is**: Any time metrics in a business are getting better, somebody is watching them.

**Why it works**: Entropy, decay and erosion are what naturally happen when nobody is paying attention. If nobody watches a number, it gets worse. If people start focusing on it, then what gets measured is what gets managed, and what gets measured is what gets done. So when a number improves, you can be fairly sure it is because somebody is paying attention to it; when nobody is, you can be fairly sure it will deteriorate. (Receivable days are the classic example: "Receivable days get worse when nobody pays attention.")

### The three-part process: have the dials, read the dials, act on the dials

**What it is**: Three separate capabilities, and failing at any one of them grounds the plane.

1. You cannot fly a plane without dials in the cockpit.
2. You cannot fly a plane if you cannot read the dials in the cockpit.
3. You cannot fly a plane if you have dials, read the dials, and then do not do anything about it.

**Where the dials come from**: The majority of the financial dials come from three things your accounting already produces: a balance sheet, an income statement and a statement of cash flow. They can be "tricked out" with an income statement in percentages (common size) and, further, an income statement per unit.

**Diagnosing your own gap**: The problem may be that you need more robust dials, or that you need to develop the muscles to read them. It may be the timing of when the dials are delivered. Or you may have the dials and not know what to do with them, which usually means you do not yet know how to read them. Take thirty seconds after learning this to write down what you will actually do with it; otherwise it becomes "shelf help."

### One column of numbers tells you nothing

**What it is**: A single income statement, or even several income statements with one balance sheet, cannot tell you how a business is doing.

**Why it works**: It is like going to the doctor and asking "How am I doing?" The doctor looks at you and says, "Well, you look okay. I don't know what your cholesterol is. I don't know what your blood pressure is. I don't know what your resting heart rate is. I don't know if you've got a tumor or not, but you look okay." One column of numbers is one little dial. Three years of income statements with one balance sheet still hides the big-time stuff that happens on a balance sheet, especially the three items that drive operating cash flow (receivables, inventory and payables), plus whether the business is buying property, plant and equipment or borrowing money.

The value comes when multiple time periods sit side by side on the same piece of paper, for both the balance sheet and the income statement. With balance sheets and income statements side by side, you can tell on the back of a napkin how a business is doing. If you want an MRI, add the statement of cash flow so you can see how the business converts assets into sales, sales into profits, profits into operating cash, and whether operating cash covers investing cash needs.

### How to lay out a management report

**What it is**: A single page, twelve and up to fifteen columns wide, that lets you compare the right things with each other.

**How to do it** (income statement example, written in August 2021):

1. **Annual averages**: the average month for 2018, for 2019 and for 2020, plus the average month so far for 2021. This shows how the average performance this year compares with prior years.
2. **Quarters**: the last four or five quarters (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4 of last year, then Q1 of this year). This lets you compare Q1 of this year with Q1 of last year and see whether there is any cyclicality.
3. **Months and year to date**: each month of this year (January, February, March and so on through August), then a year-to-date column.
4. Draw a line between each group of columns so you do not get confused and compare the wrong things.
5. Read across: average this year vs last year, year to date vs year to date, Q1 vs Q1, and how the months are unwrapping. Am I getting better or worse?

**If the business is new**: If you started this year and have no annual history, do it month by month and put the months side by side. That still gives you financial optics.

**Tools**: QuickBooks, Xero and MYOB have different buttons for multi-period reports, but all of them can export to Excel, and in Excel you have full flexibility to group the data however you want.

### Trailing periods

When looking at revenue and profits, three views help: monthly (for example the trailing fifteen months), trailing three months (quarterly), and trailing twelve months (the long-term trend). If you are on cash accounting, rely on the long-term trend, because a trailing twelve-month view bakes out the monthly swings. Comparing the trailing twelve months this July with the trailing twelve months last July shows, for example, that revenue is up while profit dollars have dropped dramatically: a signal that something needs attention. In the same example, operating cash flow is positive but not as good as profits; the indirect method (the "cash flow story") shows how the business got from 15,000 of theoretical profits to only 6,415 of operating cash flow. Give someone income statements and balance sheets and you can give them optics plus cash flow, for any period: a single month, year to date, or trailing twelve months.

## 2. The Three Kinds of Cash and the Statement of Cash Flows

### Operating, investing and financing cash

**What it is**: Every dollar of cash that comes into or goes out of the business belongs to one of three kinds, and keeping them separate is one of the critical dials in the cockpit.

- **Operating cash (O cash, OCF)**: cash generated or used in the operations of the business.
- **Investing cash (I cash, ICF)**: cash used or generated when you buy or sell property, plant and equipment. That is all it is. Investing in the stock market does not count, and investing in accounts receivable or inventory does not count. Investing cash is only the business investing in property, plant and equipment. (The formal statements also carry a line for "other investments," such as investments in affiliates; see the Larva case.)
- **Financing cash (F cash)**: cash generated or used from banking or investor activities. Raising money from a partner is financing cash. Paying a dividend is financing cash. Borrowing from the bank is financing cash.

**Why it matters**: The amount of operating cash a business generates minus its investing cash needs gives you a clue as to how much has to be financed.

**Example**: A business generates $100 of operating cash flow and needs to buy $40 of equipment (a negative investing cash flow). $60 is left over. If instead the business generates $100 and needs to buy $130 of equipment, the remaining $30 it cannot pay for out of cash flow has to be sourced somewhere: from savings, or, if there is not enough cash in the bank, from financing. Looking at operating vs investing vs financing cash tells you how much financing the business needs to sustain itself and grow.

### Why interest is operating cash, not financing cash

**What it is**: Interest paid on debt shows up in operating cash flow, even though the debt itself is financing cash.

**Why it works**: View interest as rent. You are renting someone else's money, and rent is an operating expense, just like the rent you pay on your building. You might not have designed accounting that way, but since that is the format, always treat interest as an operating expense that affects operating cash flow.

### Capitalized spending is investing cash

**What it is**: When you spend money on something you capitalize (put on the balance sheet), the cash used is investing cash.

**Example: the roof**. The old roof on an investment property is worn out and is completely replaced for $100 (or $100,000). Cash goes down by $100. The roof is capitalized, so property, plant and equipment on the balance sheet goes up by $100. Because the spending bought property, plant and equipment, it is not operating cash; it is investing cash. It does not show up on the income statement (the profit and loss), because it was capitalized. It does show up on the statement of cash flow, in the investing cash line.

**One bucket of cash**: There is no separate bank account for operating money, investing money and financing money. There is one bucket of cash on the balance sheet. When you make or use cash, the statement of cash flow tracks where it came from and where it went, so you can understand what happened. In the roof example the complete sentence is: "My total cash went down by a hundred, but the cash that I used was I cash."

### The direct method

**What it is**: A statement of cash flow that starts with how much money was collected from customers and then lists what was paid out.

**How it works**: Collections from customers, minus payments to suppliers, minus payments for operating costs, minus interest, minus income taxes, equals cash flow from operations. Investing and financing transactions follow.

**Example: Campus Products, Statement of Cash Flows, year ended December 31, 2020 (direct method)**

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| **Operating transactions** | |
| Collections from customers | $15,000 |
| Payments to suppliers | (9,000) |
| Payments for operating costs | (17,000) |
| Payments for interest | (2,500) |
| Payments for income taxes | 0 |
| **Net cash flows from operations** | **($13,500)** |
| **Investing transactions** | |
| Sales of property, plant and equipment | 0 |
| Purchase of property, plant and equipment | (40,000) |
| **Net cash flows from investing** | **($40,000)** |
| **Financing transactions** | |
| Proceeds from sale of equity | 35,000 |
| Proceeds from bank financing | 30,000 |
| Debt repayment | (7,500) |
| **Net cash flows from financing activities** | **$57,500** |
| **Net change in cash (1/1 to 12/31)** | **$4,000** |

**Pitfall**: If you have not been getting a statement of cash flow from your accountant and wonder what your cash flow was last year, the year before or last month, reconstructing it with the direct method after the fact is almost impossible. That is what the indirect method is for.

### The indirect method

**What it is**: A way to calculate operating cash flow forensically, after the fact, starting from net income. It is the method accountants use more often than not when they produce statements of cash flow, so you need to recognize it and not get confused. The investing and financing sections of a statement of cash flows are unaffected by whether operating cash flow is calculated directly or indirectly.

**What you need**: Three pieces of paper: a beginning balance sheet, an income statement, and an ending balance sheet. The balance sheets are bookends and the income statement sits in the middle. If the income statement is for March 2021, the beginning balance sheet is dated February 28, 2021 (the end of February is the beginning of March) and the ending balance sheet is dated March 31, 2021. With those three pieces of paper and the ability to add and subtract, you can produce the statement of cash flow from operations.

**How to do it (the worksheet)**:

1. Start with net income.
2. Add depreciation (a non-cash expense).
3. Add the increase in accounts payable.
4. Add the increase in taxes payable.
5. Add the decrease in accounts receivable.
6. Add the decrease in inventory.
7. Subtract the increase in accounts receivable.
8. Subtract the increase in inventory.
9. Subtract the decrease in accounts payable.
10. Subtract the decrease in taxes payable.
11. Add the column: that is net cash flow from operations.

**The formal procedure** (for more complex statements):

1. Since the indirect method adjusts accrual-based earnings, start the statement with "bottom line" net income.
2. Move down the income statement and identify (a) every item that affected accrual earnings but had no effect on cash flows (for example depreciation and amortization expenses, write-offs for discontinued operations, the cumulative effect of accounting changes or adoption of a new accounting standard) and (b) every other income statement item not related to operations that did not have an equal cash flow effect (for example a gain from the sale of buildings). Adjust net income for these items, reversing their income effect.
3. Adjust for the period's changes in all non-cash current assets and current liabilities associated with operations (the current maturity of long-term debt, for example, is a non-operating item). Increases in current assets and decreases in current liabilities are decreases to cash; decreases in current assets and increases in current liabilities are increases to cash.

**Example: Campus Products, walked through line by line**

- Net income from the income statement: **+10,400**.
- Depreciation from the income statement: **+8,000**.
- Accounts payable went from 0 at the beginning of the year to 3,000 at the end: an increase, so **+3,000**.
- Taxes payable went from 0 to 3,000: an increase, so **+3,000**.
- Add the decrease in accounts receivable: receivables went from 0 to 34,000, so they did not decrease. Does not apply.
- Add the decrease in inventory: inventory went from 0 to 3,900, an increase. Does not apply.
- Subtract the increase in accounts receivable: **-34,000**.
- Subtract the increase in inventory: **-3,900**. (If you do not have inventory, you simply have a slightly simpler airplane to fly.)
- Subtract the decrease in accounts payable: payables went up. Does not apply.
- Subtract the decrease in taxes payable: taxes payable went up. Does not apply.
- Total: **-13,500**, exactly the same number the direct method produced from collections from customers.

**On depreciation vs accumulated depreciation**: The number you add back is the depreciation charged as an expense on the income statement for this period, not accumulated depreciation (all the depreciation since the assets were bought). Even if an asset was sold during the year, if it was held for seven months there will be seven months of depreciation on the income statement. Take the number from the income statement and add it back because it is a non-cash expense. Chasing the difference between depreciation expense and the change in accumulated depreciation on asset sales is "a rabbit hole that ain't gonna make you money."

**The logic in one line**: Net income is not cash; add back the non-cash expenses; then look at two things on the balance sheet, current assets and current liabilities, which are primarily receivables, inventory and payables.

### Why the indirect method is the more useful one

**Why it works**: Both methods give the same operating cash flow number. On the direct method you only see that some cash was collected from customers and some suppliers were paid; you cannot see the changes on the balance sheet, so you cannot see where the problem is. On the indirect method you see exactly what happened: in Campus Products, operating cash flow is -13,500, and you can see why. Accounts receivable went way up, which was bad for cash, and inventory went way up, which was bad for cash. The difference between profits and operating cash flow is only three things on the balance sheet: accounts receivable, inventory and accounts payable. If profits are 10,400 and operating cash flow is -13,500, the indirect method tells you what to fix. It is another tool in reverse engineering the business.

**Pitfalls**: You do not have to memorize the worksheet or grind over it. You only need to understand that there is a way to get to operating cash flow forensically, and that it shows you how receivables, payables and inventory are moving. Most indirect statements are used only for operating cash flow; doing all three kinds of cash this way is possible but more involved and not that useful for an owner.

### Good for cash, bad for cash

A quick reference used throughout this document:

- Accounts receivable going **up** is **bad** for cash (you sold but have not collected).
- Inventory going **up** is **bad** for cash (you bought stock you have not sold).
- Accounts payable going **up** is **good** for cash (you have kept the money instead of paying your bills). Paying your bills is bad for your cash.
- Buying property, plant and equipment is **bad** for cash (investing cash used).
- On an indirect statement, a **positive** number is good for cash and a **negative** number is bad for cash. A negative "change in accounts receivable" does not mean receivables are negative; it means receivables went up.

## 3. Cash vs Accrual Accounting and the Income Statement

### EBITDA: the operating profit dial

**What it is**: EBITDA stands for Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortization. It is revenue minus cost of goods sold minus operating expenses. Another word for EBITDA is operating profit (operating income): the profit of the operations of the company. Formally, it is net income with interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization added back. (Caution: operating income may be defined in other ways by other financial professionals, and it is often used as a synonym for EBITDA.)

**Why it matters**: A lot of businesses sell for a multiple of EBITDA; it is part of calculating the enterprise value of a business. Whether or not you are a seller, watch EBITDA carefully, because it measures how you are doing on revenue and how you are doing at controlling expenses. It is also used to compare profitability between businesses, because it removes the effects of financing and capitalization decisions. It is a significant health metric. It is **not** cash, but it is a very important metric to monitor.

### What an income statement looks like on cash accounting

**What it is**: Under accrual accounting, a sale is recorded when it happens, whether or not the cash has been collected, and the expenses of that sale are matched to the same period. Under cash accounting, revenue and expenses are not recognized until cash changes hands.

**Example: Campus Products, the same year on both bases**

| Line | Accrual | Cash |
|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | $49,000 | $15,000 (sales of 49,000 minus 34,000 still in receivables) |
| Cost of sales | (5,100) | (9,000) (inventory bought, not inventory used) |
| Gross profit | 43,900 | 6,000 |
| Operating expenses | (20,000) | (17,000) (20,000 incurred minus 3,000 still in payables) |
| EBITDA | 23,900 | (11,000) |
| Depreciation | (8,000) | none (depreciation is not cash) |
| EBIT | 15,900 | (11,000) |
| Interest (500 + 2,000) | (2,500) | (2,500) (both paid) |
| EBT | 13,400 | (13,500) |
| Taxes | (3,000) | 0 (none paid) |
| Net income | $10,400 | ($13,500) |

**Why it works**: The cash-basis "net income" of -13,500 is the operating cash flow number. If you are on cash accounting, you have never seen an income statement. What you are seeing is a statement of cash flow. Cash accounting tracks the cash you received and spent, not your true revenue.

Which column is a more accurate reflection of profitability? Accrual, by far. It says: whether you collected the cash or not, you had 49,000 of sales, and the cash is tracked on a different piece of paper. On cash accounting, the business appears to have lost 13,500 in profits, which is not what happened. It lost 13,500 of **cash**. In profits it actually made 10,400. This is a critical distinction.

**The symptom**: You can usually tell someone is on cash accounting by looking at their EBITDA: one month they look like Bill Gates and the next month they look like they are going broke, popping around all over the place, because they are tracking cash, not profits.

**The danger**: On cash accounting, an owner looks at a bad month and says, "Holy cow, I'm in trouble. I need to grow this thing." Maybe they do, maybe they do not. The revenue they are looking at is only the cash received, not the sales made. In the Campus example they are in trouble on cash, but the profits are not the problem; the cash is. The indirect method shows where the cash went: receivables went up radically and inventory went up. Had they controlled receivables and inventory better, operating cash flow could have been +25,000 or +30,000 instead of -13,500, an entirely different number, just by managing the balance sheet, not expenses.

### Why many small businesses are on cash accounting

Many small businesses are on cash accounting because their accountant is a tax accountant: very good at preparing tax returns, not so good at giving you optics. Most accountants helping with the tax return are not the people who can support you with accrual accounting. You may want to keep them for taxes, but optics for the cockpit take a different set of muscles.

### Make the switch: accrual is mandatory

**What it is**: Mark this as a very important point, with two asterisks, underlined and highlighted: **it is mandatory that you get to accrual accounting.** There are one or two exceptions, not many. Without accrual accounting you will have difficulty getting the optics to fly your business.

**How to do it**:

1. Get with your accountant and sort out cash vs accrual.
2. Plan the transition for the beginning of the year.
3. Expect pushback: "Oh, this is so hard. It's gonna be so expensive." It is not. You are already tracking accounts receivable, accounts payable and inventory; they are just not on your balance sheet. Instead of doing all of that off the balance sheet, put it on the balance sheet.
4. Start talking about revenue as how much you sold, not how much cash you collected. That gives you a dial in the cockpit so you do not hit a mountain at a hundred and eighty miles an hour.

**Caveat: over a year, cash accounting smooths out**. Given a long enough period, the cash timing differences average out. Over a year you are probably looking at something reasonably close to accrual results. But if you are trying to fly the business month by month on cash accounting, you will have an incredibly hard time.

**Selling a business on cash accounting**: Would a buyer switch the accounting method to calculate a lower EBITDA and pay less? In practice a buyer looks at multiple historical years (for example 2017, 2018 and 2019), and over a year the cash-vs-accrual differences smooth out. It will not be exact, but it will be fairly close, and both buyer and seller can get fairly close by looking at several years of cash accounting.

**Scale matters**: A coaching program is sold through a publisher that does all the sales and collects the cash, including payment plans. A $5,000 product sold on a financing plan collects $2,500 up front; the author is paid only on cash collected, twice a month, and receives a 1099 at year end. Moving to accrual would give a better feel for what is owed and what future revenue might look like, which is exactly the optics wanted. That can be done on an Excel sheet or with help from accounting, depending on size: total collections of $30,000 a year are very different from $3 million a year. You do not need a sledgehammer to tap in a nail. In general, though, you are better off knowing how much money is owed to you and keeping track of it on the balance sheet.

### Bad debt under accrual accounting

**What it is**: Under accrual accounting you count a sale as revenue and profit when it happens. If the customer later does not pay, the uncollected amount becomes a loss.

**Example: the egg business, drawn as a box**. A woman buys $3 of eggs and does not pay yet. The cost of goods sold on those eggs is $1.50, so the profit is $1.50 (a fifty percent profit).

- At the sale: inventory goes down $1.50, accounts receivable goes up $3, profits go up $1.50. The left side of the box goes up $1.50 and the right side goes up $1.50.
- She moves away and never pays. The $3 you thought you would get, you will not get. Reduce accounts receivable by $3; that is bad debt, and earnings go down by $3. Left side down $3, right side down $3. The box stays in balance.
- You never got the cash at any point. You got $3 of receivable, used $1.50 of inventory, and made $1.50 of profit, which then became a loss when the receivable was written off.

### Selling before you have the inventory

**Example**: You sell $3 of eggs you do not have yet, and the customer pays. Cash goes up $3; there are no expenses yet because there are no eggs, so profits are $3. Then you buy $1 of eggs: cash goes down $1 and inventory goes up $1. You deliver them: inventory goes down $1 and earnings go down $1. Add up the whole left side: +$2. Add up the whole right side: +$2.

### Key takeaways

1. There are two ways to calculate operating cash: the direct method and the indirect method. The indirect method is more useful because it shows what happened to cash on the balance sheet. The difference between profits and operating cash flow is only three things on the balance sheet: accounts receivable, inventory and accounts payable.
2. Cash accounting does not give you an income statement; it gives you a statement of cash flow. The statement of cash flow matters, but so does the income statement, and under cash accounting you do not have one because you are not tracking true revenue.
3. The mechanics are the base. Do not grind on them; let them soak. The point is not accounting; the point is getting rich.

## 4. Free Cash Flow: Baby Eats First

### The definition

**What it is**: Operating cash flow minus investing cash needs equals free cash flow. Formally, free cash flow is operating cash flow minus fixed asset investment: the cash the business generates from operations minus the cash spent on the business's fixed assets (capital expenditures). Free cash flow is "the mother lode of the mother lode." Operating cash flow is really important, but what you want is enough operating cash flow that the baby (the business) can eat, and anything left over after the baby eats is free cash flow.

**Why it works**: Ideally free cash flow is positive, because then the business self-funds without third-party capital and has generated excess cash that can be reinvested, used to repay debt, or distributed to the shareholders (the owners) through dividends.

**Example: the taxicab**. You own a taxicab. Every year it makes $100,000 of operating cash flow; assume profits and EBITDA are the same $100,000. Every year the cab wears out and somebody has to buy a new one for $40,000. That is investing cash used. $100,000 of operating cash minus $40,000 of investing cash leaves **$60,000 of free cash flow**.

### The loop: assets, sales, profits, cash

**What it is**: The reason you want things and stuff (assets) is to produce sales. The reason you want sales is to produce profits. The reason you want profits is to generate operating cash. The reason you want operating cash is to reinvest back into the business. Anything left over after the business eats is free cash flow.

**How it works as a cycle**:

1. Minimize the assets it takes to produce revenue. "Assets" can mean dollars on a balance sheet, number of employees, or payroll dollars.
2. Convert revenue (sales) into profits.
3. Convert profits into operating cash flow.
4. If the baby needs to eat, invest in capital expenditures (investing cash needs), which grows the assets.
5. The assets grow the sales, which, hopefully, grow the profits and the operating cash flow.
6. Whatever is left over after the baby eats is free cash flow, used to repay debt and to ship to the owner.

A business with a free cash flow surplus can pay for its own growth and have something left over for you: a self-sustaining business. A business is not self-sustaining if operating cash flow is negative or does not cover its investing needs. If investing needs are greater than the operating cash flow generated, the business has to borrow money.

Note that "investing cash" here really means **growth cash**. Not every business is capital intensive. Sometimes growth is not a new piece of machinery; it is three new salespeople, a rebuilt website, an SEO hire or a digital media strategy. The question is: how much cash does the business need to generate to cover its growth needs, whether that means growing assets, number of employees or payroll, so the loop keeps turning?

### Baby eats first, mama eats last

**What it is**: A different way of thinking about paying yourself.

**Why it works**: *The Richest Man in Babylon* and "pay yourself first" are a terrific idea if you have a job. But if you are the parent and the business is the child, no parent eats before their kids, ever. Parents make sure the kids eat first. Most owners have subscribed to the theory that they eat whenever they want, so they end up eating right in the middle of the loop, "little piggies at the trough before baby eats." Operating cash flow is what feeds the baby's growth; only what is left over (free cash flow) is used to pay off debt and distribute to owners.

Warren Buffett is "a freak about free cash flow." He likes profits and he likes operating cash, but he understands that as the investor he does not get paid until the baby eats. He is the last person in line. As owners, that is the way it is: employees get to eat first; management teams, leadership and everybody else eat before the owner. Many owners have had the experience of a cash shortage in which the employees got paid and the owner did not. Baby eats first. Mama eats last.

### What happens when free cash flow is negative

If free cash flow is negative, the business has six options: delay paying bills, consume savings, sell assets, borrow money, raise equity, or go broke. You can have positive operating cash flow and still be unable to pay for investment and growth needs, at which point you dip into savings, borrow, raise equity, or do not grow.

### Financing capital expenditures does not create free cash flow

**Example**: In the taxicab example, what if you finance the $40,000 vehicle instead of paying cash? On the statement of cash flow, operating cash is +$100,000. Buying the vehicle is -$40,000 of investing cash. Financing the whole vehicle is +$40,000 of financing cash. On day one the two offset, and you still have $100,000 of cash. But the vehicle has to be replaced every year, so the loan has to be repaid during that first year, and as you pay it back (about $3,000 a month) your cash goes down. When all the dust settles, free cash flow is still $60,000. Spreading the repayment out changes the math a little, but this is the way to think about it.

**Pitfall**: In a capital-intensive business, financing part of the capital expenditure is possible and will temporarily improve free cash flow. The money is still being spent on capex; the only question is whether you are spending operating money or finance money. The debt must be repaid over time, so financing only moves the problem.

### Comparing two companies: asset-heavy vs asset-light

**Example**:

| | Company A | Company B |
|---|---|---|
| Assets | $10 million | $100,000 |
| Sales | $5 million | $5 million |
| Profits | $2 million | $2 million |
| Return on assets | 20% (20 cents of profit per dollar of assets) | $20 of profit per dollar of assets |
| Employees (alternative "assets") | 1,000 | 10 |
| Profit per employee | $2,000 | $200,000 |
| Operating cash flow | $100,000 | $2.1 million |
| Investment needs | $1.5 million | $1.5 million |
| Free cash flow | **-$1.4 million** | **+$600,000** |

**Why it works**: Asked which company they would rather own, owners pick Company B. The one person who raises a hand for Company A is a banker, because bankers love all that collateral to lend against, "which is why bankers are poor." Company B is an asset-light business: it does not take many assets to produce $5 million of revenue and $2 million of profit. So, as owners (not bankers), the job is to figure out how to reduce the amount of assets it takes to produce a dollar of revenue and a dollar of profit, whether assets means balance sheet dollars, employees or payroll.

**Employees as assets**: Nobody is sitting around thinking, "My biggest problem is I just don't have enough employees." A mentor said fifty years ago: "Business would be a lot of fun if there was no such thing as employees or customers," and that has gotten even more true recently. Yet employees are the source of 100% of your leverage: all of it comes from who is on your team. With wonky people on the team, growth will not be what you want. A major growth pillar is the leverage you get from the team you create and the structure you put them in. An owner (unlike an operator) is focused on leverage and structure; opportunity without structure is chaos and drama. Most entrepreneurs started their business because they hate structure, so on a bumper sticker: **The price of entrepreneurial success is structure.** Without it you have chaos. You need employees, just good ones. Anybody who says customers are number one has lost their mind, at least if you want to grow: employees are number one. Take care of your employees and they will take care of your customers. The same logic governs growth itself: search engine optimization, Facebook, Google Ads, salespeople, advertising copy and websites are tactics, and they matter, but structure and leverage come first.

**How can Company B have more operating cash flow than profits?** It did a good job of managing three things on the balance sheet: receivables went down a little, inventory went down a little, and payables went up a little. That is what you want to see in your business: somebody driving revenue, somebody managing expenses to increase profits, and somebody managing the balance sheet so you end up with operating cash flow.

**Only two of these numbers are on the income statement**: sales and profits. Every other number in the comparison (assets, employees, operating cash flow, investment needs, free cash flow) comes from the balance sheet or the statement of cash flow. The income statement is important, especially on accrual accounting, but to understand the health of a business you have to look deeper than revenue and profit.

### The four tests of operating cash flow

**What it is**: Four questions to ask of operating cash flow, in order.

1. **Is it positive?** The basic test.
2. **Is it greater than profits?** In an ideal world, yes. The one thing on an income statement that under no circumstances can be cash is depreciation. So if accounts receivable, accounts payable and inventory all stay exactly the same from the beginning to the end of the year (a flat balance sheet), operating cash flow will exceed profits by the amount of depreciation.
3. **Is it growing faster than profits?** Ideally, if profits grow 20%, operating cash flow grows 21%.
4. **Is it greater than growth (investing) needs?** That is positive free cash flow.

**Example for test 2**: Profits are 9 and depreciation is 10. With a flat balance sheet, operating cash flow is 9 + 10 = **19**. Now suppose receivables increase by 20 (bad for cash), payables increase by 20 (good for cash) and inventory increases by 20 (bad for cash). The payables and receivables offset, leaving a net balance sheet effect of **-20**. The income statement was good for cash by 19; the balance sheet was bad for operating cash by 20; operating cash flow is **-1**.

**Example for test 3: cascading cash flow**. Cascade it the right direction:

| | Assets | Sales | Profits | Operating cash flow |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Company A | +40% | +20% | +5% | +1% |
| Company B | +5% | +20% | +30% | +32% |

In Company B, a minimal amount of incremental assets gets a disproportionate amount of revenue, and that incremental revenue gets a disproportionate amount of incremental profit and cash.

### The four tests as a quick checklist

- Operating cash flow positive.
- Operating cash flow greater than profits.
- Operating cash flow growing faster than profits (ideally OCF growth > profit growth > sales growth > asset growth).
- Operating cash flow greater than investing needs (positive free cash flow).

## 5. Above the Line and Below the Line: Separating the Ya-Ya

### What the ya-ya is

**What it is**: The personal spending an owner runs through the business, usually for tax purposes. Examples: the business pays for your car; your children are on the payroll, do no work, but are in a different tax bracket, which reduces the business's profits; the business pays for the pool man and the landscaping; you take a trip to New Zealand and call it "searching for new markets for my business"; you buy your spouse a watch and say they were employee of the month.

**Why it matters**: You may want to do some of this for tax purposes, but it destroys your optics on how the business is doing. If the business is going to do a bunch of things for you personally, make sure that spending is segregated and you know how much it is. Most owners use the business as a little piggy bank: they want something, they have the business pay for it, with no budget and no set amount the business pays them each month. It is hard to get optics that way, and a little structure makes it much easier.

### Above the line and below the line

**What it is**: Not an accounting concept, but a practical one. Above the line you see revenue minus expenses equals EBITDA **before** the ya-ya: what the business actually does. Below EBITDA you subtract the ya-ya, and what is left is the amount you tell the government you made for tax purposes.

**Example**: EBITDA is 100. There is 60 of ya-ya. You tell the government you made 40, but in reality the business made 100, and you need to see that 100.

**Example: Omar**. An owner came into a board meeting with $3 million of revenue and -$100,000 of profit, and said, "I need to grow my business." Asked whether that loss was after his personal spending, he said, "Oh yeah, my company pays for everything." In ten or fifteen minutes the expenses were identified: the business was actually making **$900,000 a year**, and Omar was spending **$1 million a year** on himself, so it looked like a $100,000 loss. The problem was not the business. The problem was how much he was loading on his fork.

**Example from a dashboard**: With all owner's expenses above the line, a business with $3.6 million of revenue year to date appears to be making no money ("I'm in the process of going broke" is what you would tell the government). Move the owner's expenses below the line and the business actually has **$262,000 of profit** so far this year.

### How to set it up in your books

**What it is**: This is not really two sets of books. It is one set of books with the expenses categorized properly, in accrual, so you have optics.

**How to do it**:

1. In your chart of accounts, split each affected account into an A and a B version: salaries A and salaries B, travel A and travel B, venues A and venues B, and so on.
2. A is where you record what actually happened in the business. B is, bluntly, the non-business stuff.
3. Example: salaries total 120, of which 100 is actual (A) and 20 is ya-ya (B). You have now identified how much is non-business related. Do this all the way through the chart of accounts.
4. In QuickBooks, MYOB, Xero and similar systems, you can push a button to move all the B expenses below the line.
5. For the tax return, revenue minus all expenses (A and B) is the profit you report. For analysis, move the B expenses below the line and look at EBITDA before the ya-ya.

**The point**: The instruction is not "don't do it," because telling owners not to do it does not work. The instruction is: if you decide to eat early, keep an A/B format so you can see whether the business can really cover its growth capital before you eat. Then you can tell the government whatever you want to tell them, but at least you will not hit a mountain going a hundred and eighty miles an hour.

## 6. Effective, Efficient, Productive: The Ultimate Blueprint

### Three special relationships

**What it is**: Three ratios link the four stages of the loop (assets, sales, profits, operating cash flow). Each measures a different management skill.

- **Effectiveness = Sales ÷ Assets.** How many dollars of assets does it take to produce a dollar of revenue? How many employees? How many payroll dollars? This measures how effective your assets are at producing sales.
- **Efficiency = Profits ÷ Sales.** How many dollars of sales does it take to produce a dollar of profit? This measures how efficiently you control the expenses of the business to maximize profit from the revenue you receive.
- **Productivity = Operating Cash Flow ÷ Profits.** How productive are your profits at generating cash?

**Why it matters**: The goal is to keep improving the effectiveness, efficiency and productivity of your assets, sales, profits and operating cash flow. It is a never-ending wheel. The bigger you get, the more operating cash flow you hopefully have to reinvest; and if the business suddenly needs less reinvestment for growth, more free cash flow is left to reduce debt or pay the owner.

### The algebra: two minor dials make one major dial

**What it is**: Multiply the relationships together and the middle terms cancel, like high school algebra.

1. (Sales ÷ Assets) × (Profits ÷ Sales) = Profits ÷ Assets = **Return on Assets (ROA)**. The two "sales" cancel. So return on assets is simply how effective you are at converting assets into sales, times how efficiently you convert sales into profits.
2. (Sales ÷ Assets) × (Profits ÷ Sales) × (OCF ÷ Profits) = **OCF ÷ Assets**. The two "profits" cancel too. What is left is how you are doing at converting assets into operating cash.

**Why it matters**: Return on assets is one of the major dials in every investor and financial analyst's cockpit, and it is made of two minor dials (effectiveness and efficiency). Ultimately, since you cannot spend your profits, you need to watch how you are converting assets into operating cash flow. And "assets" can be balance sheet dollars, number of employees or payroll dollars.

**Questions to ask about your own business**: Is it an asset-light business? Should you be measuring assets in balance sheet dollars or in payroll dollars? Can you use the indirect method to calculate cash flow, so you can look at the balance sheet and see what is going on with assets, employees or payroll? You can look at the income statement for sales and profits, use the indirect method to get operating cash flow, look at the balance sheet to see what growth capital you need, and use above-the-line and below-the-line accounting to see how the business is doing before you eat. None of this is possible on cash accounting, or with the ya-ya mixed into operating expenses.

### The Key to the Ultimate Blueprint

**What it is**: A single page that maps each ratio to the strategies that improve it and the KPIs that measure it.

**Sales ÷ Assets × Profits ÷ Sales × OCF ÷ Profits = OCF ÷ Assets**

| | Sales ÷ Assets | Profits ÷ Sales | OCF ÷ Profits | OCF ÷ Assets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary strategy** | ↓ Inventory; ↓ Accounts receivable | ↓ Operating expenses; ↓ COGS | ↓ Inventory; ↓ Accounts receivable; ↑ Accounts payable; ↓ Expenses | ↓ Inventory; ↓ Accounts receivable; ↑ Accounts payable; ↓ Expenses |
| **Secondary strategy** | ↑ Sales; ↓ Fixed assets | ↑ Sales | ↑ Sales | ↑ Sales; ↓ Fixed assets |
| **Metrics (KPIs)** | ↓ Inventory days; ↓ Receivable days; ↓ Fixed assets | ↓ MSG&A ÷ Sales; ↓ COGS ÷ Sales | ↓ Inventory days; ↓ Receivable days; ↑ Payable days; ↓ MSG&A ÷ Sales; ↓ COGS ÷ Sales | ↓ Inventory days; ↓ Receivable days; ↑ Payable days; ↓ MSG&A ÷ Sales; ↓ COGS ÷ Sales; ↓ Fixed assets |

**How to read it**: Growing sales is the secondary strategy for every ratio. The primary strategies are all about the balance sheet (receivables, inventory, payables, fixed assets) and the expense lines (COGS and operating expenses). That is the whole argument of this material in one table: before you pull the growth lever, pull the balance sheet and expense levers.

### Key ratios: what to think about

A checklist for evaluating any set of financials:

- **Trend**: always look at several periods side by side.
- **Common size (%)**: COGS, gross profit, SG&A and profits as a percentage of sales, and their trend.
- **Operating cash flow**: positive; greater than profit; greater than investment needs (I cash); its percentage growth rate.
- **Free cash flow**.
- **S ÷ A = Effectiveness.**
- **P ÷ S = Efficiency.**
- **OCF ÷ P = Productive.**
- **P ÷ E = Return on equity.**
- **P ÷ A = Return on assets.**
- **Receivable days**: want them going down.
- **Inventory days**: want them going down.
- **Percentage growth rates**: ideally **OCF growth > Profit growth > Sales growth > Asset growth.**

### Setting targets for the ratios

**Should there be a target on the wall?** Measure effectiveness (assets producing revenue) and efficiency (sales converted into profit) every quarter, or every month, and every year. You want those relationships getting better and better, or at least not going backwards.

**Where to find benchmarks**:

- Most industries have an association that gathers data and publishes it, usually once a year.
- Most industries have at least one publicly held company. To go public it filed an S-1 with the SEC, and every quarter it files its financial statements. All of it is online at **sec.gov/edgar**. If someone in your industry has gone public, that site will cover you in industry information: that company's metrics, and comparisons to the industry on many of these same metrics.
- There is also plenty of other material online.

**Benchmark against the industry and against yourself.** Benchmarking against yourself is what really tells you whether you are doing okay.

**Example: when the industry benchmark is useless**. A car wash business (not a drive-through; seventy or eighty people at each location) could not usefully benchmark against its industry:

| | Industry | This business |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue per car | $14 | $67 |
| Profit per car | $2 | $20 |
| Payroll as % of revenue | about 32% | about 48% |

It was a different model, so comparing to the industry was not useful; it had to compare continually against itself.

## 7. Managing the Balance Sheet: Receivables, Inventory and Payables

### Somebody has to manage the balance sheet

**What it is**: Most owners have someone driving revenue and someone watching expenses. Very few have someone managing the balance sheet. But the difference between profits and operating cash flow is three balance sheet items: receivables, inventory and payables. A business with good profits and a neglected balance sheet borrows money to stay alive.

**Why it works**: Reducing receivables and inventory, and letting payables rise a little, turns profits into cash without selling one more unit. In the Campus Products example, better control of receivables and inventory could have turned -13,500 of operating cash flow into +25,000 or +30,000.

### Receivables: the balance between cash and revenue

**What it is**: One of the drivers of revenue for most businesses is the willingness to take an IOU. If you were strictly cash on delivery and had no receivables at all, revenue would likely suffer.

**How to think about it**: There is a balance. You do not want ninety-day receivables, because that costs too much money. You also do not necessarily want one-day receivables if demanding that hurts revenue. Do not destroy the business by getting overly aggressive on either receivables or payables.

### Payables: guardrails for stretching

**What it is**: The more accounts payable go up, the better for cash flow (the converse of receivables). Most owners could lengthen payables and increase operating cash.

**Why it happens**: Most businesses have a bookkeeper whose only job is to pay bills. They open the mail every day, see a new bill, and pay it. For most businesses, accounts payable are paid inside seven days. They could be paid in twenty-five days.

**Guardrails**:

1. Do not push payables to a hundred days. That makes no sense: suppliers will not want to work with you if you take forever to pay.
2. Always take an early-payment discount when a supplier offers one. It hurts operating cash flow, but "the amount it increases my profits are breathtaking."
3. Protect vendor relationships. You want to be able to call a vendor and ask for a favor, or push back when they did not do what you asked, and that is hard when you are not paying their bills. A good customer pays in about twenty-five days.
4. Ease payable days up a little; do not try to screw your vendors.

**Example: the one-day deal with professionals**. Lawyers' and accountants' biggest problem is collecting money. The frustration on the client side is calling them with something pressing and waiting two or three days for a call back. So the deal offered to every lawyer and accountant: "I'll pay your bill in one day, but you'll return my call in one day. And for every day that you delay returning my call, I delay sending you a check by a month." They become remarkably responsive. It does cost some operating cash flow, and that is a deliberate trade.

### When to fix the balance sheet vs the income statement

If profits are fine and cash is not, the problem is on the balance sheet. If profits or cash flow as a percentage of revenue start trending the wrong way, there are only a limited number of causes (see Section 15): more assets added without more profit or cash; expenses out of control; revenue falling; or the three balance sheet items (inventory, receivables, payables).

## 8. Plugging Profit Drains: The Low-Hanging Fruit

### The expense lever vs the growth lever

**What it is**: When asked to raise profits, most leaders immediately lurch for the growth lever. Cutting expenses often gets the same result faster.

**Example**: The goal is to increase profits by fifty percent. The business has revenue of 100, expenses of 80 and profits of 20.

- **Growth lever**: grow revenue 30%; expenses go up 25%; profits move from 20 to 30. That is a fifty percent increase.
- **Expense lever**: leave revenue at 100 and reduce expenses by ten to twelve percent, from 80 to 70. Profits go from 20 to 30. The same fifty percent increase.

**Why it works**: The growth lever is a multi-month initiative. The expense lever can work in ninety days. Given ninety days in charge of a typical income statement, an expert would expect to find five to seven percent of expenses to cut; maybe not all the way to ten percent, although in some businesses yes. Cutting five or six percent of expenses might move profitability not fifty percent but maybe twenty-five percent, in ninety days.

**The tighter the margin, the more powerful it is**: In a business with a 20% margin you can go produce $5 more of revenue to get $1 more of profit, or find a couple of dollars of expenses and move the needle. At a 10% profit margin, cutting just six percent of expenses increases profits by fifty percent. This strategy increases net income as quickly as possible and increases operating cash flow at the same time.

**The trap of growth**: Many owners have grown the business and then made less profit than before, working harder and making less, after all the "brain damage" of growing. To some extent (not totally), revenue is a form of business pornography: it gets you excited, but what you need is profit. That is the reason you have revenue, and what stands between revenue and profit is expenses. The goal is not how big the company can be; the goal is how rich you can be.

### Two reasons to spend money

**What it is**: Every expense should be an investment, and there are only two things worth investing in: **keeping the customers you have** and **getting new customers**.

**Why it works**: Investments earn returns, and you can measure the return on money spent keeping customers and on money spent getting new ones. Those are easily measurable. If it is just an expense, it has no shot at a return. Anything that is not one of those two becomes a jump ball.

**Keeping customers is a growth initiative**: One of the primary ways to grow is to keep the customers you already have. It is less fun than dreaming up a new marketing campaign, figuring out Facebook, hiring two new salespeople or a new brochure. Training employees so that they deliver the quality of service that supports customers' decisions to keep buying from you is harder, but it is growth.

### Want vs need

For every expense, ask: **do I need this, or do I want it?** Go through the chart of accounts line by line and look for costs that are not contributing to keeping customers or getting new ones. It is tedious, and working on growth is more fun, but that is where the money is.

**What this is not**: This is not about whacking people's salaries. Payroll is one of the biggest expenses for many businesses, but the goal is to find the spending that does not support either growth initiative.

**Go out to bid**: Not going out to bid on things like insurance is crazy. Businesses get sloppy with some expenses because they are so focused on revenue.

**Where the gold is**: Most owners look at the top line and the bottom line of the profit and loss statement and shrug at all the little lines in between. That is usually where a lot of the gold is.

### Sign every check for ninety days

**What it is**: A discipline that gets you religion on expenses faster than anything else.

**How to do it**:

1. For the next ninety days, suspend autopay and suspend electronic transfers.
2. Have your accounting team or bookkeeper prepare every check, already filled out.
3. Attach to each check the invoice for what it pays.
4. Sit with the checks and invoices and sign every one of them yourself.
5. For each one, ask: is this how I want to spend my money? Need or want? Keeping customers, getting customers, or neither?

**Why it works**: It connects you directly with the money being spent. Do this for ninety days and you will find five percent; that is a promise. The owner who teaches this has signed every check for every company he has owned over the last thirty years, including payroll checks, while also getting an income statement by the tenth of each month. The reason: it is the owner's money, not the staff's, and how much the owner has left is dictated by how much they choose to spend. Signing puts up guardrails so a bad spend is caught quickly.

**Caveat**: Not every business is the right size to do this. It is a tool, not a requirement. If you do it, "the juice will be worth the squeeze."

### The low-hanging fruit in one line

Want vs need, audit, bid, outsource, sign the checks: all of it pays enormous dividends.

### Excess capacity is a hidden profit drain

Excess capacity is a major cost for most businesses, and most owners do not pay attention to it. See the Theory of Constraints in Section 16. After the March 2020 lockdown, many business owners made more money after the first thirty, sixty or ninety days than before, not because revenue went up but because everybody "got religion" on expenses and drained the excess capacity. Everybody got skinny.

### Reading the balance sheets without a cash flow statement

**How to do it**: Go line by line through the three items that drive operating cash flow, then look at cash, debt and fixed assets.

1. **Accounts payable** (usually where people start): 0 → 30,400 → 51,000 → 53,000. Payables going up is good for cash: roughly +30,400, then about +21,000, then about +2,000.
2. **Accounts receivable**: 0 → 42,000 (bad for cash by 42,000) → 115,000 (bad by about 73,000, "a seventy-three thousand dollar hickey") → 135,000 (bad by 20,000). In 2019 the business made about 14,000 of profit, and this one line cost it 73,000.

**Does it have free cash flow in any year?** No. Operating cash flow is negative every year, so even without the vehicle there is no free cash flow. In the most recent year it lost money in operations and also bought a vehicle: negative operating cash flow plus negative investing cash flow means big-time negative free cash flow.

### Negative retained earnings

**What it is**: Retained earnings are the cumulative earnings the business has kept since the beginning of its life. They are not cash: earnings sit at the bottom of the balance sheet, and cash is at the top on the opposite side.

**Example**: If nothing was invested into a business, retained earnings are -10 and assets are 100, then total equity is -10 and liabilities must be 110. Liabilities exceed assets. The business has lost money over its life; it is still alive, but the only way it stays alive is by borrowing.

**In KTV**: Retained earnings were negative through 2019 (-22,800, then -8,000). In 2020 the business made a profit, retained it, and retained earnings finally turned positive (9,200). The shareholders invested 205,000 of common stock. Until the most recent year, total equity was less than the amount invested because of the cumulative retained losses. Total equity could only go negative if the negative retained earnings grew larger than the common stock.

## 10. Receivable Days and Inventory Days

### Why days matter more than dollars

**What it is**: If revenue is growing (KTV went from 600,000 to 900,000 to 1,125,000), you would expect receivables to grow, and you would expect inventory to grow to support the incremental revenue. So a rising dollar amount may or may not be a problem. What matters is **days**: how long it takes to collect, and how long inventory sits. If receivable days are going up, that is a big-time problem.

**Why it works**: The balance sheet alone says receivables went up 20,000 in 2020, which looks like a 20,000 problem. Measured in days, it is much bigger, because revenue increased so much at the same time. There is also a payable days calculation, which is a bit more complex (see the KPI reference).

**Pitfall**: Do not grind on these formulas or memorize them. Software will calculate them. Just know that if you ever want to calculate them by hand ("a little hand crank"), it is fast.

### Receivable days: three steps

**How to do it**:

1. **Average receivables** = (receivables on the beginning balance sheet + receivables on the ending balance sheet) ÷ 2.
2. **Receivable turnover** = sales ÷ average receivables. This is how many times per period the average receivable balance is collected.
3. **Receivable days** = days in the period ÷ receivable turnover. Equivalently: (average receivables × days in the period) ÷ sales.

**Example: KTV**

| | 2018 | 2019 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average receivables | ($0 + $42,000) ÷ 2 = $21,000 | ($42,000 + $115,000) ÷ 2 = $78,500 | ($115,000 + $135,000) ÷ 2 = $125,000 |
| Receivable turnover | $600,000 ÷ $21,000 = 28.6 | $900,000 ÷ $78,500 = 11.5 | $1,125,000 ÷ $125,000 = 9 |
| Receivable days | 365 ÷ 28.6 = 12.8 days | 365 ÷ 11.5 = 31.7 days | 365 ÷ 9 = 40.5 days |

Turnover went from 28 to 11 to 9, and the time it takes to collect went from about 12 days to 31.7 days to 40.5 days. That trend is really bad for cash. The slip from about 31 days to about 40 days is stated as a **$52,800 problem**: the business could have had $52,800 more operating cash if it had kept receivable days where they were the year before. (The source asks you to take that figure on trust and does not show the method; the simple calculation, 8.8 extra days × $1,125,000 ÷ 365, gives about $27,100.)

### Inventory days: three steps

**How to do it**:

1. **Average inventory** = (beginning inventory + ending inventory) ÷ 2. The beginning inventory for 2020 is the same number as the ending inventory for 2019: the ending balance sheet of one year is the beginning balance sheet of the next.
2. **Inventory turnover** = cost of goods sold ÷ average inventory. This is how many times the average inventory was sold during the period.
3. **Inventory days** = days in the period ÷ inventory turnover. Equivalently: (average inventory × days in the period) ÷ COGS.

**Example: KTV**

| | 2018 | 2019 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average inventory | ($55,000 + $110,000) ÷ 2 = $82,500 | ($110,000 + $148,000) ÷ 2 = $129,000 | ($148,000 + $188,000) ÷ 2 = $168,000 |
| Inventory turnover | $420,000 ÷ $82,500 = 5.1 | $624,000 ÷ $129,000 = 4.8 | $790,000 ÷ $168,000 = 4.7 |
| Inventory days | 365 ÷ 5.1 = 71.7 days | 365 ÷ 4.8 = 75.5 days | 365 ÷ 4.7 = 77.6 days |

Even though sales are growing, inventory days went from 71.7 to 75.5 to 77.6. That is bad for cash. The most recent slip is stated as a **$9,000 problem** (keeping inventory days at last year's level would have left $9,000 more cash), and getting back to about 71 days, where it was two years ago, would have put **another $12,000** in the owner's pocket: about a **$21,000** cash problem in total. (Here too the method is not shown; the simple days × daily COGS calculation gives smaller figures, about $4,500 and $8,200.)

### Note on the numbers

The worksheet figures above (12.8, 31.7 and 40.5 receivable days; 71.7, 75.5 and 77.6 inventory days) differ slightly from the Key Financial Data and Trends page in Section 12 (12.60, 31.40 and 40.00 receivable days; 70.71, 74.42 and 76.55 inventory days), and the projection footnotes use yet another set (40.56 or 40.6 receivable days; 77.62 or 77.6 inventory days). The source does not explain the differences; the trends and conclusions are the same whichever set you use.

### Days in the period

The formal KPI definitions use 360 days for a year and 30 days for a month; the worked examples use 365. Pick one and stay consistent.

## 11. Three Views of the Income Statement: Dollars, Common Size and Per Unit

### Why dollars alone hide things

**What it is**: An income statement in dollars (or whatever currency you use) hides movements you can only see in percentages or per unit. There are three formats: dollars, percentages (common size) and per unit.

**How to do it**: Put the dollar income statement and the percentage income statement on **different pieces of paper**. Having dollars and percentages on the same page does you no good. On separate pages you can compare dollars year over year, quarter over quarter and month over month, and percentages the same way. Being thoughtful about the two pages "turns on some lights."

### Common size

**What it is**: An income statement where revenue is 100% and every other line is a percentage of revenue.

**Example: KTV common size income statement**

| | 2018 | 2019 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 100.0% | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| Cost of goods sold | 70.0 | 69.3 | 70.2 |
| Gross profit | 30.0 | 30.7 | 29.8 |
| Marketing & selling expense | 5.0 | 5.3 | 5.6 |
| General & administrative expense | 26.0 | 21.3 | 20.0 |
| EBITDA | -1.0 | 4.0 | 4.2 |
| Depreciation | 2.0 | 1.3 | 1.3 |
| Amortization | 0.2 | 0.1 | 0.1 |
| EBIT | -3.2 | 2.6 | 2.8 |
| Interest | 0.6 | 0.9 | 1.2 |
| EBT | -3.8 | 1.7 | 1.6 |
| Taxes | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.1 |
| Net income | -3.8% | 1.7% | 1.5% |

Growth rates to calculate alongside it:

- Sales growth: N/A, 50% ($600,000 to $900,000), 25% ($900,000 to $1,125,000).
- Marketing and selling expense growth: N/A, 60% ($30,000 to $48,000), 30% ($48,000 to $62,500).
- G&A expense growth: N/A, 23% ($156,000 to $192,000), 17% ($192,000 to $225,000).

**What the common size statement reveals**:

1. **EBITDA is flat as a percentage** while revenue grew 50% and then 25%. Revenue is growing; EBITDA as a percentage of revenue is basically staying flat.
2. **Marketing and selling is rising as a percentage of revenue while the revenue growth rate is falling.** Marketing and selling expense growth (60%, then 30%) dwarfs revenue growth (50%, then 25%). You are spending more and more to get less and less.
3. **G&A is falling as a percentage** of revenue (26.0% to 21.3% to 20.0%), which looks good.
4. **COGS moved from 70% to 69.3% back to 70.2%.** It is easy to shrug at "pretty close."

### A small percentage of a big number is a big number

**What it is**: The discipline of converting small percentage slips back into dollars.

**Example: COGS**. The slip from 69.3% to 70.2% is 0.9 percentage points, not even one percent. It is easy to shrug. But 0.9% of what? Of $1,125,000: that is about **$10,000**. If COGS had stayed at last year's percentage of revenue (the same percentage, not the same dollar amount), net profit would have been about $10,000 higher, and profits were only about $17,000.

**Example: marketing and selling**. It rose 0.3 percentage points. 0.3% of $1,125,000 is close to **$4,000**.

With just those two lines, the business could have almost doubled net income without moving the needle on revenue, just by paying closer attention to the percentage spent on marketing and on cost of goods sold.

### The G&A trap and ZOG (zero overhead growth)

**What it is**: G&A as a percentage of revenue is declining, but the dollar amount is going up: 23% one year and 17% the next. That is a trap. You get lulled to sleep because the percentage is moving the right way.

**Why it matters**: A lot of G&A should not go up every year. Healthcare costs do; rent does not always; paper might go up a little, but nothing like that much. A big jump in G&A dollars deserves questions.

**ZOG (zero overhead growth)**: Start every budget with the question: can we have zero overhead growth? Rarely can you get all the way there. But start with the concept: if I am going to spend more on one thing, is there a way to balance the budget without needing more revenue? Is there a way to do a better job of controlling expenses? Holding G&A dollars flat can free money inside the budget, for example to accommodate raises for other people.

**The three KTV findings from common size**: cost of goods sold as a percentage of revenue is a bit of a problem; marketing and sales spend is rising faster than sales; and there may be an opportunity for zero overhead growth on the dollar amount of G&A.

### Per unit: the third income statement

**What it is**: An income statement expressed per unit of whatever drives your business.

**Example: Walmart**. Walmart's income statement comes in dollars, in percentages, and per square foot. They measure revenue per square foot, utilities per square foot, payroll expense per square foot. That is how Walmart knows whether to give Pepsi six more inches of shelf space or another shelf: does the change increase revenue and profit per square foot? Everything to them is about per square foot.

**Your unit**:

- A lawyer: per billable hour.
- A dentist: per patient.
- A Botox or cosmetic practice: revenue per patient, cost per patient, overhead per patient.
- A car wash: per car.
- An insurance agency: it may take thirty minutes of thinking to find the right unit, but it is worth it.

**Example: the car wash**. Instead of an income statement with $11 million of revenue, a pile of expenses and $3 million of profit, the per-unit view says: **$62.50 of revenue per car**. Chemicals are **$1.32 per car**, up from **$1.25** last year, a change you cannot see in dollars and percentages. Every time a car goes through: "dollar thirty-two, dollar thirty-two." Can we get it down to $1.30 and save two cents a car? Labor is all in about **$31 per car** and has gone up over time; is there a way to be more efficient? The cheapest wash is now about **$20**; the most expensive is **$1,800**, and three or four of those are sold a week. (As Warren Buffett said, the only reason to open the Wall Street Journal and read the stock prices every day is to see if somebody wants to do something stupid, and if they do, he wants to help them.)

**Why it works**: Per unit gets everything down into small increments. Suddenly you see that it is $11.40 per patient, and you ask how to get it to $12 per patient, $12 per car, $12 per billable hour. People who adopt this technique quickly become addicted to it. Make the note; this one "has got lots of juice."

**The CFO view**: Profit margin means an income statement in dollars, common size and per unit, all three.

## 12. Benchmarking Against Yourself and Projecting the Next Year

### Focus on the levers above EBITDA

**What it is**: The operating levers you can actually throw are mostly on the income statement from EBITDA up. Below EBITDA is a lot of "razzle dazzle" you cannot do much about: depreciation you cannot change; interest you might refinance, but you have already borrowed the money; taxes the government will collect no matter what. Watch net income, but put your effort above the line. ### If you've done it before, why can't you do it again?

**What it is**: The core question of benchmarking against yourself. If at one time you ran cost of goods sold at 69.3% (the transcript says 69.2%), what is preventing you from getting back there? If you once collected receivables in 31.7 days, how did it get to be 40 days? If inventory is up to 77 days but used to be 70, what is preventing you from getting it back to 70?

**Why it works**: Sometimes there is a really good answer, and sometimes there are just sorry excuses. Look for the good answers, not the sorry excuses. You are not claiming an industry standard; you are pointing at a level the business has already proven it can hit. The same logic applies anywhere: if receivable days used to be 35 and are now 40, how do you get back to 35? If inventory used to be 60 days and is now 70, how do you get back to 60? That gives you an outcome. The next question is whether you can put in place the critical drivers on the "yellow brick road" that gets you from where you are to where you want to be.

### Money left on the table

**What it is**: A monetary measure of how much cash and profit the business failed to realize because it did not operate at its own best-ever ("best in class") level in each category. It is a benchmark of you against you.

**Formal definitions**:

- **Best in Class**: the best "%" or "days" result in each key financial category across all prior periods of your business.
- **Efficiency Score**: the potential net income and operating cash the business failed to realize because expenses (in selected income statement categories) were not controlled to best-in-class levels.
- **Effectiveness Score**: the potential operating cash the business failed to realize because receivables, inventory and payables were not controlled to best-in-class levels. (Note the terminology: this "effectiveness" is about balance sheet items, while the effectiveness ratio in Section 6 is sales ÷ assets.)
- **Business Intelligence (BI) Score**: the total potential operating cash the business failed to realize because management did not control expenses, convert net income into operating cash flow, and control selected balance sheet categories to best-in-class levels.

**Examples from a dashboard**:

- Cost of goods sold is currently **57%** of revenue. The best ever was **50%**, in May 2020. Operating at 50% instead of 57% would have produced another **$231,000**.
- But the comparison period matters. If something changed dramatically after the lockdown, when people came back to work, compare only from September 2020 onward. Then the best ever is **52%**, not 50%.
- Receivable days year to date are **92**; the best ever is **60**. That gap is costing **$546,000** of cash. The trend line (monthly, trailing three months, trailing twelve months) shows receivable days at about **90** now vs **71** a year ago in July. That happened because somebody was not paying attention.
- Payable days: the best ever was **35**; now **19**. A year ago they were about 29 or 30; now 23. Do you want to tell the accounting team not to pay bills quite so fast?

**How to use it**: Look first where the most money is left on the table ("dig here"); you cannot dig everywhere, so dig where the juice is. Then look at the trend line for that metric. Also watch efficiency, effectiveness and productivity trends, operating cash flow per employee and per dollar of revenue, and profits per dollar of assets, per employee and per payroll dollar. A gradually deteriorating profit-as-a-percentage-of-revenue trend is a warning even when each month looks fine.

### Simulating before you pull levers

**What it is**: Flying the business in a simulator: compare trailing twelve months actual with projected twelve months, change one lever at a time, and see the effect on revenue, operating profit and operating cash flow.

**Example**:

1. **Grow 25% and do nothing else.** Revenue goes up $1.5 million and operating profit goes up about $290,000 (about $300,000), but operating cash flow goes **down $355,000**, from about +$260,000 next year to about -$90,000. The more you grow, the more cash you consume. So maybe do something besides growing first.
2. **COGS**: trailing twelve months 56%, best ever 52%. Getting all the way back may not be realistic; target 53%.
3. **Marketing**: 14% of revenue, best ever 10%. Target 10%.
4. **G&A**: 12% of revenue, best ever 9%. That is too far; target 11%, whacking out one point.
5. **Receivable days**: 91 on a trailing twelve-month basis, best ever 60. Target 60.
6. **Payable days**: almost 19. Stretch to 26, still paying bills within thirty days.
7. **Inventory days**: 128, best ever 79. Target 100: not all the way back, but pull the lever.

**Result without growth**: roughly a half million dollars of incremental profit and roughly $1.4 million of incremental operating cash flow.

**Then add the 25% growth**: revenue up $1.5 million, profits up $1 million, operating cash flow up $1.5 million.

**The lesson**: If you have an expectation of changing your results, you have to be able to measure them. Pull the efficiency and balance sheet levers first; then grow.

### How to project your sales increase

**How to do it**:

1. Start with sales. What is your specific sales and marketing plan?
2. What have sales been doing over the last couple of years (the trend)?
3. Who are your current customers? Do you expect them to buy more from you next year? Why?
4. Will the size of their orders or their frequency of purchase increase or decrease?
5. Who are potential new customers? How many of them can you "talk" to? How will you convince them to become customers?
6. Will you lose any customers this year? If so, why, and which ones?
7. Analyze your customers specifically: by product, location, price, size of order and so on, and figure out specifically what you will do differently next year to produce the results you want.
8. You cannot say you want a 20% increase in sales and expect that, by itself, to produce the result.
9. Determine a specific percentage increase in sales.

**Remember: hope is not a strategy for success.**

## 13. KPI Reference

This list is a resource, not a hammer you should necessarily pick up. You do not want to do all of these calculations; that would be crazy. But when you need payable days or inventory days, or when sales and earnings look wonky and you wonder what to measure, come back here. KPIs marked with an asterisk (*) are highlighted as key measures.

For the "days" KPIs, use 360 days for a year and 30 days for a month.

### Sales and earnings

| KPI | Calculation | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| Sales growth rate | (Current period's sales − prior period's sales) ÷ prior period's sales | Sales growth from one period to another, as a percentage |
| Earnings growth rate* | (Current period's net income − prior period's net income) ÷ prior period's net income | Earnings growth from one period to another, as a percentage |
| Return on sales (ROS)* | Net income ÷ sales | Efficiency in generating earnings from a given level of sales (also called net margin, net profit margin or profit margin) |

### Income statement expenses

| KPI | Calculation | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| COGS common sized | COGS ÷ sales | Management's ability to control inventory costs |
| M&S common sized | M&S ÷ sales | Ability to control marketing and sales expenses |
| G&A common sized | G&A ÷ sales | Ability to control general and administrative expenses |
| MSGA common sized | MSGA ÷ sales | Ability to control operating expenses |

### Assets (balance sheet, total payroll and number of employees)

| KPI | Calculation | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| Total ending asset growth rate | (Total ending assets − total beginning assets) ÷ total beginning assets | Asset growth from one period to another, as a percentage |
| Average assets | (Beginning assets + ending assets) ÷ 2 | Average assets for the period |
| Average net fixed assets | (Beginning net fixed assets + ending net fixed assets) ÷ 2 | Average net fixed assets for the period |
| Capital expenditures | (Ending net fixed assets + depreciation) − beginning net fixed assets | Amount spent to acquire tangible fixed assets (building, vehicles, computers, machinery) during the period |
| Asset turnover | Sales ÷ average assets | Efficiency of assets in producing sales (marketing effectiveness); for every dollar of assets, this many dollars of sales. Also called marketing leverage |
| Fixed asset turnover | Sales ÷ average net fixed assets | How effectively fixed assets produce sales |
| Payroll turnover | Sales ÷ total payroll | Efficiency of payroll in producing sales; for every dollar of payroll, this many dollars of sales |
| Turnover per employee | Sales ÷ number of employees | Efficiency of employees in producing sales |
| Return on assets (ROA)* | Net income ÷ average assets | Management's effectiveness and efficiency in using assets to produce earnings |
| Return on employees* | Net income ÷ number of employees | Efficiency of employees in producing earnings |
| Return on payroll* | Net income ÷ total payroll | Efficiency of payroll in producing earnings |

### Receivables, inventory and payables

| KPI | Calculation | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| Average receivables | (Beginning receivables + ending receivables) ÷ 2 | Average receivables for the period |
| Receivable turnover | Sales ÷ average receivables | Number of times per period the average receivable balance is collected |
| Receivable days | (Average receivables × days in period) ÷ sales | Number of days the average receivable balance is outstanding (also called days sales outstanding, DSO) |
| Average inventory | (Beginning inventory + ending inventory) ÷ 2 | Average inventory for the period |
| Inventory turnover | COGS ÷ average inventory | Number of times the average inventory was sold during the period |
| Inventory days | (Average inventory × days in period) ÷ COGS | Number of days the average inventory was on hand |
| Average payables | (Beginning payables + ending payables) ÷ 2 | Average payables for the period |
| Payable days | (Average payables ÷ (COGS + MSGA − payroll)) × days in period | Number of days the average payable balance is outstanding |

### Equity and financial ratios

| KPI | Calculation | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| Average equity | (Beginning equity + ending equity) ÷ 2 | Average equity for the period |
| Financial leverage | Average assets ÷ average equity | Relationship between equity and assets on the balance sheet (also called the assets-to-equity ratio or equity multiplier; higher means relatively more debt, which may be undesirable) |
| Return on equity (ROE)* | Net income ÷ average equity | Productivity of equity in producing earnings |
| Debt to equity | Total debt ÷ total equity | Mix of debt vs shareholders in financing the business (the glossary defines it as total liabilities ÷ total equity; a high ratio means heavy reliance on debt and more risk, and lenders watch it closely) |
| Quick ratio | (Cash + accounts receivable) ÷ (accounts payable + other current liabilities) | A conservative measure of the ability to cover current liabilities from cash and receivables only |
| Current ratio | Total current assets ÷ total current liabilities | Ability to cover all short-term obligations with short-term resources; especially important to banks as a measure of liquidity |

### Cash flow

| KPI | Calculation | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| Operating cash flow (OCF) | See the cash flow worksheets | One of the three key measures of overall performance; management's effectiveness and efficiency |
| Cash flow growth rate | (Ending OCF − beginning OCF) ÷ beginning OCF | Growth of cash flow from operations from one period to another, as a percentage |
| Free cash flow (FCF) | Operating cash flow − investment cash flow | Cash generated from operations minus cash spent on fixed assets. Ideally positive: the cash available to reinvest, repay debt or distribute to shareholders (the owners) |
| OCF value from sales | OCF ÷ sales | Effectiveness and efficiency at converting sales into cash flow |
| OCF value of earnings* | OCF ÷ net income | Effectiveness and efficiency at converting earnings into cash flow |
| OCF value of assets | OCF ÷ average assets | Effectiveness and efficiency in using assets to produce operating cash flow |
| OCF value from employees | OCF ÷ number of employees | Effectiveness and efficiency in using employees to produce operating cash flow |
| OCF value of payroll | OCF ÷ total payroll | Effectiveness and efficiency in using payroll to produce operating cash flow |

### Other ratios the market watches

From analyst data on public companies: gross margin percentage; operating margin; cash conversion cycle (built from receivable days, inventory days and payable days); operating cash flow growth year over year; free cash flow growth year over year; capital expenditures as a percentage of revenue; free cash flow as a percentage of revenue; free cash flow as a percentage of net income.

### Annualizing

To annualize, multiply a partial year's result by the number that turns it into a hypothetical twelve months: if a business earned 1,000 in one month, the annualized result is 12,000 for a year.

## 14. Key Strategies for Owners

A leader, management team, CEO or founder should be working six to eight key strategies:

1. **Plug profit drains (expenses).** Listed first because it is the easiest and generally the most productive. It can put serious money in your hip pocket and on your bottom line. (See Section 8.)
2. **Increase cash flow (balance sheet: receivables, inventory, payables).** There may be an easy fix in tightening up receivables and, if you carry inventory, tightening up inventory. Be more proactive about collecting receivables and deliberate about when you pay payables.
3. **Increase returns (profit and operating cash flow).** This follows from the first two: cut expenses and profits rise; reduce receivable days, reduce inventory days, or let payable days ease up a little (without trying to screw your vendors), and cash flow rises. Measure profitability, measure operating cash flow, and above all measure how you are converting profits into cash flow.
4. **Anticipate crisis (sales and cash).** Figure out what could disrupt revenue and what could disrupt operating cash flow.
5. **Spot danger signals (trends, market and environment).** See below.
6. **Optimize performance (measure results and activities).**
7. **Drive results (measure critical drivers).**

### What could disrupt the future stream of earnings?

**What it is**: Possibly the most important idea in this entire material. The key to anticipating crisis and spotting danger signals is asking: **what could disrupt the future stream of earnings?** Things that can disrupt the future stream of earnings are deadly. Identify them and try to mitigate the risk of them occurring.

**Risk comes in three flavors**:

1. **Probability**: the probability that the risk occurs.
2. **Cost**: the cost if it does occur.
3. **Manageability**: how manageable the risk is.

**How to use it**: Pay the most attention to high-probability, high-cost risks: for example, a risk with a sixty percent chance of happening that, if it happens, wipes out seventy percent of your revenue or fifty percent of your profits. Primary focus goes to anything that could disrupt revenue and anything that could disrupt cash and cash flow. Then look at the environment, the economy and the competition: competitive risk, trends, market risk, economic risk. Those dials are not on a balance sheet, an income statement or a statement of cash flow; they are a completely different set of dials, and you still have to monitor them.

**Example: interest rates**. A real estate business that needs interest rates to stay at their current level to make a profit has a problem if rates rise three percent, and there is a lot of pressure for rates to rise (this was the view in 2021).

**Example: $50 million of revenue I would not buy for a dollar**. A business with $50 million of revenue and $10 million of profit can still be unbuyable. If ninety percent of the revenue is with your brother, or with anybody, a buyer is unlikely to buy, because if that one customer leaves there is no business. If eighty percent of the supply chain is one supplier, a buyer is nervous: concentration risk. If one employee has the source code in their head, a buyer is nervous. Enterprise value does not just reside in revenue and profits; it depends on what could derail the business. Design the business from an owner's level around the question: what could disrupt the future stream of earnings, and what are the probability, cost and manageability of each threat?

### Optimize performance and drive results: critical drivers

**What it is**: Driving results and optimizing performance come down to your **consistency of execution on critical drivers**.

**Example: the bathroom scale**. If you step on the scale and do not like the number, the key is consistent execution on the diet and exercise regime. Diet and exercise are the critical drivers of weighing the right amount. If you measure your execution on those drivers, it is only a matter of time before you weigh the right amount, because you are doing the right things.

**In the business**: What is your consistency of execution on collecting receivables? On payroll dollars as a percentage of revenue? On causing revenue to happen, so you do not have to fret about whether to hire a full-time employee or a contractor? Consistency of execution on delivering the right amount of revenue is part of that whole problem.

**Why it works**: It is not about visualizing Oz. It is about building a yellow brick road from Munchkinville to Oz and measuring your progress along it. If visualizing Oz were the key to success, anybody with a dream would have it.

## 15. The CFO 60-Day Plan: The Big 6 Financial

**What it is**: If you (or someone) put on the chief financial officer hat for sixty days, this is where the work starts: gathering what is needed to build a cockpit. The workbook lists six items; the spoken version adds free cash flow as a separate step, giving seven.

### 1. Cash: balance, sources and burn

Understand the cash balances, the sources of cash and the burn rate (what the expenses are). "Sources" can mean two things: is the cash operating, investing or financing cash? And, within operations, where does it come from: selling a lot of books, selling a lot of software subscriptions, washing a lot of cars, running a lot of four-day courses?

### 2. Free cash flow

Understand how much operating cash the business generates and how hungry the baby is. Once you know both, you know how much is left over for the owner and for retiring debt.

### 3. Accounting: accrual, two sets and ya-ya

Get the accounting straightened out; this is critical. Whatever the size of the business, migrate toward accrual accounting. Break out the ya-ya: what you tell the government stays one view, and all of the ya-ya gets pushed below the line so you can see the business. It sounds like two sets of books, but it is really one set with the expenses categorized properly, in accrual, so you have optics. On cash accounting you do not get an income statement; you get a statement of cash flow.

### 4. Profits: two reasons and need vs want

Thinking about profits really means thinking about expenses. There are only two reasons to spend money (keeping the customers you have and getting new ones), and you want each spend to be an investment with a measurable return, not an expense. For everything else: do I need it or want it? Consider signing every check for the next ninety days. (See Section 8.)

### 5. Profit margin: dollars, common size and per unit

See an income statement in dollars, in common size and per unit. Even for a business like an insurance agency, some thinking time will reveal the right unit. Per unit is incredibly powerful. (See Section 11.)

### 6. Revenue: sources and trends

Most businesses have multiple sources of revenue: different products, different customers. You may have one customer that represents thirty percent of revenue, and that share may be declining over time, or rising. Look at your top ten customers and what the customer base provides in revenue. Look at each product or service and its trend line.

### 7. ROA: profit ÷ assets and OCF ÷ assets

Measure profit per dollar of assets and operating cash flow per dollar of assets, and watch the trends. If profits or cash flow as a percentage of revenue start moving the wrong way, there are only a limited number of causes:

1. You have added more assets and are not getting the profit and cash flow from them, so the ratio has gone wonky.
2. Expenses have gotten a little out of control.
3. Revenue has fallen, so profits have fallen, so operating cash flow has fallen.
4. One of the three balance sheet items that affect cash flow: inventory, receivables, payables.

## 16. The Big 6 Risks and the Theory of Constraints

Risks affect enterprise value. The CFO's sixty days include a second list of six: the risks.

### 1. Concentration

**What it is**: Concentration risk is another way of saying something could disrupt the future stream of earnings. It can be one customer representing forty, fifty or eighty percent of revenue; one supplier that is the source for most of what you sell; or a key employee whose departure would cause major disruption.

**How to assess it**: Attach a probability and a cost. If there is only a five percent likelihood and it would cost two dollars, that is not a big risk. If it would mean losing seventy percent of revenue and all of your profit, it deserves a whole different level of attention.

**When the owner is the concentration**: Sometimes the owner is the business. That does not make it a bad business; it is a different business model with a different risk profile. You can get filthy, stinking rich either way. Michael Dell has a very different model from Michael Jordan: Jordan had to go out and shoot the basket; Tiger Woods has to make the putt. If Tiger Woods gets into a car accident and breaks his leg, he is not making putts, so one of his risks is his health. For a speaker who is the business, taking care of their body and health is a business risk, because if they do not, the business comes to an end.

Do not let anyone tell you that you do not have a business until you can leave it for ninety days and come back to find it better than when you left. That is a stupid thing to say. If it were such a good idea, one of the ten wealthiest people on the planet would have tried it. Bill Gates and Jeff Bezos did leave, but for good, and they hired someone to replace them. If you decide you do not want to do your job anymore, that is fine: you hired you to do your job, so hire somebody who will. If the concentration risk is you, just pay special attention to it.

### 2. Sustainability: the future stream of earnings

**What it is**: Anything that could disrupt the future stream of earnings: concentration, rising interest rates, legislation, shifting markets.

**Examples**:

- Legislation has been pushed through Congress more than once to change how much real estate brokers can charge in commissions. If it passes, it radically changes the business model. You can still make money, but it requires a different model.
- Bricks-and-mortar retail is under stress as the model shifts to online. It can still work, but it is shifting.
- Live events: with twice as many people in the room as online, hybrid is here to stay, and that changes the model. A board of one hundred and fifty members that had not met in person for eighteen months kept holding meetings, but hybrid rather than in person.

Your business model is being stressed; you may just not know it yet.

### 3. Business model

Sometimes the environment changes and the model itself has to change. A disruption to the future stream of earnings can be a business model issue, a concentration issue, or an external one.

### 4. External: the gasoline

**What it is**: In a ten-story building there is probably a room somewhere that stores the fuel. If the building catches fire, it does not matter how good a firefighter you are: if the fire reaches the gasoline, it is game over.

**How to use it**: Most businesses have a game-over risk like that. There may be nothing you can do about it, but you want to know where it is so you can build a firewall around it, so that whatever happens, the fire does not reach that gasoline. Look at the business and ask: are there any bullets out there that, if one hits me, it is game over? Is there a way to minimize the chance of being hit? As W.C. Fields said: "I sure wish I knew where I was gonna die, and I would make sure I never went there."

### 5. Leverage (financial)

**What it is**: If profits or cash flow come under stress while debt stays the same, the ratio of debt to cash flow gets out of whack, and that can cause major risk.

**Example: the 1980s real estate portfolio**. A real estate portfolio built up in the 1980s was worth $100 million, carrying $300 million of personal liability debt. When the wheels came off, the debt stayed at $300 million and net worth went to negative $50 million. No cash flow, upside down, and four years of going to the office every day asking, "How do I get out of debt?" The lessons: be thoughtful about the amount of leverage, and about how many personal guarantees you sign, because personal guarantees can turn into real obligations like that. Keep enough firewall and enough dry powder to live through a hurricane.

**Why it works**: A house that can withstand a hurricane is indistinguishable from one that cannot, until after the hurricane. Only afterward do you know who built one that could.

**Example: Lee Iacocca and the first bailout**. In 1979 Lee Iacocca was fired as president of Ford Motor Company by Henry Ford II. Chrysler was in trouble and hired him as CEO. He found they were out of money and about to lay people off. Borrow from the banks? Nobody would lend. Sell more stock? Nobody would buy; they were that close to going out of business. So he flew to Washington, D.C., met twelve senators and congressmen, and asked the federal government to lend Chrysler a billion dollars. They said it had never happened in the history of the United States: the federal government had never bailed out a privately held company, and it was not going to happen now. Two weeks later he flew back to the same twelve people and said: you are going to write a check. Either you lend me a billion dollars and I try to save this thing, or we are forced into bankruptcy, 230,000 people will be unemployed, and their unemployment benefits will cost you two billion dollars in the first year. "The only question is, is it for a billion or two billion?" In 1979 the federal government bailed out a privately held company for the first time. In the thirty-five or forty years since, more privately held companies have been bailed out than you have fingers and toes. The dam broke.

**The lesson**: There will be future events that trigger another lockdown of some kind, in three, five or ten years. (This is a personal opinion and not political.) For many businesses the lockdown was brutal, and the only thing that saved them was the government pitching in with PPP, EIDL and other programs: "sugar daddy" money. There is no guarantee that happens again. What do you need to build in the way of dry powder? What machine do you need to build that can withstand a hurricane? Be thoughtful about how much debt you put on a business.

### 6. Excess capacity

**What it is**: Excess capacity is a major cost for most businesses, and most owners do not pay attention to it.

### The Theory of Constraints

**What it is**: In an assembly line, step A passes work to step B, step B passes it to step C, and after step C it can be sold. Suppose step A can do **10 units per hour**, step B **5 units per hour**, and step C **1,000 units per hour**. The line produces **5 units per hour**. Step B is the constraint; steps A and C are excess capacity.

**Why it matters**: Excess capacity is a cost the business is carrying. Without it, you could probably make a little extra money. This is where the full-time vs subcontractor question comes in: a full-time role may represent excess capacity with a cost attached. Excess capacity is both a cost and a risk if things get difficult.

**Example: the lockdown**. After the March 2020 lockdown began, many business owners made more money after the first thirty, sixty or ninety days than before, not because revenue went up, but because everybody got religion on expenses: "Holy cow, I better get real on my expenses." They battened down the hatches, drained all the excess capacity, and everybody got skinny. Then they were making more money than ever.

**Inadequate capacity is also a risk**: Too little capacity can cost you customers or employees. If step B is the constraint, you might run two shifts at step B, taking it from 5 to 10 units per hour so it is in sync with step A, while step C still has excess capacity. The opportunity runs both ways: increase capacity at the constraint, and question whether you need to carry the incremental cost of excess capacity everywhere else.

## 17. Reading the Market's Cockpit: The Apple Case

### You can read the Wall Street Journal now

**What it is**: The Wall Street Journal is written for professionals. Nobody there is trying to dumb it down: either you know what they are talking about or you are not their customer. With the concepts in this material, you can read it.

### The August 2012 article

**What it says, and how to read it**:

- "Apple manages its cash with great efficiency." There is no shortage of yardsticks for Apple's performance, but one few appreciate is its **judicious use of capital employed**. Capital employed means capital tied up in working capital (receivables and inventory) and capital employed in investing (property, plant and equipment).
- "To boost profits, most companies have to invest more capital." Yet Apple increased profits **84%** in the year ending September 2011 while capital employed **fell**.
- In 2011 profits were a record **$26 billion**, and operating cash flow was a record **$37 billion**. How can operating cash flow be greater than profits? Receivables, inventory and payables moved the right way.
- "Thanks to the efficiency with which Apple manages much of its balance sheet, the capital invested in its business is actually negative. In other words, Apple gets paid for its products faster than it pays to make them." Receivables are being hammered down; payables are probably "a little chubby"; inventory is well managed.
- Cash comes in before it goes out partly because Apple has incredible negotiating leverage with its suppliers. In fiscal 2011, on average, it did not pay suppliers for **83 days** after being invoiced. It gets to say to its vendors, in effect, "You want to do business with us? This is when we pay our bills."
- It collected on its own customer invoices in **18 days**.
- It kept just **four days of inventory**, versus an already impressive **ten days** in 2010, in a company doing around **$160 billion** a year in revenue. Going from ten days to four was not an accident: somebody in the cockpit was driving it hard, because they understood that managing the balance sheet is how a company becomes worth a lot of money.
- iPhones, iPads and Macs are made by someone else: contract manufacturers such as China's Foxconn make the huge fixed asset investments. Apple runs a much more asset-light business. Assets into sales, sales into profits, profits into operating cash flow: they figured out how to minimize the assets it takes to produce a dollar of revenue.
- That is great for investors: huge profits without big up-front investments in inventory, receivables and capital equipment.
- Return on equity for the twelve months through March 2012 was **47%**, up from an already impressive **39%** the year before. Anyone could have earned it just by owning the stock; it was not a private-equity secret.
- Apple would start paying a dividend and buying back stock, an estimated **$45 billion** of cash over three years, while accumulating cash quickly: **$44 billion** in the previous twelve months.

### What happened next

**Example**: Right after the article was written (Steve Jobs had been dead for about a year, so that was not the cause), the stock price started falling. Over the next nine months it lost **one third** of its value, about **$240 billion** at the time. A $240 billion spanking from Wall Street, for a company with all those good things going on. Why?

**The fundamentals** (Apple's fiscal year ends in September; data from 2003 through the trailing twelve months to September 2012):

- **Revenue** was still growing, maybe slowing a little. Not the problem.
- **Gross margin** had reached **43.9%** but on a trailing twelve-month basis was **39.5%**. The market said: shave four percentage points off gross profit and we will take $240 billion of your value. Forty percent is still good, and they had been at about 40% a couple of years earlier, but they had proven they could build it up, then let it slip.
- **Operating margin** had reached **35%**, but trailing twelve months it was about **30% or 31%**. Still good compared with 2010 and 2011, but they had proven they could hit 35% and let it slip. The market reads that as "if you're going to stop running the business, we'll whack a third of your market value."
- **Operating cash flow** was up to **$55 billion**, a little better than the prior year. **Capital expenditures** were tracked, and **free cash flow** was up a little.
- **Efficiency ratios** (days outstanding): receivable days were improving; inventory days got worse, from **3** to **4**; payable days fell from **74** to **56**, squeezing cash flow. The cash conversion cycle, Apple's famous negative investment in working capital, was not quite as good as it had been.
- **Key cash ratios** the market watches: operating cash flow growth year over year (the cascading idea); free cash flow growth year over year; capital expenditures as a percentage of revenue; free cash flow as a percentage of revenue; free cash flow as a percentage of net income.

None of these movements was catastrophic on its own, and this is not a claim that they alone caused the whole decline. But small deteriorations on dials the market watches had a significant negative impact: a third of Apple's value vaporized in nine months.

### The question for your business

If your company were publicly traded, would the price be going up or down? If someone were watching all these metrics, would they want to buy more because you are improving your balance sheet and your ratios? Or would your investors conclude there is nobody in the cockpit, or that whoever is there cannot read the dials, and eject before somebody hits a mountain?

You can now speak the language, and if you can speak the language, you can play the game. If you cannot speak the language, you cannot play the game. You know how to do accounting in your head by visualizing a box, you know the terminology needed to run a business (not to be an accountant), and you know the levers you can move.

## 18. Would You Buy This Company? The Larva Exercise

### The setup

**What it is**: A team exercise in analyzing a real company using only the numbers. Numbers are not the whole story: they say nothing about management or the industry, and there is no comparative industry data. But if you look only at the numbers, you can still decide whether you like what you see, and why.

**How it runs**:

1. Form teams of five or six. (Groups of two or three get overwhelmed by the number of calculations.) Introduce yourselves.
2. Divide the calculations so each person takes a section. Allow about twenty-five to thirty minutes for the math.
3. Spend the last ten or fifteen minutes (forty minutes total) putting everything on one piece of paper and looking at it together: what do we see, do we like it or not?
4. Answer one question: **do you want to own this company, and why?** The decision itself does not matter; the reasoning does.
5. You may view it as an investor who lets the current managers keep running it, or as the manager who will run it. Either way, you are looking at a financial cockpit: is this plane headed where I want to go? Is it about to hit a mountain or run out of fuel? Is it off course? Is there a reason I would not even want to get in the plane, much less sit in the cockpit?

- **Common size %**: COGS ÷ sales; GP ÷ sales; SG&A ÷ sales; profits (net income) ÷ sales.
- **OCF**: positive (Y/N); greater than profit (Y/N); greater than investment needs, I cash (Y/N). You can write the numbers instead of Y/N if you prefer.
- **% growth rate (year over year)**: assets; revenue; profits; OCF. For X1, only asset growth can be calculated, because a year-zero balance sheet is given but no year-zero income statement or cash flow.
- **Free cash flow (OCF − ICF)** in dollars.
- **S ÷ A = Effectiveness %** (sales ÷ total assets).
- **P ÷ S = Efficiency %** (profits ÷ sales).
- **OCF ÷ P = Productive %** (OCF ÷ profits).
- **P ÷ E = Return on equity %** (profits ÷ total equity).
- **P ÷ A = Return on assets %** (profits ÷ total assets, or physical assets if you prefer).
- **Receivable days** (want ↓) and **inventory days** (want ↓), using the three-step methods in Section 10.

### The debrief: what the class saw

**Things going the right way**:

- Operating cash flow exceeds investing needs, so free cash flow is positive, and it is getting bigger.
- Operating cash flow is greater than profits every year, and in the most recent year it grew faster than profits. Productivity is over 100% every year: the ability to convert profits into operating cash keeps getting better.
- Return on assets is improving. The most recent year is roughly 7%. This is a very capital-intensive business, investing a lot of money for a 7% return, which is "a little tight," but it is rising, not falling.
- Return on equity is about 34% in the most recent year and improving: every dollar of equity produces about thirty-four cents of profit. That is pretty good.
- Net profit as a percentage of revenue rises every year: profits are growing faster than revenue.
- Gross profit percentage improves every year: they are either controlling cost of goods sold well or raising prices to keep margins improving. It could be either.
- Receivable days and inventory days are roughly flat to slightly improving. But receivable days are big: somewhere around 89 to 100 days on accounts receivable alone. With leases receivable included, one team got 174, 174 and 172 days, "darn near six months to get your money." Another team calculated receivable days rising (87, 93, 101), which would be bad for cash and might make an investor hesitate; most of the room got flat or slightly improving, so a math error is possible. It is not about the math, though; it is about what the math tells you. Whether 100 days is good or bad depends on the industry, which you do not know.

**Things going the wrong way**:

- **Growth slowed sharply in one year.** Revenue and profits are still growing, but the growth rate slowed rapidly. That may indicate a cyclical business on the backside of a slope. Will growth keep slowing because of an economic force, a competitive force or the environment? Maybe the Chinese market did not like it as much as everybody thought. This one could be a showstopper.
- **Receivables and inventory are high** and may be deteriorating.
- **Margins are low.** Profitability as a percentage of revenue is really low. Is that this company's problem or the nature of the industry? This is clearly a tough business.
- **Short-term debt is rising.** Long-term debt is trending down in the most recent year, but short-term debt went down and then up. They may be managing long-term debt by borrowing short-term. Short-term debt plus the current portion of long-term debt rose substantially in the most recent year (on the statements, from $1,633 million to $2,436 million; the discussion put the figures at about $1.6 billion rising to about $2.9 billion). Pressure on current liabilities is going the wrong way.
- **Rising dividends.** Dividends grow every year as sales grow. Does that reflect a lack of confidence in their ability to redeploy capital at good rates, so they return it to shareholders? At the same time they spent $402 million buying back their own stock, which companies usually do when they think the stock is underpriced compared with other investment options. (Buybacks can have other reasons: pushing up the share price, or offsetting the dilution from stock options, which is why companies like Facebook, Amazon and Google buy back large amounts of stock every year.) Maybe they have cash and nowhere good to reinvest it.
- **Why buy back stock before paying off debt?** They are returning money to shareholders while debt, especially short-term debt, is at a higher level than ever. That gives pause: are they being thoughtful with their cash? A speed bump.
- **Retirement liabilities** are a "pretty chubby number," about $3.4 billion, coming down a little, but a lot of money owed to people who used to work there that has to be dealt with.
- **The big picture**: is this a well-managed company in a flat industry, or in an industry that may have peaked?

### The reveal

X3 was 1995, and the company was **Caterpillar**. In 1995 it was selling for about $64 a share. Twelve months later the stock was worth $95 a share, and eighteen months later $120: it doubled over the next two years. Nobody could have known that. Revealing the company or the year would have polluted the process, because the process is only about the cockpit.

If you passed on it, that is fine, as long as the decision was thoughtful, nuanced and well argued. You do not have to take every deal you see. Warren Buffett's point: the difference between baseball and investing is that in investing you can watch a thousand balls go over the plate and never be called out. When you see one you like, take a swing.

It is hard to find a company with this many things going the right way; most businesses do not have this many dials doing this well. And there were still a couple of "gotchas" (slowing sales, rising liabilities) that could reasonably lead to a pass.

### How to make the decision well

**Argue the other side.** Spend five minutes arguing against your decision. If you come out a little less confident, that is a good thing. **The quality of my decisions is directly proportional to the number of conflicting thoughts I can simultaneously entertain.**

**Why it works**: All my problems started out as a good idea. All the "dumb tax" gets paid by not questioning assumptions, by excessive optimism, and by ignoring risk. Think in second-order consequences. Ask what you are not seeing: you know what you are seeing, but what are you missing? Every deal has pluses and minuses. Once the train starts moving in one direction, people collect pluses (or minuses) and never step back to ask what the counterargument is.

**Look for showstoppers.** The cockpit is full of dials: some green, some yellow, some more green than others. What you are looking for is anything flashing red, anything that says you are out of fuel. Larva has some yellows.

**Put more people in the cockpit.** This is much easier with a group, even if nobody on the team is good at it. You can only have one person flying the plane, but it is not a bad idea to have a couple of extra people in the cockpit watching dials, giving input, questioning your assumptions and your excessive optimism. (This exercise has been run with second-year MBA students at Stanford, and a room of business owners working in teams did a better job.)

**Hit the speed bump.** Slowing down is good when making these decisions. "Whoa, not so fast." You do not have to go fast; you have to be thoughtful.

**Business is an intellectual sport.** The book is called *Think and Grow Rich*. It is not called "use your emotion, your gut and your glands and grow rich," or "dim the lights, hold hands, light incense, sing Kumbaya, and a sack full of money will fall on your head." There are times to use intuition, judgment and experience, but keep emotions on the sideline: money and emotions are a very combustible combination. The goal is to do fewer stupid things. Everybody makes mistakes, but at least do not do something outright stupid by ignoring a flashing light. Judge a decision by the work done to reach it, not by which way it went.

**The language is the skill.** Listen to the debrief: free cash flow, effectiveness, productivity, trends, receivable days and inventory days, return on assets, common sizing, short-term debt on the balance sheet. You can speak the language, so you can play the game.

## 19. Glossary

Terms and their most common synonyms.

**Accounts payable.** A current liability: what the business owes vendors and suppliers for goods or services received but not yet paid for.

**Accounts receivable.** A current asset: what customers owe the business for goods or services sold. An IOU from your customers to the business.

**Accrual.** The standard accounting practice for most businesses. Accrual accounting matches the revenue generated by a sale with the expenses or costs of providing that sale in the same period. If a sale happens in a given month, the sale and all related expenses are recorded on that month's income statement, whether or not the cash has been collected. It gives a more accurate picture of the business's financial position.

**Amortization.** A non-cash income statement line, similar to depreciation. Amortization deals only with intangible assets; depreciation deals only with tangible assets.

**Annualize.** Multiplying a partial year's results to produce a hypothetical twelve-month figure. If a business earned 1,000 in one month, the annualized result is 12,000.

**Asset turnover.** Sales ÷ average assets. A measure of effectiveness in generating sales from assets. Also known as marketing leverage.

**Assets.** One of the three major sections of the balance sheet: the things and stuff of the business that have current or future value, tangible or intangible. The most important are usually cash, accounts receivable, inventory and fixed assets.

**Assets, average.** (Assets at the beginning of the period + assets at the end) ÷ 2. Usually used instead of ending assets because it better reflects the period, though there is no rule against using ending assets.

**Assets, fixed.** Tangible, significant items of value used in operations, such as buildings, vehicles and machinery. Also known as property, plant and equipment (PP&E).

**Assets, growth rate.** The growth rate of assets over a period, as a percentage.

**Assets, intangible.** Assets that cannot be seen or touched: patents, brand names, intellectual property, goodwill.

**Assets, tangible.** Items such as buildings, machinery and property. Tangible assets do not include cash, receivables, inventory, goodwill or other assets.

**Assets-to-equity ratio.** Total assets ÷ total equity. Often a measure of the use of debt to finance the business; a higher ratio means relatively more liabilities, which may be undesirable. Also known as financial leverage or the equity multiplier.

**Balance sheet.** One of the three key financial statements. A snapshot at a point in time of assets (things and stuff), liabilities (what is owed) and equity (what is owned). Also known as the statement of financial position.

**Best in Class.** The best "%" or "days" result for each key financial category across all prior periods of your business. A trend analysis tool showing the best performance the business has achieved so far.

**Business Intelligence (BI) Score.** A dollar amount showing how much potential operating cash the business failed to realize because management did not control expenses, convert net income into operating cash flow, and control selected balance sheet categories to best-in-class levels.

**Capital expenditure.** The amount spent to acquire fixed assets (buildings, vehicles, computers, equipment, machinery), typically recorded on the balance sheet at cost.

**Capitalize.** Recording an outlay as an addition to long-term assets on the balance sheet instead of as an expense on the income statement. This reflects the true nature of the spending and spreads its cost over the asset's useful life through depreciation.

**Cash.** A current asset: currency and readily available bank deposits, including petty cash, checking accounts and money market funds.

**Cash equivalents.** A current asset: short-term certificates of deposit or other instruments that can be converted into cash immediately.

**Cash flow.** All cash generated (added) and used (subtracted) during a period.

**Cash flow from financing / investing / operating activities.** See financing cash flow, investing cash flow, operating cash flow.

**Cash flow statement, direct.** One of the three key financial statements. Summarizes all cash generated and used during a period, always categorized as operating, investing or financing.

**Cash flow statement, indirect.** A common alternative to the direct statement. Instead of listing cash inflows and outflows, it reconciles net income to cash generated by operations (operating cash flow) under accrual rules. It is typically concerned only with operating cash flow. (The investing and financing sections, where shown, are the same under either method.)

**Change in cash.** The net increase or decrease in total cash during a period.

**Collections.** On a direct cash flow statement, the cash actually received from customers during the period, by converting sales or receivables into cash.

**Common stock.** An equity line representing ownership: the amount invested by owners or shareholders. Usually used for corporations rather than partnerships. Also known as "shares" in some countries.

**Cost of goods sold (COGS).** Costs attributable to producing the goods sold during the period. In manufacturing: materials, direct labor and a portion of manufacturing overhead. In wholesale or retail: the cost of products bought for resale plus inbound shipping, only for the products sold in the period. In some service businesses: variable costs such as sales commissions.

**Cost of sales, cost of services (COS).** The costs specifically associated with providing a service business's service, typically variable rather than fixed. Also known as cost of goods sold.

**Current assets.** Assets expected to be converted into cash within one year: cash, cash equivalents, accounts receivable, inventory and other current assets such as prepaid expenses and deposits.

**Current liabilities.** Liabilities expected to be settled (reducing cash) within one year.

**Current ratio.** Current assets ÷ current liabilities. A common measure of the ability to pay short-term obligations, especially important to banks as an indicator of liquidity.

**Days sales outstanding (DSO).** See receivable days.

**Debt.** Money borrowed from another party, reported in liabilities.

**Debt-to-equity ratio.** Total liabilities ÷ total equity. A high ratio means heavy reliance on debt to fund growth or operations, and therefore more risk. Important to lenders. (The KPI list defines it as total debt ÷ total equity.)

**Depreciation.** An income statement expense that spreads the cost of fixed assets over their estimated useful life (after which their balance sheet value is zero), according to accounting rules and government regulations. It theoretically reflects the declining value of fixed assets during the period. It reduces earnings but is a non-cash expense, so it does not affect cash or cash flow.

**Depreciation, accumulated.** The total depreciation expensed on fixed assets from the date they were placed in service to the balance sheet date, excluding assets no longer owned. Gross fixed assets minus accumulated depreciation equals net fixed assets.

**Dividends.** A financing cash flow line: the portion of earnings distributed to shareholders. Dividends reduce retained earnings in the equity section.

**Earnings.** Usually net income or profits, but may also mean pretax income (EBT) or EBITDA.

**Earnings, growth rate.** The growth of earnings between periods, as a percentage.

**EBITDA.** Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortization. An earnings subtotal that typically refers to the operating income of the business: net income with interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization added back. Used to compare profitability between businesses because it removes the effects of financing and capitalization decisions.

**EBT.** Earnings Before Taxes.

**Efficiency Score.** A dollar amount showing how much potential net income and operating cash the business failed to realize because expenses in selected income statement categories were not controlled to best-in-class levels.

**Effectiveness Score.** A dollar amount showing how much potential operating cash the business failed to realize because receivables, inventory and payables were not controlled to best-in-class levels.

**Equity.** One of the three major components of the balance sheet: funds contributed by owners (shareholders) plus retained earnings. Equity = assets − liabilities.

**Equity, average.** (Equity at the beginning of the period + equity at the end) ÷ 2.

**Equity multiplier.** See assets-to-equity ratio.

**Expenses.** The operating costs of delivering sales, including marketing, selling, general and administrative expenses. More broadly, any cost that reduces earnings, including operating expenses, depreciation, amortization, interest and taxes.

**Financial leverage.** See assets-to-equity ratio.

**Financial statements.** The records of a business's financial activity for a period: balance sheet, income statement and cash flow statement.

**Financing cash flow (FCF).** One of the three categories on a cash flow statement (cash flow from financing activities). Measures cash flowing between the business and its owners (sale or purchase of stock, dividends) and between the business and its creditors (borrowing or repaying loan principal). (Note: FCF here is financing cash flow, not free cash flow.)

**Finished goods.** Inventory that is complete and ready for sale.

**Fixed asset investment.** An investing cash flow line: cash used or received from buying or selling fixed assets. Also known as capital expenditure.

**Free cash flow.** Operating cash flow minus fixed asset investment: the net cash generated from operations less the cash used to buy fixed assets. Ideally positive, because the business can self-fund without third-party capital and has excess cash to reinvest, repay debt, or distribute to shareholders (owners) as dividends.

**General and administrative (G&A).** Operating expenses for the day-to-day overhead of the business (office rent, office utilities, salaries of office staff), not directly related to COGS, COS, marketing or sales.

**Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP).** The code of accounting principles, standards and procedures followed by businesses and certified (or chartered) public accountants in compiling and auditing financial statements.

**Goodwill.** An intangible asset arising when one business acquires another and pays more than the value of its net assets. It represents what the buyer pays for client lists, non-compete covenants, processes, technology, brand name and other intangibles. It must be amortized over its estimated life.

**Gross fixed assets.** The historical cost (purchase price) of the fixed assets.

**Gross profit.** Sales minus cost of goods sold.

**Income statement.** One of the three key financial statements: a summary of sales and expenses, with the difference reported as net income (profits, earnings). Also known as the profit and loss statement (P&L), statement of operations, or statement of earnings. Under accrual accounting, the income statement is the theory of how much sales exceed expenses.

**Income taxes.** Taxes levied by local, state and federal governments, based on the earnings generated during a period.

**Inventory.** A current asset: the cost of raw materials, work in process and finished goods ready for sale. Can be valued by different methods (first in first out, last in first out, weighted average). Called "stock" in some countries.

**Inventory, average.** (Beginning inventory + ending inventory) ÷ 2.

**Inventory days.** The average number of days it takes to turn (use) inventory, including raw materials and work in progress, into sales: days in the period ÷ inventory turnover. (Simplified for ease of use.)

**Inventory paid.** A cash flow statement line: cash spent to buy inventory.

**Inventory turnover.** How many times inventory is sold and replenished in a period: COGS ÷ average inventory.

**Investing cash flow (ICF).** One of the three categories on a cash flow statement (cash flow from investing activities): the change in cash from investing activities, including buying or selling fixed assets and other investments.

**Liabilities.** One of the three major sections of the balance sheet (often called "debt"): creditors' claims on the business's assets; amounts owed.

**Liquidity.** The ability to pay current liabilities with current assets.

**Marketing, selling, general and administrative (MSG&A).** Expenses not specifically related to providing goods or services, and therefore not in COGS. Also known as overhead.

**Matching principle.** The accrual rule that transactions are recorded in the period in which something is earned, owed or used, with expenses matched to the revenue event in the same period. The critical distinction from cash accounting, where revenue and expenses are not recognized until cash changes hands.

**Net cash provided by financing / investing / operating activities.** See financing cash flow, investing cash flow, operating cash flow.

**Net fixed assets.** Gross fixed assets minus accumulated depreciation.

**Net income.** Total earnings: sales minus all expenses, including non-cash charges such as depreciation and amortization. One of the three key measures of performance. Also known as net profit, earnings, or the bottom line.

**Notes receivable, trade.** Outstanding principal on interest-bearing loans owed to the business, arising from the sale of products or services.

**Operating cash flow (OCF).** One of the three major sections of the cash flow statement (cash flow from operating activities) and one of the three key measures of performance: the cash a business generates and uses by running the business. It excludes cash related to fixed asset investment, other investments, borrowing and repaying loans, buying or selling owner's equity, dividends and shareholder distributions.

**Operating cash flow, growth rate.** The growth of cash flow from operations between periods, as a percentage.

**Operating income.** Sales minus COGS minus marketing, selling, general and administrative expenses. Also known as operating profit. (Caution: other professionals may define it differently, and it is often used as a synonym for EBITDA.)

**Owners' equity.** See equity.

**P&L.** See income statement.

**Paid-in capital.** An equity line: amounts invested (paid in) by investors (owners); funds raised by selling equity.

**Payable days.** The average time a business takes to pay its accounts payable during a period.

**Profit.** See net income, operating income, EBITDA, gross profit.

**Profit after tax.** See net income.

**Profit margin.** See return on sales.

**Property, plant and equipment.** See assets, fixed.

**Quick ratio.** (Cash + accounts receivable) ÷ (accounts payable + other current liabilities). A measure of short-term liquidity.

**Raw materials.** Unprocessed materials in inventory needed to produce finished goods.

**Receivable days.** The average number of days needed to collect cash owed by customers after a sale: days in the period ÷ receivable turnover. All revenue earned but not collected at the time of sale is recorded in accounts receivable.

**Receivable turnover.** Sales ÷ average receivables. Reflects how effective the business is at extending credit and collecting debts.

**Receivables, average.** (Receivables at the beginning of the period + receivables at the end) ÷ 2.

**Retained earnings.** An equity line: accumulated net income since the beginning of the business's life that has not been paid out in dividends or distributions.

**Return on assets (ROA).** Net income ÷ average assets. One of the three key measures of performance: effectiveness in generating earnings from assets.

**Return on equity (ROE).** Net income ÷ average equity: effectiveness in generating earnings from the equity on the balance sheet.

**Return on sales (ROS).** Net income ÷ sales: operational efficiency in generating profit from sales. Also known as net margin, net profit margin or profit margin.

**Revenue.** See sales.

**Sales.** The first line of the income statement: the total value of goods and services sold during the period. Also known as revenue; called "turnover" in some countries.

**Sales, growth rate.** Sales growth from one period to another, as a percentage.

**Shareholder's equity.** See equity.

**Shares.** In some countries, the term for common stock.

**Solvency.** The ability to keep operating while meeting long-term expenses and obligations and funding long-term growth. One test is whether assets exceed liabilities; another is whether the business can pay its bills when they come due.

**Statement of earnings / statement of operations.** See income statement.

**Statement of financial position.** See balance sheet.

**Stock.** See common stock. (In some countries, "stock" means inventory.)

**Taxes payable.** A liability: income taxes owed to governments.

**Turnover.** In some countries, sales or revenue.

**Work in process (WIP).** Inventory of partially completed goods somewhere in the manufacturing process, not ready for sale. Includes material, direct labor and manufacturing overhead (raw materials excluded).
