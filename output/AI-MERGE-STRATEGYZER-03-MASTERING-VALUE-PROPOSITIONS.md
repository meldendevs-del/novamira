# Mastering Value Propositions: Complete Reference

## 1. Why Value Propositions Fail, and the Value Proposition Canvas

### The problem

Every day, companies design products and services to improve their customers' lives. But seventy-two percent (72%) of new product and service innovations fail to deliver on expectations. In other words, customers don't care about seven out of ten new products introduced to the market. It doesn't have to be this way. Just as the Business Model Canvas lets you design how your business creates value, there is a tool to intentionally visualize, design, and test how you create value for customers: the Value Proposition Canvas.

### What the Value Proposition Canvas is

**What it is**: A two-part canvas. The right-hand side is the **Customer Profile** (drawn as a circle), which describes a specific customer segment. The left-hand side is the **Value Map** (drawn as a square), which describes the value proposition you offer that segment. The printed canvas labels the right-hand side "Customer Segment" and the left-hand side "Value Proposition".

**The Customer Profile (right-hand side, "the circle")** has three fields:

- **Customer Jobs**: what your customer is trying to get done. Jobs can be functional (getting from A to B), social (impressing friends and colleagues), or emotional (gaining peace of mind).
- **Pains**: what annoys customers while trying to get a job done. Pains are negative outcomes that customers hope to avoid: dissatisfaction with existing solutions, and challenges, frustrations, risks, or obstacles related to performing a job. Pains describe what failure looks like to the customer.
- **Gains**: how customers measure the success of a job well done. Gains are positive outcomes that customers hope to achieve: concrete results, benefits, and even aspirations. Gains describe what success looks like to the customer.

Use the Customer Profile to visualize, test, and track your understanding of the people or companies you intend to create value for. It is a map that becomes clearer the more you learn about your customers.

**The Value Map (left-hand side, "the square")** has three fields:

- **Products & Services**: the list of products and services your value proposition builds on.
- **Pain Relievers**: in which way these products, services, and features eliminate, reduce, or minimize pains customers care about, making their life easier.
- **Gain Creators**: in which way they produce, increase, or maximize outcomes and benefits that your customers expect, desire, or would be surprised by.

The Value Map makes explicit how your products and services relieve pains and create gains. Use it to design, test, and iterate your value proposition until you figure out what resonates with customers.

**How the two sides work together**: Jobs-to-be-done is the core of the Customer Profile. You describe what customers are trying to get done, then use the gains field to describe what success looks like to them and the pains field to describe what failure looks like to them. Once you understand the customer jobs well, you describe how you create value for customers in the Value Map. This makes explicit how your products and services help them get a job done.

**Fit**: You achieve fit by creating a clear connection between what matters to customers and how your products, services, and features ease pains and create gains. Great value propositions target essential customer jobs, pains, and gains, and do so extremely well. Your Customer Profile may contain countless jobs, pains, and gains, but your Value Map highlights which ones you intend to focus on.

**Caveat**: An outstanding value proposition can still fail if your business model is flawed. Successful companies embed outstanding value propositions in scalable and profitable business models. Use the Value Proposition Canvas to create products and services customers want, and the Business Model Canvas to make sure they can be delivered profitably (see Section 12).

## 2. Customer Jobs-To-Be-Done

### Origins of the concept

The origins of the jobs-to-be-done concept can be described with the analogy of a tree. At the root, different people coined the concept independently, in different corners of the world: Tony Ulwick, Rick Pedi, Bob Moesta, and Denise Nitterhouse. The concept became very popular when Tony Ulwick (of Strategyn) and Clayton Christensen (Harvard professor and founder of the consulting company Innosight) popularized it through their articles. It spread around the world and started to be used in innovation, product management, and marketing. It fits value proposition design perfectly.

### Jobs versus "what customers want"

**What it is**: A shift from describing *who* customers are and *what they want* to describing *what jobs they are trying to get done*.

**Why it works**: In marketing, we used to describe mainly who we are targeting and what these people want. The limitation is in asking what people want. Customers are notoriously bad at describing what they want, in particular when it comes to new products, services, and value propositions. The famous line attributed to Henry Ford illustrates this: if he had asked customers what they wanted, they would probably have said a faster horse. (Ford never actually said this, but the point stands.) So when you talk to customers, don't ask them what they want. Get them to talk about the jobs they are trying to get done and the pains and gains related to those jobs. To create great value propositions, focus not on what products customers want but on what jobs they are trying to get done.

**Implication for segmentation**: It is worth questioning whether segmenting customers by socio-demographic profile is the right approach. If you segment by jobs, pains, and gains, you may find very different segments, and you may discover that very diverse customers have very similar jobs.

### Definition of a job

A job is what a customer is trying to get done in their work (professional context) or in their life. It includes:

- **tasks** they are trying to perform and complete,
- **problems** they are trying to solve, and
- **needs** they are trying to satisfy.

**Context matters.** Also consider the context in which jobs are performed, because context changes the constraints. For example, making a phone call while driving a car comes with very different constraints than making a phone call from your sofa at home.

### Example: packing for a business trip

Ask yourself: what are you going to pack for your next business trip?

- **Fresh underwear**, because you need to change into fresh clothes every day.
- **A beautiful watch**, because you want to be perceived as successful by colleagues and clients.
- **A couple of photographs of your family**, because you want to feel connected with home.

The reasons on the right (changing clothes, being perceived as successful, feeling connected with home) are the jobs you are trying to get done. The items on the left are the products and their value propositions that address those jobs.

### Jobs last; solutions change

**Why focus on jobs rather than products**: jobs are relatively stable over time, whereas value propositions and their products and services can change dramatically.

**Example: the music industry.** The job to be done is listening to music. The products have changed enormously:

- In 1983 the main product was still vinyl. Cassettes were increasingly conquering the market, and CDs were only a tiny sliver.
- From 1983 onward, the cassette market shrank, vinyl faded away, and CDs grew for a long time, gaining market share all the way up to 2004.
- Around 2004 it switched: CDs started losing share, while music downloads, ringtones, and paid subscriptions gained a lot.
- By 2014 there were very many different products and value propositions helping people get the job of listening to music done, and this will likely keep changing.

If you understand the job the customer is trying to get done, it becomes clearer how you can improve your product or create new products and value propositions.

### Case: why customers "hire" a milkshake

**The situation**: A fast food company wanted to increase milkshake sales. They brought customers in and asked them what they wanted. Customers said they wanted chocolatier milkshakes, fruitier milkshakes, cheaper milkshakes. The company changed the product accordingly, and it had absolutely no impact on sales. Lesson: it is not enough to just talk to customers; you must frame the problem the right way.

**The reframe**: The company brought in Clayton Christensen's consulting company, Innosight, which asked a very different question: *What job causes people to hire a milkshake?* A consultant stood in front of a store and observed customers for hours, from early morning until late evening. He observed that:

- half of the milkshakes were sold before 8 AM,
- buyers were always alone,
- it was the only thing they bought, and
- they drove right off with it.

**The insight**: The milkshake was helping with a very specific job: having something to do during a long and boring commute to work.

**What the company changed** to serve that job better:

- a **thicker shake**, so it takes longer to drink,
- a **thinner straw**, also to slow down drinking,
- **fruit chunks** in the shake, to make the commute more interesting and the experience more unpredictable.

**Competitive framing**: The milkshake's real competitors are not other milkshakes or fast food chains. They are bananas, donuts, bagels, and Snickers bars. Frame your market not in terms of look-alike competitors but in terms of whoever provides a solution to the job to be done. Because the company framed the market around the customer's job, it saw the market was much larger, and it increased milkshake sales fourfold.

**Lessons**:

1. Jobs-to-be-done focuses on what people are trying to get done, not who they are and what they want.
2. Customer jobs are relatively stable over time; value propositions, products, and services can change dramatically.
3. Consider the context in which customers hire products and services, to build great value propositions around the right products and services.

### The four job types

**Example set-up: the business traveler.** The solution could be a hotel, Airbnb, or staying with friends. The jobs remain the same; the solutions (value propositions) that help with those jobs could be very different. For this exercise, focus only on the Customer Profile and blank out the Value Map (the solutions side).

**Exercise prompt**: Imagine you are traveling by airplane to a business meeting. What jobs are you trying to get done as a business traveler? Take a few minutes to write down as many jobs as you can think of. When trying to identify a customer's jobs, ask: "What are they trying to get done in their work and in their lives?"

**Sample brainstorm of business traveler jobs**:

- attend to business (the reason you are traveling),
- stay somewhere overnight,
- reserve accommodation (booking a hotel, or calling a friend to stay with them),
- feel connected with home (calling friends, family, spouse),
- sleep and get some rest,
- feel safe where you stay,
- eat while traveling,
- look good with clients (depending on where you stay).

**The four types**:

1. **Functional jobs**: the tasks your customers are trying to get done. Examples: mowing the lawn, eating healthily, writing a report. The business traveler list contains four functional jobs related to business travel.
2. **Social jobs**: how the customer wants to be perceived by others. Nobody buys a Rolex to read the time (that would be the functional job); a Rolex helps with the social job of self-presentation and status. The business traveler had one social job: looking good with clients.
3. **Emotional jobs**: how the customer wants to feel. Examples: peace of mind when investing, feeling job security. The business traveler had two emotional jobs: feeling connected with home and feeling safe while traveling.
4. **Supporting jobs**: jobs arising mainly in the context of purchasing and consuming value. The business traveler had one supporting job: reserving accommodation.

### Ranking jobs by importance

Once you have brainstormed jobs, ask how important each one actually is to customers. Which are really important, which are less important, and which are even insignificant? Rank them one by one, most important at the top and least important at the bottom. Then get out of the building and check whether what you came up with in the meeting room is right.

Remember the business traveler is just an example. Keep generating customer jobs until you believe you have a deep understanding of what your customers are trying to get done.

### Customer Jobs trigger questions

Jobs describe the things customers are trying to get done in their work or life: tasks to perform and complete, problems to solve, needs to satisfy. Use these trigger questions to think of different potential jobs:

1. What is the one thing that your customer couldn't live without accomplishing? What are the stepping stones that could help your customer achieve this key job?
2. What are the different contexts that your customers might be in? How do their activities and goals change depending on these different contexts?
3. What does your customer need to accomplish that involves interaction with others?
4. What tasks are your customers trying to perform in their work or personal life? What functional problems are your customers trying to solve?
5. Are there problems that you think customers have that they may not even be aware of?
6. What emotional needs are your customers trying to satisfy? What jobs, if completed, would give the user a sense of self-satisfaction?
7. How does your customer want to be perceived by others? What can your customer do to help themselves be perceived this way?
8. How does your customer want to feel? What does your customer need to do to feel this way?
9. Track your customer's interaction with a product or service throughout its lifespan. What supporting jobs surface throughout this life cycle? Does the user switch roles throughout this process?

### Exercise: map your customer's jobs

Sketch out a Customer Profile, ideally for a customer segment you know well.

1. Print out or draw a Customer Profile (or use a digital canvas tool).
2. Don't write on the canvas. Use sticky notes so you can move things around. Write only one idea per sticky note, and don't use bullet points.
3. Use the customer jobs trigger questions if you need help listing jobs.
4. Think of the jobs that really matter to your customers, beyond those related directly to your products and services.

## 3. Unbundling Customer Profiles

**What it is**: Decomposing one "customer" into separate customer profiles for each stakeholder involved in a purchase, each with its own Value Proposition Canvas.

**Why it works**: This matters most in business-to-business. When you sell a value proposition to another company, each organization has different stakeholders you are effectively selling to, and each has different jobs, pains, and gains. One combined profile hides those differences. The same applies to any complex purchase, such as a family purchase.

**How to do it** (a method from Steve Blank): decompose the company's customer profile into these customer types, and consider a separate Value Proposition Canvas for each important one:

1. **End users**: the people who will actually use the product.
2. **Influencers**: people with an informal say over the purchase, for example a power user who can influence the decision maker.
3. **Recommenders**: people with a formal say. They carry out the search and evaluation of different solutions and make a formal recommendation to the economic buyer and decision maker.
4. **Economic buyer**: the person who holds the budget and can make the sale happen financially.
5. **Decision maker**: the person with the ultimate say over purchasing or not purchasing.
6. **Saboteur**: someone without a formal say who does not want the purchase to happen and will do everything in their power to stop it. Most people who have done B2B sales have met one.

**Example 1 (B2B)**: Google wants to sell Google Apps for Work into companies that probably already use Microsoft Office. The end users are the employees who will use Google Apps; around them sit influencers (power users), recommenders (who evaluate alternatives), the economic buyer (budget holder), the decision maker, and possibly a saboteur.

**Example 2 (a family buying a game console)**, first the cliché version:

- End user: probably the child.
- Influencers: the child's friends.
- Recommender: the child, who does the search and recommends a console.
- Economic buyer: the father, who manages the budget.
- Decision maker: the mother.
- Saboteur: the sister, who won't get anything out of it and doesn't want the sale to happen.

Reality might look different: the end users are probably the father *and* the child, and the mother is the recommender (doing the search and evaluation), the economic buyer, and the decision maker. The point is not the clichés but that you must work out who plays which role.

**Lessons**: In some contexts (B2B, families, other complex purchases) you need to unbundle your customer profile. Customer types can be end users, influencers, recommenders, economic buyers, decision makers, and saboteurs. Each has its own Value Proposition Canvas, so ask which of the customers you sell to need a separate one.

## 4. Customer Pains

### Definition

A pain is an undesired outcome, a problem, or a dislike that a customer experiences before, during, or after trying to get a job done. It can also be an obstacle that prevents a customer from getting a job done well, or a risk: a potential bad outcome that, if it happens, is a big pain. Pains are the second element of the Customer Profile.

### Example: business traveler pains (finding and using accommodation)

Keep the jobs on the canvas but blank them out and focus on the pains:

- check-in and check-out are a big pain,
- tipping: you never know how much to give,
- a noisy accommodation, very annoying for a business traveler,
- Wi-Fi: after paying $400 for a room, you don't want to pay another $20 or more for a good Wi-Fi connection,
- a difficult shower: standing in front of it not knowing how to turn it on,
- a bad mattress, which prevents you from sleeping,
- finding out you paid too much because you used the wrong website,
- forgetting your laptop adapter or cable when you have work to get done,
- lacking the time to find accommodation, or to look at the choices your assistant presented.

### The three types of pain

1. **Undesired outcomes, problems, and dislikes**: things you don't like when you perform a job, or a solution that doesn't work the way it should. Most of the business traveler pains fall here.
2. **Obstacles**: things that prevent the customer from getting a job done well. The business traveler example has one: lack of time to evaluate the different solutions or look at what the assistant presented.
3. **Risks**: potential bad outcomes. They don't always go wrong, but if they do, they are a real pain. The business traveler has quite a few (for example forgetting the laptop adapter and being unable to work).

### Ranking pains by severity

As with jobs, rank pains. Ask which pains the customer finds **extreme** and which **moderate**, and order them from most severe at the top to most moderate at the bottom. (Forgetting your laptop adapter and being unable to get work done is pretty annoying.)

### Two ways to fill the pains field

**Method 1: job by job.** Select a job (start with a top job) and list all the pains related to it. Example, job "sleep and get rest":

- bad mattress,
- noise, made concrete: loud air conditioning, a room beside the elevator, noisy neighbors coming home late at night,
- the room is not dark enough,
- the wrong pillow (for example one that is too big).

Keep going until you have all the pains related to that job.

**Method 2: free-form brainstorming.** You might start with pains, jump to customer jobs, then to gains, and keep going until the entire Customer Profile is filled out. You don't have to go job by job.

### Customer Pains trigger questions

Pains describe anything that annoys customers before, during, and after trying to get a job done, or that simply prevents them from getting it done. Pains also describe risks: potential bad outcomes related to getting a job done badly or not at all.

1. How do your customers define too costly? Takes a lot of time, costs too much money, or requires substantial efforts?
2. What makes your customers feel bad? What are their frustrations, annoyances, or things that give them a headache?
3. How are current value propositions underperforming for your customers? Which features are they missing? Are there performance issues that annoy them or malfunctions they cite?
4. What are the main difficulties and challenges your customers encounter? Do they understand how things work, have difficulties getting certain things done, or resist particular jobs for specific reasons?
5. What negative social consequences do your customers encounter or fear? Are they afraid of a loss of face, power, trust, or status?
6. What risks do your customers fear? Are they afraid of financial, social, or technical risks, or are they asking themselves what could go wrong?
7. What's keeping your customers awake at night? What are their big issues, concerns, and worries?
8. What common mistakes do your customers make? Are they using a solution the wrong way?
9. What barriers are keeping your customers from adopting a value proposition? Are there upfront investment costs, a steep learning curve, or other obstacles preventing adoption?

**Lessons**: There are three types of pains (undesired outcomes/problems/dislikes, obstacles, risks). Rank them with the most extreme on top and moderate ones at the bottom. Fill the field one job at a time or free-form.

## 5. Customer Gains

### Definition

A gain describes an outcome or benefit customers want. It is how the customer measures the success of a job well done. Gains can come from functional utility, social gains, positive emotions, or cost savings.

### Example: business traveler gains

- a clean room,
- Wi-Fi, and better, high-speed Wi-Fi,
- power plugs in the right place, for example beside the bed,
- catching six to eight hours of sleep during a business trip,
- a custom mattress to help you sleep,
- room service, made more exact by specifying the wait time you expect,
- accommodation close to where your meetings are,
- free laundry.

### The four types of gain

1. **Required gains**: the solution won't work without them. For the business traveler: a clean room, and Wi-Fi.
2. **Expected gains**: gains we expect from a solution even though it could work without them. For the business traveler: high-speed Wi-Fi, and power plugs beside the bed so you can work in the evening.
3. **Desired gains**: gains beyond what you expect, which you would really love. For the business traveler: getting six hours of sleep during business travel, often not possible.
4. **Unexpected gains**: gains that go beyond what customers desire, which they might not even be able to come up with. For the business traveler: a custom mattress like the one at home, or free laundry.

### Ranking gains by relevance

Look at gain relevance: which gains really matter? Rank from **essential** to **nice to have**.

### Two ways to fill the gains field

**Method 1: job by job.** Pick a job, for example "stay somewhere overnight", and list all related gains:

- a spacious room,
- design furniture,
- selecting the room yourself while booking,
- a good gym,
- an available spa,
- a room with a view,
- a soundproof room, which helps you sleep.

This produces many detailed gains per job.

**Method 2: free-form brainstorming.** Start with gains, move to pains and jobs as ideas come, and continue until the profile is complete.

### Customer Gains trigger questions

Gains describe the outcomes and benefits customers want. Some are required, expected, or desired, and some would surprise them. Gains include functional utility, social gains, positive emotions, and cost savings.

1. Which savings would make your customers happy? Which savings in terms of time, money, and effort would they value?
2. What quality levels do they expect, and what would they wish for more or less of?
3. How do current value propositions delight your customers? Which specific features do they enjoy? What performance and quality do they expect?
4. What would make your customers' jobs or lives easier? Could there be a flatter learning curve, more services, or lower costs of ownership?
5. What positive social consequences do your customers desire? What makes them look good? What increases their power or their status?
6. What are customers looking for most? Are they searching for good design, guarantees, specific or more features?
7. What do customers dream about? What do they aspire to achieve, or what would be a big relief to them?
8. How do your customers measure success and failure? How do they gauge performance or cost?
9. What would increase your customers' likelihood of adopting a value proposition? Do they desire lower cost, less investment, lower risk, or better quality?

### Exercise: map out pains and gains

1. Retrieve the Customer Profile you started with jobs, and add pains and gains on sticky notes.
2. Use the pains and gains trigger questions if you need help.
3. Go deep. The more pains and gains you have, the better your understanding of your customers.

**Lessons**: There are four types of gains (required, expected, desired, unexpected). Rank which gains customers see as essential and which as nice to have. Fill the field one job at a time or free-form.

## 6. Case Study: Tesla Customer Profile

**Segment**: Tesla targets upper-middle-class men with an income of about $100,000 or more. (A later lesson describes the same Model S segment as "middle class male with an income of $100,000 and more".)

**Jobs**:

- Functional: commuting to work by car, occasional long-distance trips, personal mobility.
- Social: conveying an image of success, differentiating from others.
- Emotional: being in sync with personal values when buying a car (being green or environmentally friendly, or the status of the car).

**Pains** (obstacles, problems, risks to avoid):

- General car pains: accidents (number one for every driver), bad driving behavior of the car, above-average maintenance (car in the shop all the time), buying just before a price drop (one of the biggest).
- Electric-vehicle-specific pains: having to charge frequently (you want to drive whenever you like), the battery being drained by air conditioning on a hot sunny day, the risk of a dead battery at the wrong moment (for example stuck on the highway), a lack of charging stations outside the home (for example on the highway), a lack of nearby servicing (typical with a new brand), and not wanting to be laughed at for driving an electric car.
- Other vehicle pains: lack of space, lack of parking space, speeding tickets, and other general pains of driving.

**Gains** (expected, wanted, even desired):

- Performance, perhaps like a sports car, when spending a lot of money on a car.
- Good design (the design of a BMW would be nice).
- A recognizable brand, for example like Mercedes, not a poor brand.
- EV-specific: the latest battery technology, an acceptable charging time, and a range of 250 to 350 kilometers without recharging.
- Seating capacity of four to five people.
- High-end after-sales service: being treated like a valuable customer.
- A high safety rating.
- A high-performing GPS system.
- State-of-the-art electronics.
- In the dreaming space: a self-driving car (some high-end vehicles already self-drive, and Google is working on a fully self-driving car).
- Getting compliments from friends.

## 7. Customer Profile Best Practices (Common Mistakes)

**Mistake 1: sketching the profile while focused on your value proposition.** When you map jobs, pains, and gains, almost forget what you're offering. Step into the customer's shoes, live their life, and ask what their most important jobs, pains, and gains are. In the Tesla case, don't think too much about the electric vehicle: customers have a choice. They could buy a conventional car, commute by bus or train, maybe even by helicopter.

**Mistake 2: too few jobs, pains, and gains.** People sometimes only list the jobs, pains, and gains they are already targeting. Instead, build a complete picture: all the jobs customers are trying to get done, all the pains (obstacles, problems, risks they face or fear), and all the gains they expect or desire. Your value proposition will not target all of them; you will choose. But you need to see them all to select deliberately.

**Mistake 3: focusing on functional jobs only.** In the Tesla profile there are three functional jobs (commuting, occasional long trips, personal mobility), but also social jobs (conveying success, differentiating from others) and emotional jobs (being in sync with personal values such as being environmentally friendly, or communicating a certain image). This matters especially in B2B, where we over-focus on functional jobs, yet a customer may decide not on the best solution but on social or emotional jobs.

**Mistake 4: mixing up jobs and outcomes.** "Commuting to work" is a job customers get done every day. "A range of 250 to 350 kilometers" is an outcome they want to achieve, a characteristic: a gain. Jobs describe what customers are trying to get done. Pains and gains describe what they are trying to avoid (unwanted outcomes, obstacles, risks) or achieve (outcomes they want in order to perform their jobs well).

**Mistake 5: being too vague about pains and gains.** For seating, "four to five people" tells you what success means. For range, don't write "enough to commute": be specific. The more concrete you are, the better you understand how customers measure success and failure, and the better you can measure whether your value proposition creates value.

**Mistake 6: mixing too many customer segments in one profile.** The Tesla profile is fine because it is one homogeneous segment. But in B2B you may be selling to the buyer, the user, or someone else in the company. Keep a relatively concise profile for the one segment you believe you are selling to (see Section 3).

## 8. Identifying High-Value Jobs

**What it is**: A way to rank jobs not only from the customer's perspective but by how interesting they are for *you* to address as an organization.

**Why it works**: Customer importance alone doesn't tell you where to play. A high-value job combines four characteristics, like an equation: **important + tangible + unsatisfied + lucrative**.

**The four characteristics**:

1. **Important**: the customer ranks the job very high because failing it leads to extreme pains (example: a security breach at a bank) or to missing out on essential gains (example: revenues from the sales force).
2. **Tangible**: you can feel or see the pain (example: the email avalanche in our inbox every day) or feel or see the gain (example: pain relief from anesthesia).
3. **Unsatisfied**: there are unresolved pains (example: aging; for some people it is a pain with no relief yet) or unrealized gains (example: instant translation while traveling in a country whose language you don't speak).
4. **Lucrative**: there are many customers with this job, pain, or gain (example: the need to communicate on the move), or there are few customers who are willing to pay a lot (a deliberately silly example: traveling to space).

**How to do it (High-Value Jobs worksheet)**:

Objective: identify high-value customer jobs you could focus on. Outcome: a ranking of customer jobs from your perspective. The worksheet's practice case: imagine your customers are chief information officers (CIOs) and you must understand which jobs matter most to them; or apply it to your own Customer Profile.

1. List the most important jobs from your Customer Profile in the first "Jobs" column.
2. Score each job on each characteristic, discussing the worksheet questions:
   - Important: Does failing the job lead to extreme pains? Does failing the job lead to missing out on essential gains?
   - Tangible: Can you feel the pain? Can you see the gain?
   - Unsatisfied: Are there unresolved pains? Are there unrealized gains?
   - Lucrative: Are there many with this job, pain, or gain? Are there few willing to pay a lot?
3. Use the scoring scale from • (low) to • • • • (high).
4. Add up the scores in the last "High-Value Jobs" column.
5. Focus on the highest-value jobs and their related pains and gains.

**Lessons**: It's not enough to look at jobs from the customer perspective; also look at which jobs are interesting for you to address. High-value jobs are important, tangible, unsatisfied, and lucrative.

## 9. Gaining Customer Insights

### Six techniques to gain customer insights

**What it is**: Six research roles you can play, on your own or together with a market research firm. The objective is to move from an *imagined* customer dreamed up in the meeting room toward a *real-world pattern* informed by research.

**Why a mix**: Each technique has different strengths and weaknesses. Some work well for existing customer behavior; others are much better suited to new customer behavior. To understand customers well, combine several.

#### 1. The Data Detective

**What**: Explores existing data inside and outside the company to understand customer behavior (details below).

**Examples**: the social web (which topics are on customers' minds, how they talk about value propositions or brands), the CRM system (problems customers deal with today), Google Trends and the keywords customers search for, and data mining on how customers behave in relation to the company.

**Advantages**: You can start very quickly; it is a great foundation for further research.
**Disadvantages**: The data may be static and comes from a different context, so it might not apply to the value proposition you are designing.

#### 2. The Journalist

**What**: Gets out of the building and talks to potential customers and partners to understand their jobs, pains, and gains, and the value propositions they already use (details and interview rules below).

**Key principle**: Gather facts, not opinions. "Would you do something?" gets an opinion. "When is the last time you did this or that?" gets instances of real-world behavior.

**Advantages**: Quick and cheap; gives first insights and learnings for value proposition design.
**Disadvantages**: Real-world behavior is often different from what customers say. What customers say and do is not necessarily the same.

#### 3. The Anthropologist

**What**: Observes customers carefully, over a longer period, rather than only talking to them.

**Examples**:

- B2C: staying with a family to understand their context and the jobs, pains, and gains that arise in it.
- B2B: working alongside a customer, for example by consulting for them, to understand what they are really dealing with. A perfect way to understand jobs, pains, and gains.
- B2C: observing shopping behavior, a powerful way to understand the choices customers make.
- B2C and B2B: shadowing customers for a day to see the jobs, pains, and gains that arise in their work or life.

**Advantages**: An unbiased view; lets you discover real-world behavior you might not get by only talking to customers.
**Disadvantages**: Insights may not relate to new ideas and new behavior. When designing value propositions involving completely new customer behavior, you can't always observe what you want in the real world.

#### 4. The Impersonator

**What**: Steps into the customer's shoes and tries to live the customer's life.

**Example**: the mystery shopper, who lives the shopping experience in a particular context and deeply understands what customers deal with. The same applies to using a product or service, or generally dealing with a particular customer problem, job, pain, or gain.

**Advantages**: First-hand experience of jobs, pains, and gains and what is difficult about them.
**Disadvantages**: It can't be applied to all types of customer problems; sometimes it simply doesn't work.

#### 5. The Co-creator

**What**: Designs value propositions together with customers. The range goes from **co-ideation** (integrating customers to explore new ideas) to **co-design** and even **co-building** (the customer deeply involved in designing and building the product).

**Example: the product box exercise (co-ideation).** Invite customers to your offices. Give them the problem to solve and ask them to design a product box (the packaging) for a non-existing product: slogans on the outside, what the product does, why it performs well, how it looks. Then have them sell their product box back to you. You will learn a ton about jobs, pains, and gains and the pain relievers and gain creators they want, before you even start designing a product.

**Advantages**: Proximity with customers; very deep insights.
**Disadvantages**: Insights from working with some customers might not generalize to others, so be careful.

#### 6. The Scientist

**What**: Runs experiments with customers to validate or invalidate value proposition or business model hypotheses. An experiment is about getting customers to perform a **call to action**: the business scientist prompts a subject to perform an action (signing up for an email, a pre-purchase) that validates or invalidates a hypothesis.

**Example: the unique link.** Create a unique URL to a web page describing the value proposition you want to sell. When you talk to customers, don't just ask whether they are interested; tell them they can find more information at that link. Back at the office, check whether they clicked. If they did, they were probably genuinely interested. If they didn't, something else was probably more important to them.

**Advantages**: Facts: real-world evidence that something might work or not.
**Disadvantages**: It can be hard to use in existing organizations because of strict customer policies and guidelines.

### The Journalist in depth: the interviewing process and eight ground rules

**Why it matters**: Conducting interviews seems simple, but conducting great interviews that reveal relevant insights is an art. Bad interviews can lead you in the wrong direction, and you may build something customers don't care about. Focus on unearthing what matters to (potential) customers rather than pitching them solutions.

**The interviewing process**:

1. **Create a Customer Profile** of what you think the relevant jobs, pains, and gains are.
2. **Create an interview outline** from that profile: the questions you will ask in the field.
3. **Conduct the interviews**, keeping them fairly free-form, because you'll learn what really matters and may steer the conversation one way or another.
4. **Capture each interview on a Customer Profile** afterward, back in the meeting room, or even during the interview. With two interviewers, one can take notes directly on a Value Proposition Canvas: jobs, pains, gains, the products and services the customer uses or would like, and the pain relievers and gain creators.
5. **Review and create a new interview outline** based on what really matters. Especially at the beginning, the outline will change quite a bit.
6. **Search for patterns** once you've done enough interviews: put all the interview profiles on a wall and ask whether you see similar jobs, pains, and gains across people. This produces your first insights into what customers really care about.

The Customer Profile guides the whole process: in designing the interview and in capturing it.

**The eight ground rules**:

**Rule 1: Adopt a beginner's mind.** You have opinions and experience; forget them in the interview. Listen with a fresh pair of ears and avoid interpreting too early based on your business or customer experience. Don't make too many assumptions; it is the customer who should answer the questions. In particular, explore unexpected jobs, pains, and gains, because that is where you learn the most.

**Rule 2: Listen more than you talk.** Your goal is to listen and learn, not to inform, impress, or convince the customer of anything. It is easy to get off track and start rambling about what you care about; every minute spent on your own beliefs is at the expense of learning about the customer. Ask questions that get the customer talking.

**Rule 3: Get facts, not opinions.**

- Don't ask: "Would you...?" ("Would you do this? Would you buy that? What would you do in this situation?") This gets opinions, which may be acceptable sometimes, but you want evidence.
- Ask: "When is the last time you faced this situation?" "When is the last time you purchased this item?" "Tell me about a time when you faced this particular pain / had an obstacle preventing you from getting a job done well." These get concrete past instances.

**Rule 4: Ask "why" to get real motivations.** Ask "Why do you need to do...?", "Why is ___ important to you?", "Why is ___ such a pain?", "When did you last face this pain? Why did you get into that situation?" Example chain: "I want to learn a new methodology." Why? "To improve my business skills." Why? "Because I want to advance in my career." Asking why a couple of times gets you to the underlying jobs, pains, and gains.

**Rule 5: Learn, don't sell (even if a sale is involved).** Don't ask "Would you buy our solution?" Ask "What are your decision criteria when you make a purchase of...?" Learn about the jobs, pains, gains, and the products and services customers already buy or desire; that knowledge is what will help you sell later.

**Rule 6: Don't mention solutions (your prototype value proposition) too early.** Opening with "Our solution does..." pushes the interview in the direction you care about. Ask instead: "What are the most important things you are struggling with?" Test the circle (Customer Profile) before you test the square (Value Map). Presenting solutions early can give the interview the wrong direction.

**Rule 7: Follow up.** Get permission to keep the interviewee's contact information so you can come back with more questions or with a prototype of your solution, and go deeper.

**Rule 8: Always open doors at the end.** Ask: "Who else should I talk to?" and "What else should I be looking at?" This can lead you in new and very interesting directions.

### Interview Observer Scorecard

**What it is**: A tool for an observer to classify each question the interviewer asks into one of four boxes, marking a tick per question:

| | Examples |
|-|-|
| **Asked for facts** | "When is the last time you...?", "Tell me about a time when you...?" |
| **Asked for opinions** | "Would you...?", "Do you think...?" |
| **Focused on "problem" (Customer Profile)** | "What are the things you struggle with?", "How do you measure success?" |
| **Focused on "solution" (Value Proposition)** | "Our solution offers...", "Would you buy our solution?" |

The aim is a scorecard weighted toward facts and problems.

### Exercises: create and review six interview questions

**Create**: Your Customer Profile is based on assumptions. Imagine talking to the actual customer to find evidence for the jobs, pains, and gains you mapped. With pen and paper, focus on your Customer Profile and write down six interview questions to learn more about their jobs, pains, and gains.

**Review** (about 5 minutes): Go back to your six questions. Classify each into one of the four scorecard categories. Ask: "How can I improve these questions so they focus more on facts and problems?"

### The Data Detective in depth: data sources

The Data Detective researches existing data inside and outside the company for a first understanding of jobs, pains, and gains.

**Inside the company**:

- **CRM system**: study how customers interact with existing value propositions and what jobs, pains, and gains appear there around existing products and services. Even for a completely new value proposition, this can be a wealth of ideas.
- **Data mining**: a traditional approach to understand, for example, shopping behavior; for digital products, how customers use existing services.
- **Website navigation**: how customers and prospects navigate your website shows what interests them and what they struggle with.
- **Previous research reports**: reports done with market research firms or by other departments. Even from a different context, they are a very cheap start.

**Outside the company**:

- **Social media analytics**: what customers deal with today and their opinions of value propositions on Twitter, Facebook, and similar. An incredibly interesting place to see what drives customers and what matters to them.
- **Google Trends**: compare trends to see which are rising and which falling. Example: comparing "strategic plan" with "business model canvas" to understand what executives are thinking about, and using that to develop better value propositions.
- **Google keywords**: what people search for shows the jobs, pains, and gains they care about. Find the search terms that matter in your area and those related to the new value propositions you want to develop.
- **Government data**: an enormous amount is available online; you can get lost in it, but it may be worth exploring.
- **External research reports**: a very cheap start compared with commissioning your own expensive study. Your own research for value propositions will be much more dynamic than these reports.

**Caveats**: Existing data is mainly a starting point; it may come from a very different context and may not be 100% related to the value propositions you are designing. You can also work with new data: experiments produce new data, which is where the business scientist meets the data detective.

### Identifying patterns in customer research

**What it is**: A simple wall-based method for finding patterns across research.

**How to do it**:

1. Find a wall. "The wall is the new desk": displaying everything lets you see the big picture.
2. Display all your research on it: a Customer Profile per interview, perhaps even full Value Proposition Canvases.
3. Study it for patterns, for example similar jobs, pains, and gains, possibly among very different customer segments.
4. Organize similar items into groups. This is a very different way of segmenting customers: by similarities in jobs, pains, and gains.
5. Synthesize the insights into new Customer Profiles; these become the basis for new value propositions.
6. Start prototyping new value propositions, remembering things might still change. Keep everything on the wall; it will stay useful.

### The risk of outsourcing customer research

**The temptation**: When developing new value propositions, you will need market research at some point. Why not hire the professionals, such as a market research agency, or ask the marketing department, since that's their job?

**The typical (risky) process**: Identify an opportunity, hire an agency, which talks to many customers, runs surveys, and returns with a big report of learnings; you refine the idea and launch it in the market or a pilot. Or: hire the research firm first, it finds the opportunities and learnings, and you develop the idea from its report. Often these ideas blow up in our face and we're left with a big failure.

**Why it fails**: The distance between the market research, the learnings, and the value proposition design.

**The better process**:

1. Prototype your idea; perhaps just think through jobs, pains, and gains.
2. Get out of the building immediately and test and learn with customers.
3. You'll probably be completely wrong, but because it was a very rough prototype, go back to the drawing board, decide, and change the prototype.
4. Get out of the building again; test and learn. You're probably still wrong early on, but you are the master and you make the decisions.
5. Repeat a few times until you feel you've got it right, fix the last details, then launch. You'll be much likelier to succeed.

**Rule**: Design, test, and redesign in rapid iterations. If you still work with a market research firm, be deeply involved; you can't completely outsource it, because you are the one who makes design decisions. It's your job to learn fast by failing early, often, and cheaply, and to continuously and rapidly act, decide, and improve the value proposition. An agency can't do that for you.

**Lessons**: Outsourced market studies distance you from the customer insights. A market research agency can't make design decisions for you. You need to iterate between prototyping, testing, and learning.

## 10. The Value Map

### Products and services

**What it is**: The heart of the Value Map (the left-hand side of the canvas): the bundle or list of products and services around which you build a specific value proposition for a specific customer segment. It is what customers hire to help them get one or more jobs done.

**Why it matters**: Products and services only create value in the context of customer jobs, pains, and gains. If those jobs, pains, and gains don't exist, your products and services may be irrelevant or obsolete, and there is no value creation.

**Example: citizenM (business traveler).** citizenM is a young hotel chain that started out in Amsterdam. Against the business traveler's Customer Profile, its products and services are:

- a hotel for "mobile citizens": travelers who stay one or two days in a city, such as party travelers or business travelers,
- online booking,
- extremely simple self-service check-in and check-out,
- instead of a restaurant on the premises, a high-quality self-service food bar open 24 hours a day, 7 days a week,
- high-speed Wi-Fi.

**Ranking by relevance**: Products and services have different relevance to customers. Ask, in the context of the Customer Profile, which are essential and which are merely nice to have, and order them. For the business traveler, move high-speed Wi-Fi higher up, because it is very important.

**The four types of products and services** (not mutually exclusive; they help you think through what you offer):

1. **Physical / tangible**: espresso in a restaurant or bar; cars from a producer or reseller.
2. **Intangible**: a technology license (the right to use a technology), or services such as medical advice or after-sales service.
3. **Digital**: a music download or music subscription (also intangible, showing the types overlap).
4. **Financial**: a loan, or financing for B2B industrial goods.

**Two ways to populate the field** (the same two directions apply to pain relievers and gain creators, below):

- **Driven from the left (products first)**: Start with a product or service idea and ask how it relieves pains and creates gains. Or, if you already have a set of products and services (a whole list of components), put them together into a new offering and map it back to the Customer Profile by describing how the bundle relieves pains and creates gains.
- **Driven from the right (customer first)**: Start with the Customer Profile. Ask which pains you will address (which tells you what pain relievers to create) and which gains you will address (which tells you what gain creators to build). Then ask what products and services you need to put together to deliver those. The same works when choosing from an existing portfolio: select pains and gains first, then bundle the products and services that deliver them.

### Pain relievers and gain creators

**Pain relievers: what they are.** A description of how specifically your products and services alleviate customer pains related to their jobs. In this field you highlight which specific pains you intend to eliminate or reduce, pains that prevent the customer from getting a job done well. The Customer Profile shows *all* customer pains; the pain relievers describe only how your products and services alleviate *some* of them.

**Gain creators: what they are.** A description of how explicitly your products and services create customer gains related to their jobs. Here you highlight which outcomes and benefits your products and services produce, because customers expect, desire, or would be surprised by them. The Customer Profile shows all gains customers expect, desire, or would love; the gain creators describe only how you address some of them and help customers materialize those gains.

**Why they matter**: Pain relievers and gain creators are the bridge between your products and services and the customer's pains and gains. They make explicit how you create value.

**Example: citizenM, mapped to the business traveler.**

- **Hotel room and online booking** help travelers get the gains they expect from staying somewhere overnight.
- **Location**: a quantified gain is that customers want to walk 0 to 15 minutes to their meetings. All citizenM hotels are well located, so business and party travelers don't have far to go.
- **Excellent price/quality ratio** addresses the pain of paying too much. Their target traveler cares about money but still wants a high-quality room.
- **Trendy feel**: travelers want to look good with clients even if they haven't paid much, so the hotel must feel trendy and not make them lose face. Note that there was no matching gain on the right-hand side; it's a judgment call whether to update the Customer Profile if you believe this addresses a real gain.
- **100% soundproof rooms** address the job of sleeping and getting rest, which noise, a real hotel pain, prevents.
- **Self-service check-in and friendly staff** get you through in an instant, addressing the big pain of long check-in and check-out; business travelers don't want to spend more than a couple of minutes waiting in line.
- **Around-the-clock, high-quality self-service food bar** addresses the desire for room service. citizenM doesn't deliver room service 100%, but customers can get good food and take it up to their room. You don't always have to deliver the expected gain 100%; you must address it in a way that still satisfies customers.
- **Free Wi-Fi** addresses both the pain of paying extra for Wi-Fi after paying for the room and the expected gain of high-speed Wi-Fi.

A great value proposition can't address all of a customer's jobs, pains, and gains; some remain unaddressed on the right-hand side.

**Ranking**: As with products and services, rank pain relievers and gain creators from essential to nice to have, in the context of the Customer Profile.

**Two directions**: Fill pain relievers and gain creators from the left (start from what you want to offer or from a portfolio bundle, then define how it relieves pains and creates gains) or from the right (start from which pains to relieve and gains to create, then design or select the products and services required).

### Case study: Tesla Model S value map

**Products and services**: the Model S with a choice of 60 or 85 kWh batteries, an eight-year battery warranty, and various options you can add.

**Addressing both pains and gains**:

- **Range**: depending on battery and driving behavior, 350 to 450 kilometers without charging.
- **Safety**: the highest safety rating of any car at the time (rated the safest car on the market by a government agency).

**Gain creators** (these target customers, even buying electric, really value performance, design, and brand):

- **Performance**: 0 to 100 km/h in 4.4 seconds with the fastest battery type. (Another lesson states "under three seconds"; the figures conflict and depend on the model variant, untested which holds.)
- **Design and style**: beautiful cars with a high-tech feel, including a 17-inch touchscreen in the cockpit that feels almost like an airplane.

**Pain relievers**:

- **Charging time** relatively fast: a battery charges at 45 to 90 kilometers (of range) per hour, so you regain independence quickly.
- **Space**: a large car with five seats for adults and two seats for children.
- **Supercharging network** for the less important but real job of long-distance trips: free charging that enables long-distance travel, with the battery half full in 30 minutes on a highway trip between cities.

### Value Map best practices (common mistakes)

1. **Listing all of your company's products and services.** A value proposition is designed for a particular segment; list only the products and services particularly relevant to this customer.
2. **Confusing products and services with pain relievers and gain creators.** Products and services are the *what*: concrete things customers use and interact with. Pain relievers and gain creators explain explicitly *how* you create value. Example: "accident" is a pain in the profile. You don't describe how Tesla addresses it in the products and services box; you do it in the pain relievers/gain creators, e.g. "rated the safest car on the market by a government agency."
3. **Adding pain relievers and gain creators unrelated to the Customer Profile.** This happens easily when describing an existing value proposition: you list all the benefits without checking how they connect to the profile's pains and gains. Only include those that address pains and gains in the profile.
4. **Trying to address all the profile's pains and gains.** No value proposition solves them all. Focus on the pains and gains most relevant to the customer.

### Pain Relievers trigger questions

Pain relievers describe how exactly your products and services alleviate specific customer pains: how you intend to eliminate or reduce some of the things that annoy customers before, during, or after trying to complete a job, or that prevent them from doing so. Ask: **Could your products and services...**

1. ...produce savings? In terms of time, money, or efforts.
2. ...make your customers feel better? By killing frustrations, annoyances, and other things that give customers a headache.
3. ...fix underperforming solutions? By introducing new features, better performance, or enhanced quality.
4. ...put an end to difficulties and challenges your customers encounter? By making things easier or eliminating obstacles.
5. ...wipe out negative social consequences your customers encounter or fear? In terms of loss of face or lost power, trust, or status.
6. ...eliminate risks your customers fear? In terms of financial, social, technical risks, or things that could potentially go wrong.
7. ...help your customers better sleep at night? By addressing significant issues, diminishing concerns, or eliminating worries.
8. ...limit or eradicate common mistakes customers make? By helping them use a solution the right way.
9. ...eliminate barriers that are keeping your customer from adopting value propositions? Introducing lower or no upfront investment costs, a flatter learning curve, or eliminating other obstacles preventing adoption.

### Gain Creators trigger questions

Gain creators describe how your products and services create customer gains: how you intend to produce outcomes and benefits that customers expect, desire, or would be surprised by, including functional utility, social gains, positive emotions, and cost savings. Ask: **Could your products and services...**

1. ...create savings that please your customers? In terms of time, money, and effort.
2. ...produce outcomes your customers expect or that exceed their expectations? By offering quality levels, more of something, or less of something.
3. ...outperform current value propositions and delight your customers? Regarding specific features, performance, or quality.
4. ...make your customers' work or life easier? Via better usability, accessibility, more services, or lower cost of ownership.
5. ...create positive social consequences? By making them look good or producing an increase in power or status.
6. ...do something specific that customers are looking for? In terms of good design, guarantees, or specific or more features.
7. ...fulfill a desire customers dream about? By helping them achieve their aspirations or getting relief from a hardship?
8. ...produce positive outcomes matching your customers' success and failure criteria? In terms of better performance or lower cost.
9. ...help make adoption easier? Through lower cost, fewer investments, lower risk, better quality, improved performance, or better design.

### Exercise: complete your Value Map (about 10 minutes)

1. Retrieve your Customer Profile.
2. Place a Value Map on its left-hand side.
3. Select a bundle of products and services targeted at your segment, and list them first.
4. Make explicit how those products and services relieve pains and create gains, using the pain reliever and gain creator trigger questions if needed.

## 11. Fit Between the Customer Profile and the Value Map

### Observe, understand, design

A great value proposition starts with a deep understanding of customers. Customer centricity means putting yourself in the customer's shoes, and the canvas helps you do it. On the right-hand side you **observe** and try to **understand** the customer, making explicit their jobs, pains, and gains as assumptions and hypotheses. On the left-hand side you **design** the value proposition (products and features, pain relievers, gain creators) and try to fit it to the profile. Right side: observe. Left side: design.

### What fit is

You have fit when there is a clear connection between the Value Map and the Customer Profile: when your products and services address jobs, pains, and gains that really matter to customers, relieving pains and creating gains. There is a clear bridge between gain creators and gains, and between pain relievers and pains. Your value proposition will not address all the customer's jobs, pains, and gains.

### The three types of fit

1. **Problem-solution fit**: you have evidence that customers care about certain jobs, pains, and gains (you understand the problem; you have validated the right-hand side, **the circle**), and you have prototyped, on paper, a value proposition that addresses them. You don't yet have proof it will fly with customers, but you have a working prototype.
2. **Product-market fit**: your value proposition gets traction in the market. You have evidence your products and services address real pains and gains customers actually care about. You have validated the left-hand side, **the square**.
3. **Business model fit**: a great value proposition isn't enough if it doesn't make more money than it costs to bring to market. You have business model fit when you have evidence you can embed the value proposition in a profitable and scalable business model (**the rectangle**). This also means having fit for *all* value propositions the business model requires. In a double-sided platform like Google, with users and advertisers, you need fit with both segments and with the business model.

It's like climbing up a building: problem-solution fit, then product-market fit, then business model fit. You must go through all three stages to create a value proposition that is profitable and scalable and makes money.

### Fit examples

**Tesla Model S.** Tesla, a Silicon Valley startup, probably created the first electric vehicle to go mass market. Segment: middle-class men with incomes of $100,000 and more. Job: personal mobility. Pains (kept simple): fear of not being able to drive far enough (range), fear of not being able to charge fast enough. Gains: not just an electric vehicle, but one that looks good, has great design, and performs almost like a sports car. Fit: the pain relievers are adequate (a decent range, just enough so customers don't get scared), but the real strength is in the gain creators: 0 to 100 km/h in under three seconds (see the conflicting 4.4-second figure in Section 10) and a car that looks really good.

**Google search ads (AdWords).** How it works: an advertiser buys a search term and their ad appears when people search for it. Segment: advertisers. Job: promote products and services. Pain: wasting marketing money. Gain: ads that appear only for targeted segments. Pain reliever: advertisers pay only for ads that are clicked; if an ad shows and nobody clicks, they pay nothing. Gain creator: ads appear in a very targeted context (e.g. only a certain geographic region or customer type), and because people are actively searching, they are much likelier to buy, so the ads perform very well.

**Hilti fleet management.** Hilti makes light machine tools (e.g. drills) for builders on construction sites. Facing growing low-cost manufacturing competition, it targeted construction company CEOs with a new value proposition: fleet management.

- Jobs: finding and executing contracts; above all, keeping construction schedules.
- Pains: penalty costs for delays; putting upfront money into tools (CEOs are not interested in investing in tools).
- Gains: profitable construction work; access to the newest tools, for performance and safety.
- Pain relievers: no investment in tools, just a monthly subscription for access to a fleet; Hilti guarantees the right tools are at the right site at the right time, reducing downtime and the fear of penalties.
- Gain creators: much easier cost prediction; access to a very modern fleet.

The fit helped Hilti become competitive again against low-cost manufacturing countries.

### Quantifying how customers measure success and failure

**What it is**: Going beyond qualitative fit by understanding, in numbers, how customers measure pains (failure) and gains (success).

**Why it works**: If you can make value quantitative, you can much better measure whether your products and services create value. The better you understand the customer's metric of success, the better you can show how much value you create.

**How to do it**:

1. For your target segment's jobs, ask what their pains and gains mean in dollar terms, percentages, or time.
2. On the Value Map, quantify how your products and services eliminate, decrease, reduce, or minimize pains: reducing cost, reducing price, minimizing waste in percentages, saving time.
3. Quantify how your gain creators create, increase, improve, or maximize gains in dollar value, percentages, or time.

**Example 1: credit card fraud technology.** Customers are credit card issuing institutions. Their pain is fraud, and they know well what percentage of transactions are fraudulent and how much money they lose. You can quantify how your fraud detection reduces that pain in percentages or dollars.

**Example 2: conversion rate optimization.** You help web merchants turn more website visitors into paying customers, i.e. earn more revenue. You can quantify, in percentage points, how much you improve their conversion rate, and so how much more money they make.

### Exercise: map the fit

1. Retrieve your Customer Profile and Value Map.
2. Draw a circle around one gain creator or pain reliever on the Value Map.
3. Circle each job, pain, or gain on the Customer Profile that it addresses.
4. Repeat until all gain creators and pain relievers are circled together with what they address.

You should normally still have uncircled jobs, pains, and gains, since no value proposition can address them all. The greatest value propositions address only a limited number of a customer's important jobs, pains, and gains, but do so extremely well.

### Same customer, different contexts

**What it is**: The same customer can have a very different Customer Profile in different contexts, because context changes jobs, pains, gains, and constraints.

**Example 1: different contexts.** A businessperson at a business lunch versus the same person on a romantic dinner date, even at the same restaurant. At the business lunch, cost may be an important pain. On the dinner date, cost is probably not the most important thing, and a different gain matters: that the dinner is particularly romantic.

**Example 2: different constraints.** Communicating on the go while driving: a big pain is having to take your eyes off the road. The same customer with the same job at home, calling with the family around, faces a different constraint: background noise.

**Lessons**: The same customer can have a different profile in different contexts, and some contexts create very different constraints that affect jobs, pains, and gains.

## 12. Business Model and Value Proposition Fit

### What a business model is: the Business Model Canvas

An organization's business model can be described with nine building blocks. Listing them isn't enough; map them on a pre-structured canvas, the Business Model Canvas, a tool to map, discuss, design, and invent business models. It works for startup entrepreneurs as well as the most senior executives, and lets you map an entire business model in one image.

1. **Customer Segments**: all the people or organizations for which you create value, including simple users and paying customers.
2. **Value Propositions**: for each segment, the bundle of products and services that creates value for those customers.
3. **Channels**: the touch points through which you reach and interact with customers.
4. **Customer Relationships**: the type of relationship you establish with each segment.
5. **Revenue Streams**: how, and through which pricing mechanisms, the business model captures value.
6. **Key Resources**: the assets indispensable to the business model.
7. **Key Activities**: the things you really need to perform well.
8. **Key Partners**: who helps you leverage the business model, since you won't own all key resources or perform all key activities yourself.
9. **Cost Structure**: the costs that follow once you understand the business model's infrastructure (resources, activities, partners).

Blocks 6 to 8 are the infrastructure (the "backstage") to create, deliver, and capture value; the customer-facing blocks are the "front stage". The printed canvas also carries fields for Designed for, Designed by, Date, and Version.

### Create value for the customer and for the business

A great value proposition lives in a sustainable, profitable, scalable business model. It isn't enough to focus on the value proposition alone, however innovative. To create value for your customers you need to create value for your business model, and to create value for your business model you need to create value for customers, because without customers there is no value for the business. Embedding a value proposition in a business model means, for example, finding the right channel to reach customers, having profitable revenue streams, perhaps acquiring new resources, or forming strategic partnerships.

### Going back and forth between business model and value proposition

**What it is**: Zooming out from the value proposition to ask "How do I deliver this profitably?", and zooming back in to change the value proposition when the answer is "I can't", repeating until both work.

**Why it matters**: The Value Proposition Canvas describes how you create value for the customer, but a company only succeeds with a profitable and scalable business model. Changing a value proposition can reshape the whole business model.

**How to do it**:

1. Map the current business model.
2. Understand the customer's real jobs, pains, and gains, and design a new value proposition.
3. Zoom out: overlay the new value proposition on the old business model (like tracing paper) and ask which blocks it changes: channels, relationships, revenue streams, and especially the backstage (activities, resources, costs).
4. Redesign the business model; if it can't work, zoom back into the value proposition and adjust it.

**Example: Hilti in full.**

*Original business model*: High-end light machine tools for builders on construction sites. A sales force and certain stores, giving a one-to-one direct relationship. Revenue from transactional tool sales. Backstage: manufacturing, selling, and branding (a strong brand name as a resource), manufacturing partners; these resources, activities, and partners drive the main costs.

*The problem*: low-cost manufacturing; Hilti was no longer competitive.

*The customer insight*: The customer's main job wasn't buying tools; it was managing, maintaining, and replacing them: having the right tool at the right place at the right time. Hilti shifted focus from construction workers to construction company CEOs.

*CEO profile*: Jobs: finding and executing contracts, respecting deadlines, which means fleet planning and scheduling to avoid delays. Pains: upfront investment in tools, broken tools, theft, and delays that lead to penalties and stress. Gains: profitable contracts, access to the newest tools, safety, uptime, and predictable costs.

*The value proposition, as pitched*: "Have you ever wanted to just focus on your work and forget about administrative issues and managing your tool fleet? If so, Tool Fleet Management, Hilti's long-term tool usage service, is for you. We take care of your tools while you focus on the job. Hilti helps assure that your team will have the best tools, each unique and labeled using RFID and an inventory number so you can maintain control of your tool fleet. If something breaks down, no problem. Hilti has another way to make your work easier. We repair or replace your tools quickly at no additional cost. Hilti will also update your tools on a regular basis, always with the latest generation models. All this on a single monthly invoice."

*Mapped*: Pain relievers: no more repair and replacement costs; a subscription, so no upfront investment. Gain creators: access to a great fleet of tools; sound cost management. A great fit.

*Impact on the business model*:

- Value proposition: an online fleet management system for construction company CEOs.
- Channels: salespeople who used to sell to construction workers had to be trained to sell to CEOs; the online fleet management system is itself a new channel.
- Revenue: from transactional sales to large, long-term contracts.
- Key activities: in addition to manufacturing, managing a service fleet, a very different business.
- Key resources: Hilti no longer sells the tools but manages them for customers, so the inventory stays on Hilti's balance sheet.
- Costs: the new service component has a huge impact on the cost structure.

Hilti became a service-centric company, and fleet management became a substantial part of its business. It is not as simple as changing a value proposition.

**Lessons**: Zoom out from the value proposition to find the business model that makes it work; zoom in from the business model to find the detailed value proposition; go back and forth until you get it right.

### Case study: SunEdison, searching for the right business model

This case illustrates the search for the right business model through back and forth between the Value Proposition Canvas and the Business Model Canvas.

*Background*: Jigar Shah created and launched SunEdison in 2003. In less than two years it became the largest solar provider in the United States.

*Starting question*: Where is solar energy particularly useful? Answer: shopping centers, shopping malls, and big retailers, because their cold-storage requirements mean high energy bills, the electricity is mainly used between noon and four or five p.m. (when the sun shines and grid electricity is most expensive), and they have large rooftops.

**Value proposition 1: to shopping centers.**

*Customer Profile (shopping center executives)*:

- Jobs: running a shopping center business; managing power and other technical facilities.
- Pains: volatility of energy prices; fear of power outages; dealing with non-core activities (they aren't engineers; they are oriented toward their customers); and the main pain, upfront investment in power or technical facilities.
- Gains: attractive energy prices; above all, predictable energy costs; and responding to the green movement (in the 2000s, regulation asked them to avoid carbon emissions).

*Value Map: the Power Purchase Agreement (PPA)*:

- Solar panels installed on the rooftop for free, in exchange for a long-term contract (subscription) called a Power Purchase Agreement.
- Green energy (addresses the green gain).
- No capital outlay; SunEdison provides the financing (addresses the upfront-investment pain).
- A fixed price for ten years thanks to the long-term contract, making costs predictable; the long-term contract also allows competitive energy pricing.
- Installation and maintenance included: a turnkey solution, so they don't deal with non-core activities.
- Strong monitoring.

*Zooming out to the business model*:

- Channel: a sales force to reach shopping centers.
- Customer relationship: long-term, because of the long-term contract.
- Revenue streams: regular PPA payments, i.e. recurring revenue.
- Key activities: acquiring PPAs (acquiring customers); installing panels. SunEdison never manufactured panels itself.
- Key partners: strategic contracts with panel manufacturers.
- Key resources: patents; talented engineers and solar experts; and SunEdison owns the solar installations.
- Costs: customer acquisition cost, and the investment in the installations, which are free for the shopping centers. Financing the installations is a big issue.

**Value proposition 2: to investors.** To solve financing, Jigar Shah explored whether investors might be interested in something SunEdison could offer.

*Customer Profile (investors)*:

- Jobs: increasing their wealth; for some, a less functional job of satisfying their green or ecological conscience.
- Pains: huge losses, too much risk, and volatility of returns.
- Gains: returns better than the market; doing some good while keeping peace of mind.

*Value Map: the "green investment return"*: a green investment vehicle built on the PPAs (the long-term contracts with shopping malls), i.e. a green portfolio with guaranteed returns from profitable electricity sales to the malls (relieves risk and volatility pains); attractive returns, plus tax refunds, since some states or countries refund solar energy investments (gain creators).

*Zooming out again*:

- Channels: financial institutions to reach investors, not the technical sales force.
- Revenue: investment fees from investors; and the revenue model from shopping centers changed to development and monitoring fees.
- Key activities: a new activity of packaging and selling PPAs.
- Key resources: the pool of PPAs (long-term contracts).
- Costs: investor acquisition costs.

*Result*: SunEdison became a double-sided platform between two customer types, shopping centers and investors, and needs both to succeed.

### Multiple fits

**What it is**: You have multiple fits when your business model needs several value propositions to succeed.

**Situation 1: multi-sided platforms. Example: Airbnb.** The website connects travelers from around the world with people who want to rent out their flat or house for a few days. Travelers need a value proposition to find a place; hosts need a value proposition to list their home. Airbnb only succeeds with a great value proposition for both.

**Situation 2: intermediaries. Example: Haier**, the Chinese home appliance maker. It has a clear value proposition to consumers, but reaches them through retail, so it also needs a clear value proposition for retailers (its direct customers) to get them to stock Haier appliances. It needs both to succeed.

**Situation 3: business-to-business.** Selling to a company really means selling to different stakeholders within it, each needing their own value proposition: end users, influencers, recommenders, economic buyers, decision makers, and perhaps saboteurs who could undermine a purchase (see Section 3).

**Lessons**: A business model may require several value propositions to succeed. Multiple fits arise in multi-sided platforms, intermediary models, and B2B.

## 13. Value and Differentiation

### Key value drivers: vitamins, headache pills, or both

**What it is**: A value driver is a strong value-creating theme in your value proposition. Drivers focused on gain creation are like **vitamins**: they boost value. Drivers focused on pain relief are like **headache pills**: they alleviate pains. Some do both at once.

#### Vitamins (strong gain creation)

Associated verbs: enable, increase, improve, progress, raise, grow.

- **Performance.** Example: game consoles, where graphic performance is a very important selection factor. Caveat: performance matters only up to a point; beyond it you can no longer differentiate, and what matters can change quickly. The Nintendo Wii did not perform well on graphics because it competed on very different gain creators and pain relievers.
- **Newness.** Example: Apple; many people buy new iPhones simply because they are new, even though they usually don't need the newest model. Apple created strong demand, making newness a competitive advantage. Caveat: newness matters only to the extent customers really care about a new versus old device.
- **Brand and status.** Example: Swiss watches such as Rolex. Nobody buys a Rolex to read the time; it conveys status and success.
- **Customization.** Example: My M&M's, which let you personalize M&M's. Mass customization has made this driver accessible even to the largest mass manufacturers.

#### Headache pills (strong pain relief)

Associated verbs: disable, decrease, save, eliminate, stop.

- **Low cost** (when cost or price is the pain). Example: easyJet, a low-cost point-to-point airline; the pain reliever is simply the cheap price. How the business model makes the price possible is a different story.
- **Reducing cost** (different from price). Example: salesforce.com, the first company to really push software as a service, starting with customer relationship management. The software ran on Salesforce's infrastructure, not yours, so no IT infrastructure or installation was required in-house. What mattered was not just the software price but the low cost of ownership.
- **Simplicity.** Example: complicated remote controls for TVs, satellite, and other media devices; making things simple to use is a strong pain reliever.
- **Risk reduction.** Example: Box, an enterprise file storage solution with very strong security, often better than enterprises have in-house, reducing IT infrastructure risk for file storage.

#### Both at once (vitamins and painkillers)

These create a lot of customer value because they relieve pains and create gains simultaneously.

- **Uber**: a car service activated by a mobile app so simple that one button press brings a car to pick you up (pain relief), with often many more cars available in a city than other taxi services (gain). It gets the job done better than the previous solutions.
- **Amazon Web Services**: storage and computing power over the Internet made high-end IT infrastructure accessible to anybody, including small and medium companies. It frees you from owning infrastructure (like salesforce.com) and gives even a small company or a single developer access to world-class infrastructure, the same Amazon uses for its e-commerce.
- **Design**: example, a Braun radio designed by Dieter Rams. Well-designed devices are aesthetic and simple at the same time, so great design relieves pains and creates gains simultaneously. (The example may remind you of another company usually cited for great design.)

**Lessons**: Value propositions can have strong value-creating themes (value drivers). Gain-focused drivers are vitamins; pain-focused drivers are headache pills. The greatest value propositions do both.

### The 10 characteristics of great value propositions

Use this as a checklist to assess existing value propositions or those you intend to design (for example, hang it up as a poster in a workshop or office). Great value propositions:

1. **Are embedded in great business models.** Kodak helped invent the digital camera, had a great value proposition for it, and sold many digital cameras, but went out of business because it didn't have a great business model.
2. **Focus on the jobs, pains, and gains that matter most to customers** (the most significant jobs, most severe pains, and most relevant gains). This requires truly understanding customer priorities: ranking jobs, pains, and gains from the customer's perspective, then getting out of the building to test the list.
3. **Focus on unsatisfied jobs, unresolved pains, and unrealized gains.** Some jobs, pains, and gains that matter most may already be covered by other companies' value propositions. Find where the dissatisfaction is and where the most potential lies.
4. **Target few jobs, pains, and gains (few pain relievers and gain creators), but do so extremely well.** You can't address all of them. Great companies focus on a few things done in a way that blows the customer away: an extremely low price, great design, an unbelievable customer experience. Focus where your value proposition creates the most value.
5. **Go beyond functional jobs and address emotional and social jobs as well** (all together). The iPod's white earphones addressed a social job in addition to the functional job of listening to music. This applies equally in B2B: people don't decide purely on functional jobs.
6. **Align with how customers measure success and failure.** Deeply understand how customers measure a job done well and what a failed job means to them.
7. **Focus on jobs, pains, and gains that a lot of people have, or that some will pay a lot of money for.** Focusing on the most lucrative jobs helps you build a profitable business model.
8. **Differentiate from competition on jobs, pains, and gains customers really care about** (differentiate in a meaningful way). Don't differentiate on things that don't matter to customers; understand their number one priorities and differentiate there.
9. **Outperform competition substantially on at least one dimension.** Not 10% better: 10x. The iPod let users store far more music on their digital player than any other solution at the time.
10. **Are difficult to copy.** Example: Amazon Web Services; it is very hard for companies like IBM or HP to compete on AWS's convenience, price, and reliability, even though they are great companies.

### Exercise: assess your value proposition

1. Take the 10 questions (the checklist above).
2. With pen and paper, select the value proposition you have been working on.
3. For each question, score the value proposition from 0 (poorly done) to 10 (brilliantly done).
4. As you add up the numbers, think about ways to improve the score.

### Comparing your value proposition with competitors (Strategy Canvas)

**What it is**: A visual comparison of your value proposition against competitors' using the Strategy Canvas from *Blue Ocean Strategy* by Kim and Mauborgne, with your pain relievers and gain creators as the factors of competition. It is a powerful combination of value proposition design tools and Blue Ocean tools.

**How to do it**:

1. **Set up**: On a flip chart, whiteboard, or large sheet of paper, draw an x-axis for the factors of competition and a y-axis for performance, with a scale from 0 to 10. Retrieve your Value Proposition Canvas and focus on the Value Map.
2. **Select the factors of competition**: pick pain relievers and gain creators from your value proposition and place them on the x-axis.
3. **Score your value proposition**: go through each factor and ask whether you perform well or badly on it. A high score sits high on the y-axis, a low one at the bottom. Mark high/low or give a score (the lesson mentions 1 to 10; the worksheet uses 0 to 10).
4. **Select competing value propositions**: choose those most representative of the competition. Don't only choose look-alikes; include propositions that compete with you but look very different. If you are a book author, compete not only with other books but with YouTube, workshops, and so on. A European airline competes with high-speed trains.
5. **Score the competitors** on the same factors, which produces a clear visual comparison.
6. **Add competitors' factors**: also consider some pain relievers and gain creators from competitors, since they may focus on things you don't.
7. **Analyze (your sweet spot)**: go factor by factor, then look at the big picture. How does your line look overall? Is your value proposition very similar to competitors' or very different? Analyze the curves, uncover opportunities, and ask whether and how you differentiate.

## 14. Design Attitude and Prototyping Value Propositions

### The design attitude

**What it is**: Treating your first idea as a starting point to be thrown away, and deliberately exploring several directions before choosing.

**Why it works**: Very successful business models such as Amazon.com, Google, and Netflix did not copy existing models; they invented and designed new business models and value propositions. Today, people put a premium on the first idea and then focus on execution. But your first idea doesn't matter that much, because it is going to change. Corporations and executives are trained to decide: they are good decision makers, not necessarily good designers, and creating new business models and value propositions requires being designers. Much of what happens in organizations is planning, and planning is about what *should* happen rather than actually doing something.

**How to do it**:

1. Accept that your first idea will change and must evolve.
2. Take your initial value proposition, throw it away, and come up with two others. They will be better than the one you threw away.
3. Let go and try different things; explore as much as you can, rapidly, without losing time.
4. Generate alternatives, then choose the best one among them.
5. Try this: take the most exciting idea you've had in the past few days, make a 30-second sketch, and show it to a colleague. Something magic will happen.

### Exercise: the bicycle exercise (alternative directions from one starting point)

1. Your starting point is a bicycle and €1,000. With pen and paper, come up with 10 different value proposition ideas (e.g. a bike rental service for your neighborhood). No need to map a canvas; just write down 10 ideas.
2. Select three ideas and make them more tangible by sketching an advertising poster for each, containing a title/headline, an image, and a slogan.
3. For each poster, write three reasons why it's a brilliant idea and three reasons why it's a silly idea.

Design thinking processes like this help create alternative directions to improve an existing value proposition or create a new one.

### The 10 prototyping principles

Unlock the power of prototyping. Resist spending time and energy refining one direction only; use these principles to explore multiple directions with the same time and energy. You will learn more and discover better value propositions.

1. Make it visual and tangible.
2. Embrace a beginner's mind.
3. Don't fall in love with first ideas: create alternatives.
4. Feel comfortable in a "liquid state".
5. Start with low fidelity, iterate, and refine.
6. Expose your work early: seek criticism.
7. Learn faster by failing early, often, and cheaply.
8. Use creativity techniques.
9. Create "Shrek models".
10. Track learnings, insights, and progress.

### Six ways to innovate from customer jobs

**What it is**: Six trigger questions to spark your next value proposition move once you've mapped the Customer Profile. **Can you...**

1. **Address more jobs?** Address a more complete set of jobs, including related and ancillary jobs, more than you have so far or more than any other value proposition. Example: iPod and iTunes went beyond listening to music to downloading, buying, copying, and transferring music onto the device, then listening: a much larger set of jobs than anyone had focused on. Caveat: sometimes addressing more jobs makes the solution impractical.
2. **Switch to a more important job?** Move from a basic or long-established job to one that is much more important to the customer, a job different from what most value propositions currently focus on. Example: for decades builders bought Hilti tools, but Hilti realized construction companies have a much more important job: getting the right tool to the right place at the right time, with no delays and no penalties. Hilti Fleet Management ensured customers always had the right tools in the right place, and helped with sound cost management.
3. **Go beyond functional jobs?** Keep serving functional jobs, but create new value by fulfilling important social and emotional jobs. Examples: the Mini Cooper and Tesla went far beyond functional jobs.
4. **Help a lot more customers get a job done?** Make a job that was complex, difficult, or inaccessible because of price accessible to a much larger audience. Example: Amazon Web Services gives access to the world's best infrastructure through a very simple application programming interface; before it, only very wealthy, very large companies had such infrastructure.
5. **Get a job done incrementally better?** Help customers do a job better through a large series of small (micro-)improvements to an existing value proposition. Example: the Bosch circular saw, whose many small improvements produced a much better-performing saw that succeeded in the market.
6. **Help a customer get a job done radically better?** A new value proposition that dramatically outperforms older ways of getting the job done: the stuff of new market creation. Example: the graphical user interface. The first commercial one was the Xerox Star, but the Macintosh was the first mass-produced computer with a GUI and conquered the world through its simplicity.

**Exercise: six ways to innovate from your customer jobs**

1. Take the Six Ways worksheet and your Value Proposition Canvas, focusing on the jobs in the Customer Profile.
2. For each customer job, go through the six questions and brainstorm alternatives; capture your ideas.
3. Ask: "What impact will my new jobs-to-be-done have on my value proposition?"

### Exercise: brainstorm alternatives to improve the fit, and pitch the best idea

1. **Identify the gaps** in your canvas: revisit the fit, Strategy Canvas, and six-ways exercises. Where could you improve your value proposition?
2. **Brainstorm alternatives**: in five minutes, generate as many ideas as possible to address the gaps, with a quick napkin sketch for each on a sticky note.
3. **Select your best idea and improve the fit**: your updated canvas should show how you improved fit between Customer Profile and Value Map, and which important jobs, pains, and/or gains the new idea addresses.
4. **Sketch an advertising poster** of your best idea with a title/headline and a visual, plus a sentence explaining which important customer job, pain, and gain it addresses.

## 15. Running a Value Proposition Design Workshop

### Defining objectives and design constraints: invent versus improve

**What it is**: Before designing a workshop, clarify whether you are on the **invent** or the **improve** end of the value proposition design spectrum. Objectives, constraints, activities, and mindset differ completely.

**Invent (left side)**: Design new value propositions regardless of existing value propositions or business models; far fewer constraints. Focus here when you want to make a proactive bet on the future, are fighting a crisis, are creating value propositions for new technologies, or are fighting off an insurgent disrupting your space. Activities: searching for the right value proposition, then testing, validating, and evaluating ideas in the market.

**Improve (right side)**: Improve existing value propositions within the context of other value propositions and an existing business model you won't change; many more constraints. Focus here when you want to renew yourself and regain lost fit, need to work on profits or costs, want to maintain growth, or have customer complaints and need to improve. Activities: assessing existing value, then planning and executing improvements.

**Invent workshops in context**: The workshop is only the very beginning of a long, messy process. You've just started the search; execution is far away. Uncertainty is very large, and your main task afterward is to reduce the risk of failure of the ideas. It will probably be the first of a series of workshops, with testing, spending, and sharing results in between, until a workshop decides to move toward implementation.

*Suggested invent workshop structure* (adapt to your objectives):

1. Set the scene: why are we doing this? To create new growth engines? To fight off a disruptor?
2. Train people in value proposition design tools, if needed.
3. The core: idea generation and iterative prototyping.
4. Select an idea.
5. Admit this is only the beginning: extract hypotheses and prepare the testing phase in the market.

**Improve workshops in context**: You've already gone from idea to business and are improving a value proposition that has succeeded in the market. The search phase is much shorter because you have much more customer knowledge. Once you know how to improve, you mainly focus on execution. One workshop might be enough.

*Suggested improve workshop structure*:

1. Set the scene: why must the existing value proposition improve?
2. Training, if needed.
3. The main part: map the existing value proposition, gather customer knowledge, assess strengths and weaknesses.
4. Ideation: how could we improve it?
5. Select an idea.
6. Plan implementation. You likely need to spend less energy testing than with a completely new value proposition in a new market.

**Lesson**: Define which side of the spectrum you're on before designing the workshop structure. Invent focuses on search, testing, and evaluation; improve focuses on assessment, then planning and execution.

### Facilitation ground rules

**The facilitator's role**: A great facilitator guarantees a great workshop, and facilitation starts long before the workshop, with designing its structure.

- **Design the workshop structure**: use tools and processes in a way that guarantees outcomes. An agenda isn't enough; think through the structure and how each tool helps produce the results you want.
- **Facilitate** the conversations, prototyping, feedback, and synthesis to get to results.
- **Lead the group toward results**, keeping everyone on track toward the goal. This can mean cutting off unproductive conversations, even important ones; if they don't relate to the workshop goals, they happen after the workshop.

**Characteristics of a great facilitator**:

- Tool- and process-driven but not dogmatic: adapts to where the workshop goes and brings in new tools and processes when needed.
- A great listener and observer: reads where the group's energy is (frustration, a sense of not advancing, excitement) and whether everyone is working toward results.
- Able to lead and shape a conversation, but uses the group's words and doesn't impose their own opinion.

**Blockers to manage**:

- A poor workshop structure: if it wasn't thought through, you're unlikely to reach results effectively.
- People fighting to get their idea through, and refining ideas far too early at the expense of designing and exploring alternatives.
- Losing time.
- People getting off track; the facilitator keeps everyone on track and on time and ensures every conversation contributes to the objectives.
- **"Blah blah blah"**, one of the biggest blockers: long, unstructured discussions without tools, processes, or facilitation. They jump wildly between topics or dive into implementation before it's time, go in circles, and don't lead to actionable outcomes. Avoiding them is the job of both the facilitator and the workshop structure.

**Example of a feedback structure: Edward de Bono's thinking hats.** When a team presents a Value Proposition Canvas, instead of a long conversation:

1. Everyone (metaphorically) puts on the **white hat** and asks questions of clarification: what didn't we understand?
2. Everyone puts on the **black hat** and argues why the idea won't work. This is gold for the presenting team: every argument is something to integrate into the next prototype. The team must not respond or defend; they just take it as input.
3. Everyone puts on the **yellow hat** and argues why it's a great idea, again constructive input.
4. Everyone puts on the **green hat** and shares ideas for improving the canvas.

In five to seven minutes, you produce the most valuable feedback and the team can improve its canvas.

**Enablers to put in place**:

- A safe space with a shared mindset: explore and experiment before making decisions.
- Crystal-clear ideas, which is where visual tools help.
- A strong workshop structure.
- Strict timekeeping: project a clock on the wall so everyone sees how much time is left for each exercise.

**Ground rules**:

1. Make everything tangible and visual with the tools: sticky notes, Value Proposition Canvases, Business Model Canvases, whiteboards and flip charts as needed.
2. One person speaks at a time; no side conversations; everyone focuses on one topic at a time.
3. Stay on topic and work toward the workshop objective.
4. Evidence trumps opinion: proof from the market or experiments beats pure opinion; distinguish what you know from evidence from what you believe.

**Basics of working with visual tools**:

- The wall is the new desk: don't sit around a table; put up canvas posters and use flip charts.
- One element per sticky note, no bullet points, because you may need to prioritize jobs, pains, and gains and can't do that with lists on one note.
- Use large markers and capital letters so notes are readable from the back of the room. Have the person with the nicest handwriting write, so you can simply photograph the result.
- Use color coding (e.g. existing versus desired jobs, or jobs versus pains versus gains) only when it makes sense, because the brain always tries to read meaning into colors.

**Lessons**: Workshop success depends heavily on the selected tools and processes and on how the facilitator guides people through them; a facilitator can make or break a workshop. Even if you're not a great facilitator, following these rules produces good outputs. Get buy-in for the facilitation rules (you may edit them), with everyone agreeing to use them together.

### People, materials, and room set-up

**Ideal skill set in the room**:

- at least one person highly skilled in the value proposition tools,
- someone with entrepreneurial spirit and drive to push the workshop and project forward,
- design thinking skills (rough and conceptual prototypes, exploring alternatives),
- customer empathy or customer knowledge,
- experimentation and testing skills, because value propositions must be tested in the market to prove customers care.

**Who to invite** (based on your objectives):

- product-centric people: product managers, engineers, designers,
- market-oriented people: marketers, salespeople, customer-facing teams such as support,
- people you need buy-in from: decision makers, operations, production, finance, and anyone you'll need for implementation,
- possibly customers, who can be very valuable as co-creators,
- project sponsors, for the whole workshop or at least parts, so they understand how you do value proposition design.

**Venue** (often underestimated):

- A large room: about 50 square meters per 10 people. You need space to sit and listen during presentations and plenary discussions, and separate space for workstations on the wall (several Customer Profiles, value propositions, perhaps a Business Model Canvas).
- If you spend more than an hour together, consider an interesting venue; it helps creativity.

**Materials**:

- Value Proposition Canvases printed in B1 format (100 cm x 70 cm), a poster size suited to teams of five to seven people.
- Sticky notes of about 10 x 7 cm for that format, possibly in different colors to distinguish jobs, pains, and gains.
- Optionally, the Customer Profile and Value Map printed separately, so you can sketch several Customer Profiles and try alternative value proposition prototypes on different Value Maps.
- A Business Model Canvas in B0 format (about 141 cm x 100 cm), with larger sticky notes of about 20 x 10 cm.
- Markers, so people write in capital letters and sketch images on sticky notes.
- A camera to photograph outputs, or a digital tool to capture them during or after the workshop.
- For first-timers: the trigger questions for the canvases and tools.

### The end is the beginning: starting validation

**What it is**: A reminder that even the best workshop is only the start.

**Why it matters**: The better the workshop (outstanding ideas, high energy), the higher the risk you'll believe its ideas will work, which makes great workshops your biggest risk of failure. The journey from great ideas to a value proposition that succeeds in the market is long and messy, with two phases: **search** (figuring out what customers really want, their jobs, pains, and gains, and a value proposition that fits) and then **execute**.

**How to act on it**: Accept that after the first workshop uncertainty is at its highest; you don't know what customers really want, even if some customers were in the room. Your biggest task as an innovator or intrapreneur is to reduce the risk of failure and prove your ideas can work, by gradually testing and spending more over time. Expect your ideas to change as you test them and turn them into real value propositions.

## 16. Introduction to Testing

### Why businesses fail, and how to reduce risk and uncertainty

**The evidence**: Almost 40% of companies that start out build something nobody wants; they start with a broken value proposition. A second statistic shows the same trend: 42% of companies that fail do so because they focus on something with no market need, a value proposition that doesn't create value for the customer. These statistics come from the startup world, but they are probably a very good proxy for new initiatives in corporations.

**The mindset**: Admit that turning an idea into a real business, with customers standing at the door, is difficult and messy. It has two phases: **search** (what value proposition really creates value, with which business model?) and, once found, **execute**. At the start, we really don't know what will work: uncertainty is at its highest, so risk is at its highest. It is the innovator's responsibility to reduce both.

**How to do it**: Spend money only gradually while testing and learning whether the value proposition could work.

1. Start with a brainstorming session.
2. Immediately invest a little in talking to customers.
3. Perhaps run a survey or build first prototypes.
4. Only then move to something more substantial, such as a pilot.
5. Once you have proof that it could work and customers are interested, and uncertainty is sufficiently reduced, move to execution and invest more.

This requires changing behavior: test with small budgets and learn.

**Caveat on pilots**: Pilots in corporations today are rarely real experiments anymore; we expect them to work. Run cheaper customer experiments well before launching a first pilot.

### Technology risk versus market risk

**Definitions**: Risk here is the possibility that things don't go as planned because the assumptions (business hypotheses) underlying your ideas turn out to be wrong, resulting in loss: wasted resources, time, money, and so on.

- **Technology risk: "Can we do it?"** Research inside the building, focused on product features, technology, R&D, and the business model backstage. Key hypothesis: you can make the technology work, and at a particular cost.
- **Market risk: "Should we do it?"** Research outside the building, focused on customers, value propositions, and the business model front stage. Key hypothesis: you know what the right thing to do is.

It is not either/or but both, and not linear: you navigate back and forth between the two and must manage both well.

**The six market risks** (outside the building):

1. **Targeting the wrong market.** Hypothesis: you know which customer segment to target in terms of market size, available budget, etc.
2. **Not focusing on the right things.** Hypotheses: you focus on the right jobs (what matters to customers), the right pains (those customers really care about), and the right gains (those customers really care about). Each needs evidence.
3. **Misunderstanding the required performance.** Hypotheses: you know how to relieve pains, and relieve them sufficiently that the product performs well enough; and, on the gain side, you create sufficient gains, i.e. your products and services perform well enough to create the gains customers care about.
4. **Not differentiating sufficiently.** Your value proposition exists among competitors' value propositions, not in a vacuum. Hypothesis: you are sufficiently different from, or better than, the competition and outcompete other organizations.
5. **Not being able to reach and keep the market.** Hypothesis: you can reach the market: you've figured out the right channels and can establish the right relationships.
6. **Not making enough money.** Hypothesis: you can generate more revenue than you incur costs with the value proposition and business model.

**The three technology risks** (inside the building):

7. **Can't handle the technology, products, or services.** Hypothesis: you can manage and create the technologies, products, features, and services required for the performance customers really want.
8. **Can't handle the infrastructure** (the business model backstage). Hypothesis: you have access to the right resources, activities, and partners: you can perform the activities, manage the resources, or obtain them from key partners.
9. **Can't handle the costs.** Hypothesis: you can create the value proposition at less cost than the revenue it generates.

In short: technology risk is maybe not being able to create and deliver the value proposition, or doing so at too high a cost. Market risk is maybe not creating something customers want or will pay enough for.

**Example 1: airplane manufacturing.** A main customer pain is fuel consumption.

- Market risk (profile): do customers actually care about fuel consumption?
- Market risk (value proposition): if your products create fuel savings, are those savings good enough? Do customers care about the performance you provide; do you alleviate the pain sufficiently?
- Technology risk: can you build the airplane or technologies that deliver the performance required?

**Example 2: game consoles.**

- Market risk (profile): do customers really care about graphic performance?
- Market risk (value proposition): what performance is required, and do customers care about what your console provides?
- Technology risk: can you build a console that delivers the required performance?

Graphic performance matters a lot in consoles, but the Nintendo Wii targeted casual gamers, who didn't care much about graphics. The assumptions turned out completely different, and for a while the Wii dominated the market.

**Lessons**: Risk means the technology or market hypotheses under your ideas could lead to loss if wrong. The two risk types differ in nature and need different approaches, and you must manage both. Market risks: right market? right jobs, pains, and gains? sufficient performance? right differentiation? Technology risks: can you do it, deliver the right performance, and at the right cost?

### Validating the circle, the square, and the rectangle

**What it is**: Validating in sequence: first the Customer Profile (circle), then the Value Map (square), then the business model (rectangle).

**Why this order**: Customers aren't good at telling you what they want (the "faster horse" line, which Ford never actually said), so don't start by asking what they want or whether they like your value proposition. If you frame the conversation around your value proposition, you may never learn which jobs, pains, and gains really matter. Frame it around what matters to them.

**Step 1: Validate the circle.** Find evidence that the jobs, pains, and gains you intend to address really matter to customers.

- **Card deck technique**: write different jobs on cards, give the deck to customers, and ask them to prioritize the cards and set aside those that don't matter. This gives a first insight into which jobs matter most.
- The whole range of insight techniques is available, but remember that what customers say and what they do differ. The co-creator and scientist approaches are powerful because they reveal what customers do, not just say.
- **Unique link tracking**: create a trackable URL. During a customer interview about jobs, pains, gains, and maybe your value proposition, give them the URL for more information. Back at the office, check whether they clicked. If not, they probably weren't interested even if they said they were. A simple way to test whether customers mean what they say, to be followed by more solid testing.
- **Google AdWords**: buy search terms relevant to your value proposition. If people click your ad, that indicates interest. If they don't, it cost you nothing, but you probably need to change the value proposition.

**Step 2: Validate the square.** Provide evidence that customers care about how your products and services kill pains and create gains. All the same techniques apply. Especially useful: the **minimum viable product (MVP)**, a representation or prototype of a value proposition whose only purpose is to test hypotheses underlying it. Make it cheap and quick, test, learn, and probably throw it away. Sometimes you don't need to build anything at all.

- **Landing page**: a web page describing your value proposition, even if it doesn't exist yet, collecting emails as evidence of interest.
- **Video**: a make-believe video representing your value proposition long before you build anything, possibly with pre-orders as strong proof of interest. Afterward, tell customers it was a test, to be transparent, and perhaps give them a discount when the product really launches.
- **Wizard of Oz**: if the value proposition would require big infrastructure investment, deliver it manually with people before building the automated platform. This tests real-world interest; if there is interest, invest step by step in automating the service and make the Wizard of Oz real.

**Step 3: Validate the rectangle.** Provide evidence that you can build a profitable and scalable business model.

**Lessons**: Validate the circle before the square. What customers say and do differ, so run experiments for more solid evidence than interviews. You don't need to build anything to test MVPs; make-believe comes before investing in technology or other prototypes.

### Finding your earlyvangelists

**What it is**: An earlyvangelist (a term coined by Steve Blank, the father of the Lean Startup movement) is a potential early adopter of, and evangelist for, your new value proposition.

**The five characteristics** of an earlyvangelist:

1. **Has the job, problem, or need** you intend to address; otherwise they are not interesting to you.
2. **Is aware of having it.** Not everyone who has a job to be done is aware of it.
3. **Is actively looking for a solution.** The job is so important they aren't waiting for someone else to solve it.
4. **Has put together a solution out of piece parts** if none exists; it's so important they can't wait.
5. **Has, or can acquire, a budget.** Very important for you.

**Why it matters**: Earlyvangelists are very valuable for testing ideas and as evangelists when you launch. Start your validation process with them to learn about a market you may still need to build.

### Overview of the testing process

The process has two parts, supported by two tools:

- **Design** (using the Test Card): Extract hypotheses, Prioritize hypotheses, Design tests, Prioritize tests.
- **Testing** (using the Learning Card): Run tests, Capture learnings, Make progress.

### Defining critical business hypotheses

**What it is**: A business hypothesis is one of several assumptions or beliefs (also called guesses) that must be true for a business idea (a value proposition, a business model, or any business idea) to work. Each needs a lot of testing before it can be labeled **validated** (true) or **invalidated** (wrong), based on evidence, facts, and experimentation, never personal opinion. Hypotheses are the starting point for testing a business idea.

**Why not a business plan or an expensive prototype**: With a Customer Profile, Value Map, and maybe a Business Model Canvas sketched, many people write a business plan. But a business plan is an execution document; it doesn't prove hypotheses right or wrong, so it often leads to failure and lost money. Building prototypes can also be very expensive for technology or physical products, again risking loss. Instead, admit uncertainty is at its maximum; your only task is to find the most critical uncertainty (hypothesis) and test it first.

**How to do it**:

1. From your Business Model Canvas and Value Proposition Canvas, ask: **"What needs to be true for this idea to work?"** The answers are the hypotheses.
2. Prioritize them with the most critical at the top. Lower-ranked hypotheses are less critical and may become irrelevant if the most critical proves wrong.
3. Test the most critical first.

**Example: Ceres Imaging** (drone-based crop data). The idea: target farmers with drones carrying hyperspectral cameras that deliver crop data, sell the data, and expand worldwide.

- Farmer profile: jobs of managing and treating fields and forecasting production; gains of a profitable harvest; pains of diseases and other annoyances.
- Value proposition: hyperspectral image data so farmers can make targeted interventions and improve and forecast the harvest.
- Hypotheses ("what needs to be true?"): Can drones deliver the images? Can the images be stitched into data? Can the data be presented to farmers in a usable way? Do farmers actually need the data? Are they willing to change how they work with data (maybe they work more traditionally)? Do they want to forecast production? Do they have a budget for this data? Are they willing to pay for it?
- Prioritized: the most critical hypothesis is whether farmers are actually interested in data about their fields and crops. Whether drones can deliver images is irrelevant if farmers won't use the data.

### Exercise: extract your critical hypotheses

Your prototypes are really a set of assumptions. Use your Value Proposition Canvas prototype to identify the most important things that must be true for your idea to work; these are what you'll test when you get out of the building.

1. For each idea on your improved canvas, identify the assumptions it is built on, and articulate a hypothesis for each.
2. Rank all hypotheses by how critical they are for the idea to survive and thrive. Start with the Customer Profile, then move to the value proposition.
3. Ask: Are the hypotheses critical to the success of your customer? Critical to the success of your value proposition? Have you prioritized the most critical ones first?

## 17. Designing and Running Value Proposition Tests

### From hypothesis to market experiment

**What an experiment is**: A testing procedure carried out to discover something you don't know yet. You run experiments to validate or invalidate the hypotheses underlying a value proposition, business model, or any business idea you want to de-risk. An experiment produces concrete evidence that an idea will or won't work, ideally under relatively controlled conditions.

**Three possible outcomes** of testing a hypothesis:

1. The evidence **validates** the hypothesis.
2. The evidence is **inconclusive**, and you keep testing.
3. The evidence **invalidates** the hypothesis. That's fine: if tests are cheap and quick, you haven't wasted much time and money on something that doesn't work.

**Components of an experiment**:

- the **hypothesis** to test (start with the most critical one),
- the **experiment** (procedure) used to support or reject it,
- the **metrics** that measure the evidence,
- the **threshold** (criteria) for labeling the evidence validated or invalidated.

**Example 1: a "say" experiment.**

- Hypothesis: customer segment X frequently struggles with problem Y.
- Experiment: talk to 50 customers and ask them about their struggle with problem Y.
- Metric: how many experienced the struggle at least 4 times in the last 30 days.
- Threshold: validated if 25 or more customers struggled with Y at least 4 times in the last 30 days.

This is a good early experiment, but customers don't always do what they say.

**Example 2: the same experiment tweaked into a "do" experiment.**

- Hypothesis: customer segment X cares about the struggle with problem Y.
- Experiment: talk to 50 customers and give those who struggle a trackable URL pointing to a solution (if the problem matters, they'll click).
- Metric: the number of customers who actually clicked the URL.
- Threshold: validated if 25 or more customers clicked to get information about the solution.

**The bigger picture**: Uncertainty is highest at the start; your task is to reduce it by gradually increasing spending. Start with a small budget and cheap, quick experiments on the most critical assumption. Move to less important assumptions afterward, and only once uncertainty is reduced invest in costlier, longer, more time-consuming experiments.

**Characteristics of a good experiment**:

- **Well defined**: clear what exactly you're testing and what you hope to learn.
- **Quick and cheap**, especially early on; even later, spend no more money and time than you must.
- **Minimal**: optimized for learning; eliminate anything that doesn't help generate insights.
- **Measurable**: generates measurable evidence to validate or invalidate the hypothesis.

**Case: Ceres Imaging's experiments.** The idea: drones with hyperspectral cameras collecting images and data so farmers can do precision agriculture; the value proposition: targeted interventions that improve and forecast the harvest.

*What they did first*: They got out of the building and asked farmers, "Would you be interested in data from drones that would allow you to perform precision agriculture?" Farmers found it interesting (an opinion). Their next experiment focused on the solution: proving the drone worked, that software could stitch together images of a field, and that the data could be presented usably. Activities: buying a drone, buying a hyperspectral camera, buying image processing software, and engineering it all together.

*The problem*: very costly and very time-consuming, the opposite of what early experiments should be. They should have asked "what needs to be true for this idea to work?", extracted and prioritized the hypotheses, tested the most important first with cheap, quick experiments, moved to less critical hypotheses later, and only later assessed whether drones could deliver the data.

*What they did next*: They went back to farmers and talked about the *data*, leaving drones for later, after initial validation. The farmers said: "We don't care how you acquire the data. Why don't you use the crop dusters that fly over the fields all the time to spray pesticides?" A big moment: about 2,400 crop duster businesses fly over US farm fields, collectively managing about 5,000 airplanes. Ceres changed its experiments to use this existing aerial infrastructure: demonstrate that data can be collected with crop dusters, then investigate whether farmers would pay for it. Activities: hiring an existing plane, attaching a camera, flying over fields, and processing the data by hand. That is how an experiment should look: cheap and quick, reducing uncertainty very quickly.

**Lessons**:

1. The goal of an experiment is to test a hypothesis as cheaply and quickly as possible.
2. An experiment doesn't necessarily mean building a smaller version of your final product or service; the goal is to learn, not to build.
3. Test your most critical hypothesis first. Failing to do so is one of the most frequent mistakes.

### Build, Measure, Learn (and don't build when you "build")

**What it is**: The Build-Measure-Learn feedback loop at the heart of the Lean Startup process:

0. **Generate a hypothesis**: use the Value Proposition Canvas and Business Model Canvas to define the critical hypotheses underlying your ideas, so you design the right experiments.
1. **Design/build**: design or build an artifact specifically conceived to test your hypotheses, gain insights, and learn. It could be a conceptual prototype, an experiment, or a basic prototype (MVP) of the products and services you intend to offer.
2. **Measure**: measure the artifact's performance.
3. **Learn**: analyze the performance, compare it to your initial hypotheses, and derive insights. Ask what you thought would happen, describe what actually happened, then outline what you will change and how.

**The pitfall**: Entrepreneurs and intrapreneurs often start building early versions of their products and services far too soon. They don't realize the search phase is optimized for learning: the goal early on is to create the most learning as quickly and efficiently as possible. In the "build" stage you create early prototypes and MVPs. An MVP is an artifact or representation of a value proposition designed to test the validity of one or more critical assumptions (hypotheses) about a business model or value proposition. People often mistake prototypes and MVPs for limited versions of the future product, but "build" should not always be taken literally. The goal is not a limited version of your product; it is maximum learning for the least effort.

### The experiment library

**What experiments produce evidence for**:

1. **Interest and relevance**: are customers interested, and do they think the idea is relevant to them?
2. **Priorities**: their most important jobs, pains, and gains; which features, gain creators, or pain relievers they prefer and find relevant.
3. **Willingness to pay.**

**Classifying experiments on two axes**:

- **Say versus do**: what customers say can differ substantially from what they do, so experiments showing what customers *do* are far more reliable.
- **Direct interaction versus indirect observation**: direct means you talk to customers or they are in contact with you; indirect means customers are unaware you are testing. Your presence can influence what customers say or do, so indirect observation is more reliable.

**Call-to-action (CTA) tests**: a testing procedure that prompts a subject to perform an action in order to validate or invalidate a business hypothesis. CTAs produce much more reliable evidence than traditional surveys, interviews, focus groups, and similar techniques. The more the customer must invest (time, money, or other), and the more the CTA resembles a real-world situation such as a purchase, the more reliable the evidence that this is how the customer will act in the real world.

**The four quadrants**:

| | Say | Do |
|-|-|-|
| **Direct interaction** | Interviews, focus groups, participatory design (co-creation); storyboards, card sorting, innovation games (product box, speedboat) | Field studies (working alongside, consulting, shadowing); lab studies (customers interact with prototypes) |
| **Indirect observation** | Data analysis of existing data (e.g. what customers say on social media about brands, solutions, jobs, pains, and gains) | Simulated sales and tracking customer actions: landing pages, mock sales, concept stores, pre-sales, memorandums of understanding, ad-click tests, tracked links, split tests |

The most reliable evidence comes from watching what customers *do* through *indirect observation*. Interviews and focus groups are very good for getting started.

**Choosing your mix**: At the start, uncertainty and risk are at their maximum; reduce them by gradually investing more in different experiment types. Test the most critical assumptions first with cheap, quick experiments, so you don't waste time, money, and energy on ideas that don't matter to customers. Use a mix: weigh **cost, data reliability, and time required**. Some experiments are quick and cheap but less reliable; others are harder but more reliable, so you use different ones at different moments. Rule of thumb: start cheap when uncertainty is at its maximum, and spend more the more evidence you have.

**Minimum viable products (MVPs)**: A representation or prototype of a value proposition designed specifically to test the validity of one or more hypotheses underlying your business idea; that is its only purpose. Test as quickly and cheaply as possible, so early MVPs may not resemble the final product at all. Types:

- **Storyboards** presenting the value proposition in a real-world context.
- **Product box**: packaging for a non-existent product, making it more real so customers can give feedback.
- **High- or low-fidelity prototypes.**
- **Variations** of a product, service, or value proposition to test different aspects.
- **A website** describing the solution with a call to action to learn whether customers are interested.
- **Video**: for products very difficult to create, a video making customers believe the product already exists.
- **Wizard of Oz**: for products requiring huge capital investment, perform the service manually to test interest; with proof, scale up the infrastructure.
- **Spec sheet** of a non-existing product, used for pre-sales or memorandums of understanding.
- **Life-size prototypes**, so customers get a real feel for the final value proposition; typical in the automotive and hotel industries.

**Direct interaction, "say" experiments**:

- **Storyboards**: after prototyping the value proposition, define scenarios that make it feel more real, create the visuals, and test them with customers to learn what they like and dislike; debrief and adapt.
- **Card sorting**: give customers cards with all the jobs, pains, and gains you imagined (or features you have in mind) and ask them to prioritize, most important on top. Across a series of customers, patterns emerge in how they prioritize.
- **Innovation games**, games played with customers to learn their preferences:
  - **Product box** (co-creation): invite customers to design the packaging representing their ideal value proposition, then pitch it back to you. You learn about their jobs, pains, gains, and the pain relievers and gain creators they find important. Used even in B2B to co-create value propositions.
  - **Speedboat**: draw a boat on a whiteboard and ask customers to drop the anchors: the pains holding them back from performing a job well. You learn everything that annoys them and may build a new value proposition on it. More tangible than an interview.

**Direct interaction, "do" experiments**:

- **Field studies**: work alongside customers or consult for them to get a very good perspective on their jobs, pains, gains, and desired solutions; shadow customers for a day or a week, in consumer goods and in B2B. Watching customers in their real environment gives the best clues about important jobs and relevant pains and gains.
- **Lab studies**: bring customers in to interact with your value proposition (products or services). Big hotel chains build life-size prototypes of hotel rooms to learn what customers struggle with and what a good solution looks like.

**Indirect observation, "do" experiments** (customers don't know they're being tested):

- **Landing pages**: a web page with a headline, a value proposition, and a call to action; invite customers to it. Measure (1) the total audience you invited, (2) how many visited, showing interest, and (3) how many visitors performed the requested action, such as signing up with their email or clicking buttons. Finally, reach out to some of them and ask about their behavior. Works in B2C and B2B without building a real product or prototype.
- **Pre-sales actions**: online **mock sales**, simulating a sale with a "buy now" button (more appropriate in B2C); physical **concept stores** to learn about customer behavior; **pre-orders**, increasingly popular in B2C; and **memorandums of understanding** in the physical world, which customers sign even though they have little legal value; customers won't sign if they have no interest.
- **Google AdWords** (or other ad sites): buy a search term related to your value proposition, or to the jobs, pains, and gains, put up an ad, and watch whether people click. If nobody clicks, the test was free, but nobody is interested. It applies to B2B too: where do people go first when searching for something in B2B? Often Google.
- **Enhanced interview (tracked link)**: create a landing page and a trackable URL. After interviewing customers, give them the URL for more information. Back at the office, measure whether they clicked. If they said they were interested but didn't click, they probably have other jobs, pains, gains, or priorities that matter more; go back and ask what's on their mind. A simple way to verify customers mean what they say.
- **Split testing**: compare two variations of a value proposition and see whether customers behave differently. Example: split testing was used to choose the title of the book *Value Proposition Design*; that title was the most successful at getting people to sign up for the book launch.

**Indirect observation, "say" experiments**: data analysis, for example of social media, to learn what customers say about jobs, pains, gains, or brands.

**Lessons**: Reducing risk and uncertainty needs a mix of experiments, not one type. What customers say and do differ: start with customer discovery interviews but move toward call-to-action tests. Direct or indirect contact can affect the outcome. The bigger the customer's investment, or the closer the experiment is to a real-world situation, the more reliable the evidence.

### The Test Card

**What it is**: A tool to describe how you will test a hypothesis, in four steps.

**Header fields**: Test Name, Assigned to, Deadline, Duration.

**The four steps**:

1. **Hypothesis: "We believe that..."** One thing that has to be true for your idea to work, selected from the many you have. Mark whether it is **Critical**.
2. **Test: "To verify that, we will..."** The experiment you'll run. Rate the **Test Cost** and **Data Reliability** (and **Time Required**).
3. **Metric: "And measure..."** The data you'll measure.
4. **Criteria: "We are right if..."** The threshold that validates or invalidates the hypothesis.

**Example: pricing the value proposition design course.**

- Test name: Pricing the value proposition design course; assigned to the person responsible for testing and data.
- We believe that people are willing to pay $500 for a self-service value proposition design course. This was tested before designing and filming the course. It was marked as critical; but before testing price, the team had already tested whether people even wanted the course.
- To verify that, we will simulate the sale of the online course on our website. Cost: circled on the card. Data reliability: fairly high, because a simulated purchase situation is close to reality, unlike talking to people, whose answers are less reliable because people don't always do what they say.
- And measure how many website visitors convert.
- We are right if the conversion rate is at least as high as that of the previous course, the Business Model Generation course, which sold at $500.

**Exercise: design your experiments with the Test Card** (about 20 minutes):

1. Select a high-priority hypothesis.
2. Outline the experiment that will verify whether it is correct or must be rejected and revised (test).
3. Define what data you'll measure (metric).
4. Define a target threshold to validate or invalidate it (criteria).
5. Repeat for all the most critical hypotheses.

Ask yourself: Do you have a test for the top three most critical hypotheses? Will your tests produce results quickly and cheaply? Can any test be made smaller or split into multiple tests? Are your tests clearly measurable?

### The Learning Card

**What it is**: A tool to capture the learnings from experiments and turn them into decisions, in four quick sections.

**Header fields**: Insight Name, Person Responsible, Date of Learning.

**The four steps**:

1. **Hypothesis: "We believed that..."**
2. **Observation: "We observed..."** Include the **Data Reliability**.
3. **Learnings and insights: "From that we learned that..."** Mark whether **Action Required**: if what you learned could put you out of business, identify something to do about it (drastic action).
4. **Decisions and actions: "Therefore, we will..."**

**Example**:

- We believed that product managers are interested in the Value Proposition Canvas.
- We observed that 32% of product managers clicked on the link we sent them, against a target of 25%: good news. (Data reliability noted.)
- From that we learned that product managers can be a potential customer segment for the online course.
- Therefore, we will test whether an online course generates a high click-through rate as well.

### Get out of the building

Once you've extracted hypotheses and created Test Cards, get out of the building and run experiments:

1. Start performing the experiments at the top of your list.
2. Capture insights with the Learning Card.
3. **If hypotheses are invalid**: go back to the drawing board and pivot. Find alternative segments or value propositions to make the idea work.
4. **If hypotheses are valid**: expand your experiments to the next building block. Move to the next important hypothesis when you're satisfied with your insights and the data reliability.

This is just the beginning of a long journey. Keep your eyes on the prize: track whether you are advancing from your initial idea toward a profitable and scalable business.
