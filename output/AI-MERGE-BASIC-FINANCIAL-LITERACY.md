## 1. Why Businesses Fail: Market Cycles and the Cost of Do-Overs

### 1.1 All Markets Go Up and Down

**What it is**: Every market moves in a cycle, rising and then falling. The length of time from one peak (or trough) to the next is about ten years.

**Why it matters**: This cycle explains why most businesses are only alive for two, five or ten years, and why ninety six percent of all businesses fail within that period. Businesses do not fail at random. They fail because the tide quit going up. Most businesses that survive two, five or ten years only survive that long because everything is good while the tide is rising. Rising markets hide bad flying. As soon as the tide starts going down, the owner discovers they do not know what to do.

**Example**: The owner in a falling market is like a pilot who suddenly notices they are out of gas. They look at the altimeter, the compass and all the levers in the cockpit of the plane called their business, and they do not know which to use.

### 1.2 The Fourteen-Levers Mistake

**What it is**: When trouble hits, the untrained owner pulls many levers at once: cutting this, launching that, changing prices, changing staff, all at the same time.

**Why it fails**: Throwing fourteen levers at one time sends the plane lurching around. Even if something changes, you have no idea which of those levers caused it. You cannot learn, you cannot repeat what worked, and you cannot undo what hurt.

**Pitfalls**: Pulling levers without being able to read the instruments. The fix is learning to read the dials (see Section 2) so you know which single lever to pull and can see its effect.

### 1.3 Off Course, Out of Fuel, Wrong Altitude

**What it is**: In practice, most businesses are off course, running out of fuel and flying at the wrong altitude. It is only a matter of time before they hit the mountain. In business, hitting the mountain is called a bankruptcy, a failure.

**Why it matters**: The real problem with bankruptcy is not that you lose your money. The problem is that you lose your time. The cost of a do-over is paid, first, in your confidence and, second, in your time.

Wealth is created by compounding. That is how wealth is created. Warren Buffett did not wake up one morning and go from zero to forty billion overnight. He has been at it thirty five, forty years, compounding his wealth and minimizing the number of do-overs he has had. Every failure resets the compounding clock to zero.

Most entrepreneurs just ride the cycle: times are good, then they fail; the market comes back and they start another business; it lasts a while, the market goes back down, and they are back into failure again.

### 1.4 The Carousel: Making Money Versus Keeping It

**What it is**: The core lesson of the cycle: the trick is not making money when the tide is going up. The trick is keeping it when the tide is going down.

**Why it works**: When the tide goes up, everybody gets rich. When the tide goes down, everybody gives it back. Then the tide goes up and everybody gets rich again, and the tide goes down and everybody gives it back again. People know how to ride the wave up, but not how to hold on to what they made. Everybody wants to teach you how to make money, and making money is about the easiest thing in the world. The skill that separates survivors is keeping it.

**Example**: On a ten to fourteen day vacation in Italy (Milan, then Florence), after a full day of sightseeing among the Michelangelo and Leonardo da Vinci works, a couple sat at a coffee shop on the edge of a giant plaza, about eight times bigger than a hotel ballroom. On the far side was a carousel, the kind with little horses that go up and down and round and round, with a huge line of people waiting to get on. After both kept glancing at it, the wife asked, "What's that remind you of?" Her answer: "It reminds me of most people's lives. They just go up and down, up and down, and end up right back where they started." That is exactly what most people, and most businesses, do.

**Pitfalls**: Judging yourself by how much you made in a good market. Anyone can do that. The test is what is left after the downturn.

## 2. Flying the Business: The Three Big Dials

**What it is**: The goal of financial literacy is to make you proficient as a pilot, so you can successfully fly your business: stay on course, stay at the right altitude and not run out of gas. Those three failures are what kill businesses.

**Why it works**: A business has lots of dials, but only three really big ones. Master those three and you can read where the plane is, instead of pulling levers blind. The three big dials, or scorecards, are:

1. The balance sheet (a snapshot of things and stuff, and whether you owe or own them).
2. The income statement (a movie of revenue, expenses and profit over a period).
3. The cash flow statement (the record of cash, split into three kinds).

There are lots of little scorecards, but these are the three big ones.

***

## 3. Dial One: The Balance Sheet

### 3.1 What the Balance Sheet Tracks

**What it is**: The first big dial keeps track of the things and stuff in a business, and whether we owe money on our things and stuff or own our things and stuff. That is all it does. A business has things and stuff; do we owe or do we own them?

The balance sheet is in three parts: assets (the things and stuff), liabilities (what we owe) and equity (what we own).

### 3.2 Why It Is Called a Balance Sheet

The left side always equals the right side. It balances.

**Example**: Assets of a hundred equal a hundred on the other side: eighty plus twenty equals a hundred. A hundred equals a hundred.

### 3.3 The Balance Sheet Is a Snapshot

A balance sheet is like a snapshot, a digital photo: this is the way things looked right then, at one moment. Compare this with the income statement, which is a movie (Section 4.5).

### 3.4 What the Business Owns

A business really owns two things: the investment that was made by the partners, and its earnings. Both sit in the equity part of the balance sheet. Cash, by contrast, is a thing and stuff, so it sits in assets.

## 4. Dial Two: The Income Statement

### 4.1 Many Names, One Report

**What it is**: The second big scorecard is called an income statement, a statement of operations, a P&L or profit and loss. All of these mean the same thing. Accountants give everything fourteen different names to keep you guessing, so an owner who says "I don't know what a P&L is, but I do have an income statement" is holding the same document.

### 4.2 How It Is Built

The income statement is divided into two parts:

1. **The top part**: revenue, also called sales, or turnover (the term used in some countries).
2. **Deductions**: all the expenses are subtracted from revenue.

What is left over is the profit, also called earnings or net income. The formula: revenue minus expenses equals profit (or earnings).

### 4.3 Financial Statements Versus the Tax Return

**What it is**: Some owners never get financial statements. Instead, once a year, whether they need to or not, they look at their tax return.

**Why it fails**: This is two bad ideas at once. First, looking at the numbers only once a year is a bad idea. Second, looking at a tax return to figure out what is going on in your business is a really bad idea, because it does not tell you the information you need to know. This habit helps explain why most businesses do not beat the odds. If you are going to go to all the trouble and work of running a business, get the skills to fly the plane and not crash.

**The statistics**: Only fifty percent of business owners get financial statements. Of the fifty percent who get them, only three percent know how to read them. Those numbers look suspiciously like the business success ratio.

### 4.4 The Bottom-Line Trap

**What it is**: The typical owner who gets financial statements looks at one number first: the bottom line, net income. If it is a good number, they go have a drink. If it is a bad number, they go have a drink. Everyone stares at this one number, which is another reason most businesses do not succeed.

**Why it fails**: The bottom line alone hides the shape of the business.

**Example**:

| | Business A | Business B |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 10 | 1,000 |
| Expenses | 6 | 996 |
| Profit | 4 | 4 |

Is there a difference between these two businesses? There is. And yet, when you look at their profits, they are exactly the same. Looking only at the bottom line, you cannot tell them apart.

### 4.5 Profit Is a Theory

**What it is**: The whole income statement, the entire piece of paper, is a theory. Nothing on it is real. It is an important theory, but a theory: the theory of whether revenue exceeds expenses, and if so by how much, or whether expenses exceed revenue, and if so by how much.

**Why**: You cannot spend profits. Try to pay your bills with your profits. Check out of a hotel and tell the cashier, "I don't have any cash. Will you take some of my profits? Will you take some of my retained earnings?" They will slap you. They do not want your profits. Your profits are not real; neither you nor they can spend them. The income statement is not designed to tell you the facts. It is designed to tell you the theory of whether revenue exceeds expenses and by how much. That is all.

(This claim usually meets disagreement at first. The reason becomes clear once profit is compared with cash; see Sections 6 and 7.)

### 4.6 The Income Statement Is a Movie

Unlike the balance sheet, which is a snapshot, the income statement is a movie. It covers a period of time: for the month of January, or for the year 2008. It has a beginning and an end and shows what happened over that period, whereas the balance sheet shows how things looked at one moment.

## 5. Dial Three: The Cash Flow Statement

**What it is**: The third big dial is the cash flow statement. It is all about cash. It starts with how much cash you had, then shows how much you generated or used in each category of cash, and adds it all up to an ending cash number.

There are three kinds of cash:

- **Operating cash**: cash the business generates from its operations, as opposed to borrowing or selling things.
- **Financing cash**: money you borrow.
- **Investing cash**: money from buying or selling long-term things, for example selling your computer, your vehicle or your real estate.

**Why it matters**: Unlike the income statement, this report tracks something real. Cash can be spent; profits cannot. Which kind of cash the business produces is the key health question (Section 7).

***

## 6. How the Three Statements Fit Together

### 6.1 World Atlas Versus Street Map

**What it is**: The balance sheet is a world atlas: it tells you there is such a place as North America. The income statement and the cash flow statement are street maps: they tell you how to get to 123 Elm Street in Austin, Texas. The balance sheet shows where you are; the other two show how you got there. Each works like a worksheet behind one number on the balance sheet.

### 6.2 Profit Links the Income Statement to the Balance Sheet

The profits, or earnings, on the income statement and the earnings the business owns on the balance sheet are exactly the same number. (Accountants will object to "exactly," but stay with the idea.) The balance sheet keeps track of what the profits are. If you want to know how those profits were generated, you do not look at the world atlas, you look at the street map: the income statement.

**Example**: The balance sheet says profits are four. How did you get to four? The income statement answers: revenue was a thousand and expenses were nine hundred and ninety six, so you ended up with four. Or revenue was ten and expenses were six, and that is how you ended up with four. Four is the number on the balance sheet; the income statement shows the route.

### 6.3 Cash Links the Cash Flow Statement to the Balance Sheet

Cash is things and stuff, so it sits in assets on the balance sheet. The ending number on the cash flow statement and the cash number on the balance sheet are exactly the same.

**Example**: The balance sheet says, "You've got a hundred in cash." Good, but how did you get to a hundred? The cash flow statement answers: you started with fifty, used ten in one category, made fifty in another, and made ten more in the third. Add all that up and you get a hundred (50 − 10 + 50 + 10 = 100). Both numbers are a hundred.

**Summary of the links**:

- Income statement net income = earnings on the balance sheet.
- Cash flow statement ending cash = cash in assets on the balance sheet.
- The balance sheet tells you where you are; the other two are the worksheets that tell you how you got there.

## 7. The Business Owner's Job: Assets to Sales to Profits to Operating Cash

### 7.1 The Conversion Chain

**What it is**: Why does a business have assets at all? Not vaguely "to create value." The whole reason a business has assets is to produce sales. The whole reason it has sales is to have profits. And the whole reason it has profits is to get cash.

So a business owner's job is:

1. Buy assets.
2. Convert those assets into sales.
3. Convert those sales into profits.
4. Convert those profits into cash.

That is the business owner's job. That is what you are supposed to be doing.

### 7.2 Which Cash? Operating Cash

**What it is**: Not just any cash, and not simply take-home cash. What you really want is operating cash. Money you borrow is financing cash. Money you get from selling your computer, your vehicle or your real estate is investing cash. Neither shows that the business itself works.

**Why it works**: The health of a business is not dictated by how much the profits are. The health of a business is dictated by how good it is at converting its profits into cash, specifically operating cash. (This is why profit alone is only a theory; see Section 4.5.)

**How to do it**: Look past the bottom line and ask of every period's numbers: how good is my business at converting profits into operating cash? Cash from borrowing (financing) or from selling computers, vehicles or real estate (investing) does not answer that question.
