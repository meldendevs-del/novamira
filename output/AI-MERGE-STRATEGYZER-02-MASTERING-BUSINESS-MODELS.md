## 1. Business Models and the Business Model Canvas

### What a business model is

A business model describes the rationale of how an organization creates, delivers, and captures value. It can best be described through nine basic building blocks that show the logic of how a company intends to make money. The nine blocks cover the four main areas of a business: customers, offer, infrastructure, and financial viability. The business model is like a blueprint for a strategy that is then implemented through organizational structures, processes, and systems.

The concept has to be simple, relevant, and intuitively understandable, while not oversimplifying the complexities of how enterprises function. It lets you describe and think through the business model of your own organization, your competitors, or any other enterprise. It has been applied and tested around the world in organizations such as IBM, Ericsson, Deloitte, and the Public Works and Government Services of Canada.

### Why you need a shared language

**What it is**: A common, visual vocabulary for discussing business models, so that everyone in a meeting is talking about the same thing.

**Why it works**: Ask a room of people who don't know the canvas "What is a business model?" and have them write down a definition. You get a Tower of Babel: in the story, people tried to build a tower all the way to heaven, and God created different languages so they could no longer cooperate on it. That is exactly what happens when people discuss business models, value propositions, and strategy without a shared language.

A typical meeting without one looks like this. The smartest people sit around the table. The boss opens: "The world is changing. We urgently need to reinvent our business model." The first person says, "Services are the way to go." Someone else says, "The numbers show we should grow in emerging markets." Another says, "What about that new technology we've looked into?" The boss replies, "I actually know the right person we should hire regarding that technology." Three hours later, people are still talking and have produced no great results. This doesn't happen because people aren't smart. It happens because they aren't using the right business tools. There is a word for the phenomenon: "blah blah blah" (from Dan Roam's book *Blah Blah Blah: What to Do When Words Don't Work*).

The starting point for any good discussion, meeting, or workshop on business model innovation is a shared understanding of what a business model actually is. Without a shared language it is difficult to systematically challenge assumptions about your business model and innovate successfully. A picture or visual language makes business models, strategies, and value propositions much clearer. The instruction is to leave the land of blah blah blah behind and enter the land of visual tools, starting with the Business Model Canvas.

The canvas did not fall from the sky. It came out of a PhD dissertation Alexander Osterwalder completed in 2004 with his supervisor Yves Pigneur at HEC Lausanne. When it was applied in the field, people were so interested that the book *Business Model Generation* was written with a core team and 470 practitioners from 45 countries.

### The Business Model Canvas

**What it is**: A visual business tool, a preformatted "painter's canvas" of the nine building blocks, on which you sketch out how an organization creates, delivers, and captures value. It lets you map, discuss, design, and invent business models, and shows an entire business model in one image. It works for startup entrepreneurs just as well as for the most senior executives.

**Why it works**: It is not sufficient to enumerate the nine building blocks. Mapping them onto a pre-structured canvas turns an abstract conversation into a tangible one that fosters understanding, discussion, creativity, and analysis.

The nine building blocks, in one line each:

1. **Customer Segments**: all the people or organizations for which you are creating value, including simple users and paying customers.
2. **Value Propositions**: for each segment, the bundle of products and services that creates value for those customers. An organization seeks to solve customer problems and satisfy customer needs with value propositions.
3. **Channels**: the touch points through which you communicate, distribute, sell, and deliver the value proposition.
4. **Customer Relationships**: the type of relationship you establish and maintain with each segment.
5. **Revenue Streams**: how, and through which pricing mechanisms, the business model captures value. Revenue streams result from value propositions successfully offered to customers.
6. **Key Resources**: which assets are indispensable in your business model to offer and deliver the previous elements.
7. **Key Activities**: which things you really need to be able to perform well.
8. **Key Partners**: who can help you leverage your business model, since you won't own all key resources yourself nor perform all key activities. Some activities are outsourced and some resources are acquired outside the enterprise.
9. **Cost Structure**: once you understand the infrastructure, you have an idea of its costs. The business model elements result in the cost structure.

The right-hand side (segments, value propositions, channels, relationships, revenue streams) is the value side; the left-hand side (resources, activities, partners, costs) is the infrastructure that creates, delivers, and captures that value. The canvas is sometimes read as "right brain, emotion, value" on the right and "left brain, logic, efficiency" on the left.

### The canvas as a theater

**What it is**: A metaphor for the dynamics of the canvas.

**How it works**: Imagine the right-hand side of the canvas as the front stage of a theater, visible to the audience. The front stage is where you interact with your customers, clients, and users; it represents the value they are willing to pay for. Go behind the curtain and you are backstage. The backstage is what makes the show in front possible, but most customers won't see who is back there or what they are doing. The backstage drives cost. The equation is simple: a sound business model requires revenues at least as big as, and hopefully much bigger than, your costs. That outcome is called profit.

### Practical use: materials, scope, and timing

- **Materials**: paper, large posters, and sticky notes, or digital tools (Strategyzer, or simply PowerPoint). Paper and digital are complementary: each lets you do things the other can't. The canvas works best printed on a large surface so groups can jointly sketch and discuss with Post-it notes or board markers.
- **Scope**: use it to sketch existing business models you want to improve, or to invent new ones. It applies to the whole spectrum from improve to invent.
- **Timing for existing business models**: from about ten minutes if you do it alone, up to half a day or more with a management team.
- **Timing for new business models**: keep first prototypes very short, five minutes or thirty minutes to sketch them briefly. Only later, once you know something could really work, add detail and granularity. (Elsewhere in the material the recommended range for early prototypes is five to fifteen minutes, and in the bicycle debrief five to thirty minutes; all agree the point is to keep them rough and fast.)

### The six ground rules

**Rule 1: Avoid writing directly on the canvas. Use sticky notes.** Sticky notes let you have a tangible, strategic conversation by sketching out every building block. There is no risk: nothing is cast in stone. You can move notes around, and if you put one up that shouldn't be there, you take it away.

**Rule 2: Don't get stuck in blah blah blah. Just get started.** People often stand in front of a canvas talking about which block they could sketch out, or what they should do, instead of sketching. Put up the sticky notes. You want a tangible, visible, concrete conversation, and there is no risk because a note can always be moved or removed when the conversation shows it doesn't belong.

**Rule 3: Start with any building block.** You can start with an easy one like customer segments or the value proposition, but for a new business model you don't have to. Examples of other starting points:
- **Key partners**, if you want to build a business model together with a partner (Nike and Apple with the iPod: a perfect fit and a partnership that let them create a business model together).
- **Key resources**, if you have a patent and want to build a business model around it.
- **Cost structure**, when you have to change or reduce costs.
- **Channels**, if you have a great channel reaching a lot of customers but don't know what else you could do with it.
- **Revenue streams**, if you want to create new ones.

**Rule 4: Never use bullet points. Never.** With bullet points on one sticky note you can't move things around, so you can't use the canvas as a strategic playground. Put each element on a separate sticky note so you can play with it.

**Rule 5: Avoid too much detail.** If you write a lot on each note and have twenty notes on a canvas, your brain can't process it and you lose the big picture. Write as little as possible: use the thickest marker in the room and only large capitals. Better still, use visual thinking: sketch on the notes (clearly), because your brain processes images faster than words. Best of all is images and words together, so it is crystal clear and still quick to process. On granularity: when people first get excited about the canvas they put up everything that comes to mind, and that is the right way to start. But once too much is up there, you can't see the essence of the business model. So afterwards, synthesize: take away the elements that don't show much about the big picture, and reorganize until you have a story, the big picture of what you are doing with your business.

**Rule 6: Be precise for every building block, without writing too much.** Everybody knows Google earns most of its money from advertising, but "advertising" is not precise enough. Google earns money by auctioning off search terms. That is still one of its most profitable mechanisms and it is very concrete; it is what made Google very wealthy.

### Six best practices for clearer canvases

A bad canvas has three typical problems: far too much granularity, so you can't see the big picture; random color coding, which makes your brain read meaning into colors that mean nothing; and no story.

**Practice 1: Use color coding with a system.** Say you have a networking platform targeting free users, plus two other segments, advertisers and app developers. Give each segment a color and color every element related to that segment accordingly. You then see immediately which elements belong to which segment. You can color-code other things too, but only when the colors say something.

**Practice 2: Separate the as-is from the to-be business model.** The as-is model is your business model today, as it stands. The to-be model is what you want to design for tomorrow, whether a new model or improvements to the existing one. Distinguish them with color coding, or better, with two separate canvases.

**Practice 3: Distinguish facts from assumptions.** Facts are what you know with certainty; assumptions are what you don't know yet and still have to prove. This applies mostly to new business models (a new venture, a new idea, or new elements added to an existing model). Earmark known elements as facts and unknown ones as assumptions. Anyone looking at the canvas then immediately understands the hypotheses underlying the model and the risks involved.

**Practice 4: Design business models with a story.** In a business model with a story, every building block has a role to play. Take Google with search ads for advertisers and a search engine for users: pull out the search engine and the whole business model crumbles. Great business models have a story: pull one piece out and the whole thing falls apart.

**Practice 5: Connect every building block; no orphan elements.** Say you have a news site targeting free users, so revenue from those users is zero, and you want to earn money from banner advertising. If "banner advertising" just sits there, you are not telling the whole story. Ask who is interested in paying for banner advertising and which value proposition they are paying for. This seems obvious, yet senior executives constantly design business models with revenue streams that come from somewhere with nobody paying for them and no specific value attached. The same applies to all other elements: no single piece should be unrelated to another.

**Practice 6: Separate different ideas or stories into different canvases.** Many ideas on one canvas can't be told apart, and you understand none of them well. Separate them into different prototypes. At the end you can still put them together: sometimes it makes sense to take ideas apart to understand each, and sometimes to put them together to see the synergies.

### Exercises

- **Sketch Google's business model.** Print or draw a canvas (or use the Strategyzer app), sketch your understanding of Google's search business with sticky notes, and apply the ground rules. Don't go into initiatives such as Android (the mobile operating system) or Google Apps (the enterprise offering), which both support the search business; just sketch the big picture. (A worked answer is in section 3.)
- **Sketch an existing business model of your choice.** Pick one you find particularly interesting or know well. Print a large canvas poster or draw one on a whiteboard. Don't write on the canvas; use sticky notes, one idea per note, no bullet points. Apply all ground rules and best practices, for example color coding for segments and/or value propositions. Keep this canvas: later exercises build on it.

### How practitioners use the canvas

Uses reported by practitioners show the range of the tool: helping a public-sector department view itself as a service-oriented business with externalized as-is and to-be models; illustrating to small companies how the counterintuitive freemium model (giving core products away free) makes financial sense; planning owners' transition and exit through a business model innovation program; helping artists, cultural producers, and game designers envision business models for the creative industries; designing and aligning the leadership of a new non-profit program and clarifying its true value proposition and sustainability; showing all members of a complex print-to-digital publishing project the big picture, their roles, and the interdependencies (saving hours of explaining and arguing); assessing a friend's personal business model, which revealed strong core competences and value proposition but weak use of strategic partners and customer relationships; getting 60 first-year students who knew nothing about entrepreneurship to pitch a viable idea in less than five days; translating early-stage entrepreneurs' business plans into the business processes they will need; preparing a startup plan attractive to VCs; redesigning an NGO's language service by linking it to people's day-to-day needs; keeping startup teams holistic and out of the details; creating a common language to explore growth opportunities and assess competitors' models; moving Dutch health care organizations from budget-driven institutions to entrepreneurial ones; restructuring a public company's value chain after regulatory change; designing a future organizational structure in a global manufacturing company with 15,000 Post-its and more than 100 meters of brown paper; reality-checking a new startup; clarifying SMEs' current models and the impact of e-commerce; and aligning key staff on shared goals and strategic priorities that fed into a balanced scorecard.

### Related bodies of knowledge

The canvas is the foundation for a wider toolkit. *Business Model Generation* organizes it into: business model **patterns** (Unbundling, the Long Tail, Multi-Sided Platforms, FREE, and Open Business Models; a single business model can incorporate several patterns, and the point of patterns is to recast well-known business concepts in the standard canvas format so they are immediately usable); **design techniques** (Customer Insights, Ideation, Visual Thinking, Prototyping, Storytelling, Scenarios), based on the conviction that businesspeople don't just need to understand designers better, they need to become designers; **strategy** through the canvas lens (the Business Model Environment, Evaluating Business Models, a business model perspective on Blue Ocean Strategy, and Managing Multiple Business Models); and a generic **design process**. Five open topics are flagged for further exploration: business models beyond profit (public and non-profit sectors), computer-aided business model design, the relationship between business models and business plans, implementing business models in new or existing organizations, and business model and IT alignment.

## 2. The Nine Building Blocks in Depth

### 2.1 Customer Segments

**What it is**: The block at the far right of the canvas; the entire canvas is geared towards creating value for the customer. A customer segment is a group of people, users, or organizations you are creating value for: whose problems you help solve or whose needs you help satisfy. Customers are the heart of any business model; without (profitable) customers, no company survives for long.

**Why it matters**: An organization must make a conscious decision about which segments to serve and which to ignore. Once that decision is made, the whole business model can be designed around a strong understanding of specific customer needs.

**When groups are separate segments**: customer groups are distinct segments if:
- their needs require and justify a distinct offer;
- they are reached through different distribution channels;
- they require different types of relationships;
- they have substantially different profitabilities;
- they are willing to pay for different aspects of the offer.

**Key questions**: For whom are we creating value? Who are our most important customers?

#### Segmenting by jobs to be done

**What it is**: Traditionally, segments are described with demographics, psychographics, and behavior. Add a further lens: what jobs are customers trying to get done in their work and their lives? Don't only ask who your customers are; ask what jobs they are trying to get done. A job to be done is a fundamental problem customers hope to address or a need they hope to satisfy: what customers "hire" a product or service to do for them.

**Where it comes from**: Two groups framed the concept. Clayton Christensen at Harvard says we hire products to do things for us; in a famous talk he explains why customers hire milkshakes: it turns out it is to kill time during a long and boring ride to work, not just to fill themselves up. Tony Ulwick, founder of the consultancy Strategyn, says we get much better results if we focus on what customers are trying to get done.

**Why it matters (example)**: Banks offer wealth management services to high net worth individuals, people or families with more than one million dollars of investable assets. By demographics they look alike. By jobs, the picture can look very different: one group may focus on retiring very early, another on having an impact with the money they earned. The consequences for the business model are major: you need different value propositions for the different jobs, and the whole canvas is geared towards helping each group get its jobs done in a profitable, scalable way. Lesson: it can be very interesting to segment customers not just by who they are but by the jobs they are trying to get done.

**Three job types** (with wealth-management examples):
- **Functional jobs**: getting a task done or solving a problem; very technical. "I need somebody to manage my investable wealth."
- **Social jobs**: how a customer wants to be perceived by others. "I want to show off my success," or "I'd like to meet like-minded people."
- **Emotional jobs**: how a customer wants to feel. "I want to sleep well at night. I don't want my money at risk. I want peace of mind."

#### Types of customer segments

- **Mass market**: no distinction between segments. Value propositions, channels, and relationships all focus on one large group with broadly similar needs and problems. Often found in consumer electronics.
- **Niche market**: specific, specialized segments, with value propositions, channels, and relationships all tailored to the niche's requirements. Often found in supplier-buyer relationships; for example, many car part manufacturers depend heavily on purchases from major automobile manufacturers.
- **Segmented**: distinguishes between market segments with slightly different needs and problems. The retail arm of a bank like Credit Suisse may distinguish between a large group of customers each possessing assets of up to U.S. $100,000 and a smaller group of affluent clients each with net worth exceeding U.S. $500,000. Both have similar but varying needs, with implications for value propositions, channels, relationships, and revenue streams. Micro Precision Systems, which provides outsourced micromechanical design and manufacturing, serves three segments (the watch industry, the medical industry, and industrial automation) with slightly different value propositions.
- **Diversified**: serves two unrelated segments with very different needs. In 2006 Amazon.com diversified its retail business by selling "cloud computing" services (online storage space and on-demand server usage) to a totally different segment, Web companies, with a totally different value proposition. The rationale: its powerful IT infrastructure could be shared by retail operations and the new cloud unit.
- **Multi-sided platforms (multi-sided markets)**: serve two or more interdependent segments (see below).

#### Direct customers versus intermediaries

Nestlé sells one of its yogurt brands to consumers through retailers. Is the consumer the real customer? It is the end customer. The direct customer is the retailer, reached through a sales force with a specific value proposition for retailers. In business models with intermediaries you have two customer segments and need a specific value proposition for both.

#### Double-sided and multi-sided markets

**What it is**: A business model that only works when two (or more) interdependent segments are both present, each attracted by the other.

**Examples**:
- **Google search**: users come for free search; advertisers come to reach a large audience. Advertisers only come if there are many users, and users only come if search is free, which advertisers subsidize. Both segments are needed.
- **Apple iOS**: smartphone users come for the device, design, technology, and increasingly the number and quality of applications. Application developers come not just for the technology but only if there are a lot of users, attracted by the number of people on the platform and their ability and willingness to pay.
- **Mastercard**: buyers come if the card works, purchasing is easy, and it is accepted in many stores worldwide. Merchants come for a different value proposition: a large base of users willing to spend money. Merchants come for the number of buyers; buyers come for the number of shops.
- A **credit card company** needs a large base of card holders and a large base of merchants; a **free newspaper** needs a large reader base to attract advertisers and needs advertisers to finance production and distribution.

More complicated multi-sided markets have several segments and several value propositions.

#### B2B: several customers inside one organization

In business to business you are likely serving several people within one organization, each needing a distinct value proposition. Using Steve Blank and Bob Dorf's customer and user types (illustrated with selling MS Office through the cloud):
- **Users (end users)**: the people who will actually use the service.
- **Influencers**: have some say in the purchase, perhaps no formal influence, but some power in the decision.
- **Recommenders**: more formal power; they can make or break a sale.
- **Economic buyers**: control the budget and invest the money.
- **Decision makers**: hold the final decision-making power.
- **Saboteurs**: people or organizations that could undermine the purchase even without a direct interest in it. This group is often forgotten.

#### Frequently asked questions

**Why are free users a customer segment?** Users of Google search have a choice: come to Google or go elsewhere. As soon as you have to attract people with a value proposition, they are a customer segment, whether or not they pay. Here it matters especially because the number of users is itself the value proposition to advertisers.

**Can a key partner also be a customer segment?** Google bought Android in 2005, believing more and more search would move to mobile. Initially Google worked only with partners like Samsung who manufactured the phones sold to smartphone users (today Google also offers its own phones). But Samsung is also a customer segment, because it has a choice whether to use Android or another operating system, in particular its own. Whenever you have to attract someone with your value proposition, they are a customer segment.

Closing question to ask yourself: who do you want to create value for, and what jobs are you helping them get done?

### 2.2 Value Propositions

**What it is**: The center of the business model, always defined in relation to a specific customer segment. A value proposition outlines how a bundle of products and services creates value for a specific segment. It helps customers address a fundamental problem or need (a job to be done), kills their pains, and creates gains or outcomes for them. It is the reason customers turn to one company over another, and what attracts them to do business with you. Some value propositions are innovative and disruptive; others resemble existing offers with added features and attributes. Values can be quantitative (price, speed of service) or qualitative (design, customer experience).

**The key message: it is not the product or technology per se.**

**Example 1, Nestlé yogurt**: Saying the product is what Nestlé offers consumers is not correct; the product is a key resource. The value proposition is that it creates a gentle and natural regulation of digestion. It addresses a real customer pain (constipation) and a customer job (a healthy bowel movement). Addressing this specific pain and job is what made the brand take off.

**Example 2, the hygiene company workshop**: At a global hygiene product company, the B2B team for washroom installations worldwide had a canvas that said they offered "hygiene solutions" to three segments: facilities managers, washroom users, and wholesalers. Asked for each segment's job:
- **Facilities managers**: replace the toilet paper, do it at very low cost, keep the facilities clean.
- **Washroom users**: wash their hands, dry their hands, and leave.
- **Wholesalers**: sell as much as they can.

Three very different jobs, but one "value proposition." It clicked immediately: "hygiene solutions" is not a value proposition. It is what they have as key resources (technologies and products), and they need to build a specific value proposition around those resources for each segment. Your product or technology is not your value proposition; the value proposition is how specifically you create value for a segment.

**Value drivers**, grouped by the job they help with (each only matters if it is relevant to your customers):

*Functional jobs*
- **Performance**: it performs better than anything else out there. Improving performance has been a traditional way to create value, and the PC sector long relied on it, but performance has limits: faster PCs, more disk space, and better graphics eventually failed to produce corresponding growth in demand.
- **Getting the job done better**: value can come simply from helping customers get jobs done. Rolls-Royce's airline customers rely entirely on Rolls-Royce to manufacture and service their jet engines, letting them focus on running their airlines; in return, airlines pay a fee for every hour an engine runs.
- **Customization**: tailoring products and services to individual customers or segments. Mass customization and customer co-creation allow customized offers while still taking advantage of economies of scale.
- **Accessibility**: giving access to something that wasn't accessible before, through business model innovation, new technology, or both. Amazon Web Services gives access to infrastructure that was previously only accessible to large companies. NetJets popularized fractional private jet ownership, giving individuals and corporations access to private jets previously unaffordable to most. Mutual funds made diversified portfolios possible for people of modest wealth.
- **Convenience/usability**: making things easier to use. With iPod and iTunes, Apple offered unprecedented convenience in searching, buying, downloading, and listening to digital music, and came to dominate the market.
- **Price**: similar value at a lower price for price-sensitive segments. Low price has big implications for the rest of the business model: no-frills airlines such as Southwest, easyJet, and Ryanair designed entire business models to enable low-cost air travel; Tata's Nano made a car affordable to a whole new segment of the Indian population. Free offers are spreading across industries, from free newspapers to free e-mail and free mobile phone services.
- **Cost reduction**: helping customers reduce their costs. Salesforce.com's hosted CRM relieves buyers of the expense and trouble of buying, installing, and managing CRM software, reducing their cost of ownership of IT infrastructure.

*Social jobs*
- **Status / brand**: customers value using and displaying a brand. A Rolex signifies wealth; at the other end, skateboarders wear the latest "underground" brands to show they are "in."
- **Better communication**.
- **Being fashionable**.
- **Newness**: satisfying an entirely new set of needs customers didn't perceive before because there was no similar offering. Often, but not always, technology related: cell phones created a whole industry around mobile telecommunication, while ethical investment funds have little to do with technology.

*Emotional jobs*
- **Risk reduction**: customers value reduced purchase risk. A one-year service guarantee reduces a used-car buyer's risk of breakdowns and repairs; a service-level guarantee partly reduces the risk of buying outsourced IT services; you can reduce a customer's worry about not getting the lowest price.
- **Design**: important but hard to measure; a product can stand out through superior design, particularly in fashion and consumer electronics.
- **Emotional state**: helping customers feel a certain way.

**Key questions**: What value do we deliver to the customer? Which of our customers' problems are we helping to solve? Which customer needs are we satisfying? What bundles of products and services are we offering to each segment?

**Common mistake: overloading the value proposition block.** People put a lot of information in this block to show off how they create value. But the canvas is about the big picture of how you create, deliver, and capture value, not one block. Put the details in the Value Proposition Canvas, give the value proposition a title there, and put only that title on the Business Model Canvas. Use the Business Model Canvas to answer how you create value profitably; use the Value Proposition Canvas to answer in detail how you address customer jobs, pains, and gains.

### 2.3 Channels

**What it is**: What connects your organization with your customers: customer touch points or interfaces through which you communicate with customers and, in particular, deliver your value proposition. Communication, distribution, and sales channels make up the company's customer interface and play an important role in the customer experience.

**Functions of channels**:
- raising awareness of the company's products and services;
- helping customers evaluate the value proposition;
- allowing customers to purchase specific products and services;
- delivering the value proposition;
- providing post-purchase customer support.

**The five channel phases** (each channel can cover some or all of them):
1. **Awareness**: How do we raise awareness about our products and services?
2. **Evaluation**: How do we help customers evaluate our value proposition?
3. **Purchase**: How do we allow customers to purchase specific products and services?
4. **Delivery**: How do we deliver the value proposition to customers?
5. **After sales**: How do we provide post-purchase customer support?

**Channel types**:
- **Direct channels**: your own stores, websites, mobile channels, a sales force, key account managers in B2B, and, especially in B2B, your senior executives, who are also a channel to customers.
- **Indirect channels**: the partners you use to reach customers: third-party stores, websites, and mobile channels, a hired professional sales force, wholesalers, and in particular media partners for communication.
- **Owned versus partner**: owned channels can be direct (in-house sales force, website) or indirect (retail stores owned or operated by the organization). Partner channels are indirect: wholesale distribution, retail, partner-owned websites.

**The trade-off**: partner channels lead to lower margins but let you expand reach and benefit from partner strengths. Owned channels, particularly direct ones, have higher margins but can be costly to set up and operate. The trick is to find the right balance, integrate channels into a great customer experience, and maximize revenues.

**Example**: Nestlé sells yogurts through retailers (an indirect channel) to end consumers. But the retailer channel is also a customer, so Nestlé needs another, direct channel, a sales force, to reach the retailers. This combines indirect and direct channels to reach the market.

**Key questions**: Through which channels do our segments want to be reached? How are we reaching them now? How are our channels integrated? Which work best? Which are most cost-efficient? How are we integrating them with customer routines? Better than asking how you can reach customers: ask how your customers want to be reached.

### 2.4 Customer Relationships

**What it is**: The link you establish between your organization and each customer segment: the nature of the relationship, specifying how you acquire, retain, and grow customers. A relationship is based on a channel. The relationships your business model calls for deeply influence the overall customer experience.

**Motivations**: customer acquisition, customer retention, and boosting sales (upselling). Early mobile network operators drove relationships through aggressive acquisition with free mobile phones; when the market saturated, they switched to retention and increasing average revenue per customer.

**Four themes for defining a relationship** (each a continuum):

1. **Type (indirect to direct)**: from customers dealing only with intermediaries to customers dealing only with you. In coffee, Nestlé's Nescafé brand has an indirect relationship: it sells through retailers. Nespresso has a mixed one: indirect through stores for the machines, direct for the pods.
2. **Bond (transactional to long-term)**: a transactional relationship is a loose bond; it is easy for customers to leave. A long, tight relationship makes leaving hard. Nescafé had a very transactional relationship: nothing held a customer back from switching coffee brands. Nespresso turned a transactional industry into one with a long-term bond by locking customers in: once you bought the machine you couldn't easily leave without buying a new machine.
3. **Intimacy (automated to personal)**: from very automated, low-touch relationships where customers deal only with machines, to very high-touch, personal relationships where the same people deal with the same customers and know each other well. UBS Wealth Management (a private bank) sits at the personal end; web companies like Amazon at the automated end. But Amazon isn't just automated: its personalized services mean it knows you pretty well, so there is a certain intimacy even though you deal with machines.
4. **Lifecycle (acquire, retain, grow)**: acquisition strategies (how do you get customers?), retention strategies (how do you keep them in your sphere of influence?), and growth (how do you turn them into better customers who buy more from you?).

**Categories of relationships** (several may co-exist for one segment):
- **Personal assistance**: human interaction; customers can reach a real representative during the sale or after purchase, on site, through call centers, by e-mail, or other means.
- **Dedicated personal assistance**: a representative dedicated to an individual client; the deepest, most intimate type, normally built over a long period. Private bankers serving high net worth individuals; key account managers maintaining personal relationships with important customers.
- **Self-service**: no direct relationship; the company provides all the means for customers to help themselves.
- **Automated services**: a more sophisticated self-service mixed with automated processes. Personal online profiles give access to customized services; automated services can recognize individual customers and their characteristics and offer order or transaction information. At their best they simulate a personal relationship (book or movie recommendations).
- **Communities**: user communities to get more involved with customers and prospects and connect members, who exchange knowledge and solve each other's problems. Communities also help companies understand customers: GlaxoSmithKline launched a private online community when it introduced alli, a prescription-free weight-loss product, to understand the challenges faced by overweight adults and better manage customer expectations.
- **Co-creation**: going beyond the traditional customer-vendor relationship to co-create value. Amazon.com invites customers to write reviews that create value for other book lovers; some companies involve customers in designing new products; YouTube.com solicits customers to create content for public consumption.

**Key questions**: What type of relationship does each segment expect us to establish and maintain? Which have we established? How costly are they? How are they integrated with the rest of our business model? Think about customer relationships strategically.

### 2.5 Revenue Streams

**What it is**: How you earn money from a particular customer segment: how the business model captures value in return for the value it creates. Revenue is an outcome of the choices you make on the front stage. If customers are the heart of a business model, revenue streams are its arteries. Revenue should always be bigger than the cost structure; that sounds obvious, but the canvas lets you show it on one page. (Costs must be subtracted from revenues to create earnings.)

**The central question**: For what value is each customer segment truly willing to pay? Answering it lets you generate one or more revenue streams per segment.

**Two kinds of revenue**: transaction revenues from one-time customer payments, and recurring revenues from ongoing payments to deliver a value proposition or provide post-purchase support.

**Types of revenue streams**, each shown with how it looks on the canvas and with an example from the car business:

- **Asset sale**: revenue from transferring ownership rights to a tangible or intangible product. *Canvas*: an asset (key resource) is packaged into a value proposition offered to a buyer, who pays a sales fee in exchange for the asset; ownership rights transfer. *Cars*: the car industry sells millions of cars a year. Other examples: Amazon.com sells books, music, and electronics; Fiat sells cars that buyers are free to drive, resell, or even destroy. *Important*: this is transactional revenue, so you have to sell again and again and again.
- **Rental / lending / leasing**: temporarily granting someone the exclusive right to use a particular asset for a fixed period in return for a fee. *Canvas*: the asset stays a key resource; the value proposition is the rental, offered to a renter who pays a rental fee. *Cars*: car rental. Example: Zipcar.com rents cars by the hour in North American cities, and has led many people to rent rather than buy. *Why it's interesting*: the asset remains the organization's property and produces recurring revenue; renters enjoy incurring expenses only for a limited time rather than bearing the full cost of ownership.
- **Usage fee**: revenue from use of a particular service; the more you use it, the more you pay. *Canvas*: a service-producing asset (key resource) delivers the service (value proposition) to a buyer who pays a metered usage fee. *Cars*: taxis. Others: a telecom operator charging per minute, a hotel per night, a package delivery service per parcel. The asset remains property of the organization and produces recurring revenue.
- **Licensing**: giving customers permission to use protected intellectual property in exchange for licensing fees. *Canvas*: IP as key resource; the value proposition is the permission to use it, offered to a licensee who pays a licensing fee. *Cars/technology*: licensing fees from IP found in many products. Licensing lets rights holders earn from their property without manufacturing a product or commercializing a service; common in media (content owners keep copyright and sell usage licenses) and technology (patent holders license patented technology). *Why it's interesting*: the IP remains yours and produces recurring and very scalable revenue.
- **Advertising**: fees for giving visibility to a particular product, service, brand, or other item. *Canvas*: two key resources, the advertising channel and the audience, packaged into a value proposition (access to the audience) offered to an advertiser who pays an advertising fee. *Cars*: advertising on taxis. Traditionally media and event organizers relied heavily on advertising; software and services increasingly do. *Caveat*: the channel remains yours, but you also need a sizable, high-quality audience or you can't generate advertising fees.
- **Subscription fee**: selling continuous access to a service. *Canvas*: an asset offered as a service to subscribers who pay a subscription fee. *Cars*: magazines. Others: a gym selling monthly or yearly memberships; World of Warcraft Online charging a monthly fee to play; Nokia's Comes with Music selling access to a music library. *Why it's interesting*: recurring and often very predictable revenue.
- **Brokerage fee**: revenue from an intermediation service performed for two or more parties. *Canvas*: a seller and a buyer; the value proposition is the intermediation; the intermediary collects a brokerage or transaction fee from one party, the other, or both. *Cars*: an insurance broker brokering between insurer and driver. Others: credit card providers taking a percentage of each transaction between merchants and customers; brokers and real estate agents earning a commission each time they match buyer and seller. You will often see one revenue stream subsidizing another.

**Pricing mechanisms**: distinct from revenue streams; a pricing mechanism is how the price is established, and each is tied to a revenue stream. The choice can make a big difference to revenues. Two categories:

*Fixed ("menu") pricing: price set in advance on static variables*
- **List price**: fixed prices for individual products, services, or value propositions (a car's list price).
- **Product feature dependent**: price depends on the number or quality of features; the more features in a car, the higher the price, but each feature price is fixed in advance.
- **Volume dependent**: price as a function of quantity; ten cars cost less per car than one.
- **Customer segment dependent**: price depends on the type and characteristics of a segment; the same car sold at a different price in countries with different purchasing power, still fixed in advance.

*Dynamic pricing: price set on the spot based on market conditions*
- **Negotiation (bargaining)**: price discussed and adapted between two or more parties, depending on negotiating power and skill. The oldest mechanism. Found in flea markets and, at the other end, industrial goods: the higher the price, the more likely it is negotiated.
- **Yield management**: price depends on inventory and time of purchase; used for perishable resources. An airline seat is worthless once the flight has left, so airlines price by seats available and time of purchase (buy far ahead or the day before and you pay very different prices). A hotel room night can't be sold after it passes, so hotels use yield management to optimize room sales and prices.
- **Real-time market**: price set dynamically in real time by supply and demand. Only works with many buyers, many sellers, and a large volume of transactions; the stock market is the classic case.
- **Auction**: price determined by competitive bidding. This turned Google into a very successful company: rather than just selling advertising, advertisers compete for search terms in automated auctions, which drives prices up.

**Key questions**: For what value are our customers really willing to pay? For what do they currently pay? How are they currently paying? How would they prefer to pay? How much does each revenue stream contribute to overall revenues?

**Common mistake: the orphan revenue stream.** Make sure you define exactly who pays and for what. A common sketch: free services sold through the web to users, "financed through advertising." Advertising here is an orphan: users aren't paying for advertising, and they aren't paying advertising for a free service. The correct model has a free revenue stream from users and an advertising revenue stream paid by advertisers for a specific value proposition. The business model only works with two segments and a clear value proposition for each. Think hard about the right revenue stream and pricing mechanism, and don't hesitate to experiment with different possibilities.

### 2.6 Key Resources

**What it is**: The first element of the backstage (the left side), which makes the front stage possible. Key resources are the most important things (assets) you need to have for your business model to work. They let you create and offer a value proposition, reach markets, maintain relationships, and earn revenues. Different business models need different resources: a microchip manufacturer requires capital-intensive production facilities, while a microchip designer focuses more on human resources. Key resources can be owned, leased, or acquired from key partners.

**Tangible resources**:
- **Physical**: manufacturing facilities, buildings, vehicles, machines, systems, point-of-sale systems, distribution networks. Intel has very expensive semiconductor plants; a chip company rarely survives without adequate infrastructure. Wal-Mart has an enormous global network of stores and logistics; Amazon.com an extensive IT, warehouse, and logistics infrastructure. Physical resources are often capital-intensive.
- **Human**: every enterprise needs people, but they are especially prominent in some models, notably knowledge-intensive and creative industries. UBS Wealth Management needs great bankers, because bankers establish the trust and relationship with clients. Novartis relies on an army of experienced scientists and a large, skilled sales force.
- **Financial**: cash, lines of credit, or a stock option pool for hiring key employees. A life insurer needs capital to cover claims; an insurer rarely survives without sufficient capital. Ericsson may borrow from banks and capital markets and use part of the proceeds to provide vendor financing to equipment customers, ensuring orders go to Ericsson rather than competitors.

**Intangible resources**:
- **Intellectual property and trademarks**: brands, proprietary knowledge, patents, copyrights, partnerships, customer databases. Hard to develop, but valuable once created. Google acquired Motorola because it needed a patent portfolio to survive the patent wars with other tech companies. Microsoft and SAP depend on software and IP built over many years. Qualcomm built its business model around patented microchip designs that earn substantial licensing fees.
- **Brand**: important for every business, but critical for some. Sony with the PlayStation: in consumer electronics you can't survive without a brand. Nike and Sony rely heavily on brand as a key resource.
- **Traffic**: for Facebook, relying on advertising, traffic is the product it sells to advertisers.
- **User base**: for Apple's iOS platform, users are the value proposition to application developers; you can't survive in a platform business without a sufficient user base.
- **Trust**: in Credit Suisse wealth management, clients' trust is essential; private banking without trust is not possible.

**Key questions**: What key resources do our value propositions require? Our distribution channels? Customer relationships? Revenue streams?

**Common mistake: getting too granular.** People tend to put many things in this box, and not all are essential. Ask: what is essential to my business model? What really matters? You can add detail when implementing, but when thinking strategy, focus on essentials. Example: great people are essential for every business, but not necessarily a key building block of every business model. UPS needs great managers and people to implement its model, but they are not a key element of the business model.

**Challenge your key resources.** Bharti Airtel, one of the biggest mobile operators in the world, based in India, had a business model based on network and IT infrastructure; the whole industry believed these were key resources. Bharti asked: what if we didn't have to own these? It outsourced them to partners, which let it focus on brand and customers. Challenging a key resource changed the way it did business. Keep asking which resources really drive your business, and challenge that belief constantly.

### 2.7 Key Activities

**What it is**: The second building block of the backstage: the most important things you must be able to perform, the things you need to excel at, for your business model to work. Like key resources, they are required to create and offer a value proposition, reach markets, maintain relationships, and earn revenues, and they differ by business model type. For Microsoft, software development; for Dell, supply chain management; for McKinsey, problem solving.

**Why it matters (example)**: Kiva, a microfinance platform, connects lenders with businesses in developing countries. Risk management is a key activity: you cannot run a lending business without good risk management. At the beginning, Kiva neglected it. Identify the activities that make or break your business model.

**Three activity configurations** (non-exhaustive):

1. **Production (value chains)**: designing, making, and delivering a product in substantial quantities and/or superior quality; dominates manufacturing, such as car manufacturing. *Canvas*: production activities in key activities, production facilities in key resources, products sold to buyers with ownership transferred. The process is linear and sequential, pushing products from left to right. It is very replicable; the aim is replicability and scale.
2. **Problem solving (value shops)**, a term from academics Stabell and Fjeldstad: coming up with new solutions to individual customer problems; dominates consultancies, hospitals, doctors, and other service organizations. *Canvas*: problem-solving activities, talented people as key resources, consulting or advice as the value proposition, targeted at clients. The client explains a unique problem; the business takes it on and tries to solve it. The process is cyclical and very iterative: attempt, check with the client, repeat. Such models call for knowledge management and continuous training. *Caveat*: often limited in replicability and scalability, and very time dependent.
3. **Platform/network (value networks)**: models with a platform as key resource are dominated by platform-related activities: platform management, service provisioning, and platform promotion. Networks, matchmaking platforms, software, and even brands can function as platforms. Credit cards: the activity is managing the network; the key resource is the platform; the value proposition is connecting and mediating between merchants and buyers. eBay must continually develop and maintain eBay.com; Visa must run its transaction platform for merchants, customers, and banks; Microsoft must manage the interface between other vendors' software and Windows. *Trade-off*: often very high upfront investment because the platform must be built first; but network effects make a successful model very hard to copy.

**Key questions**: What key activities do our value propositions require? Our distribution channels? Customer relationships? Revenue streams?

**Common mistake: too much granularity.** People fill the box with HR, accounting, project management. That's fine when talking implementation, but these are probably not the key activities that make or break the model. Too much granularity is like fog: it hides the strategic story. Ask what you really need to excel at.

### 2.8 Key Partnerships

**What it is**: The block at the far left: the network of partners and suppliers that leverage your business model and help make it work. Partnerships are becoming a cornerstone of many business models. It is illogical for a company to own all resources or perform every activity itself.

**Four types of partnership**:
1. **Strategic alliances between non-competitors**: Nike and Apple created a running shoe that integrates with the iPod so you can track your performance while running.
2. **Coopetition (partnerships between competitors)**: common in the car industry, often sharing the backstage: R&D, production, and so on. Blu-ray was jointly developed by a group of leading consumer electronics, PC, and media manufacturers who cooperated to bring the technology to market while competing in selling their own Blu-ray products.
3. **Joint ventures to develop new businesses**: Nespresso began as a joint venture between Nestlé, the world's biggest food company, and coffee machine manufacturers, creating a new growth industry: single-portion espresso.
4. **Buyer-supplier relationships to assure reliable supplies**: Intel Inside. Intel was a simple supplier, but by focusing on the brand it became a very strong supplier and partner to many computer manufacturers.

**Three motivations**:
1. **Optimization and economies of scale**: the most basic form, designed to optimize the allocation of resources and activities; usually to reduce costs, often via outsourcing or shared infrastructure. When General Mills expanded in Europe, instead of building its own distribution and brands it partnered with Nestlé, a strong, tight relationship that helped both grow.
2. **Reduction of risk and uncertainty**: Google created Android as a platform for partners so the web wouldn't go mobile without Google; if people searched on the mobile web without Google, where would its revenues come from? Competitors also form alliances in one area while competing in another (Blu-ray).
3. **Acquisition of particular resources and activities**: few companies own all the resources or perform all the activities; they rely on others for knowledge, licenses, or access to customers. Starbucks needs a lot of coffee, so strong supplier relationships are crucial. A mobile phone maker may license an operating system rather than build one; an insurer may rely on independent brokers rather than its own sales force.

**Networks of business models**: it is increasingly rare that a business model exists in isolation. To build a successful network you need to understand your partners' business models, because no network survives without a win-win for all partners.

**Key questions**: Who are our key partners? Who are our key suppliers? Which key resources are we acquiring from partners? Which key activities do partners perform? How could partners leverage our business model, and what win-win situations help everybody succeed and grow?

### 2.9 Cost Structure

**What it is**: All costs incurred to operate the business model; an outcome of the choices you make in the backstage. Creating and delivering value, maintaining relationships, and generating revenue all incur costs, which can be calculated relatively easily once key resources, activities, and partnerships are defined. Costs should always be smaller than revenues, or the model isn't sustainable.

**Fixed versus variable costs**:
- **Fixed costs** are independent of the volume of goods and services produced: salaries, rents, physical manufacturing facilities. Some businesses, such as manufacturers, have a high proportion of fixed costs. *Pharmaceutical example*: very high R&D costs and a sales force distributing the inventions (high sales and marketing costs), both fixed and very high; the manufacturing of the drugs is a variable cost that can be very cheap. So a pharma company's main costs are fixed.
- **Variable costs** vary with the volume of goods and services produced and sold. *Retail example*: fixed costs exist (a network of stores if you don't sell online; usually high sales and marketing), but variable costs, mainly purchasing goods from partners (cost of goods sold), are higher. The proportion of variable costs in retail is often much higher than fixed. Music festivals are another business with a high proportion of variable costs.

**Turning fixed costs into variable costs (Airtel)**: As a mobile operator, Airtel's key activities included designing and building the network; network and IT infrastructure meant very high fixed costs and high capital expenditure. Bharti asked what would happen if it didn't own these. It outsourced network and IT infrastructure to a consortium through vendor contracts, so backstage costs became purely variable: it only paid for the mobile services it actually offered customers. This shift from fixed to variable costs changed the way Airtel could do business.

**Cost-driven versus value-driven models** (many fall between the extremes):
- **Cost-driven**: focus on minimizing costs wherever possible, usually because you compete on price. The aim is the leanest possible cost structure, using low-price value propositions, maximum automation, and extensive outsourcing. Low-cost (no-frills) carriers such as Southwest, easyJet, and Ryanair do everything in their business model to keep costs low; the backstage is focused on driving costs down.
- **Value-driven**: less concerned with cost, focused on the high end and on value creation: premium value propositions and a high degree of personalized service. Luxury hotels with lavish facilities and exclusive services are typical. Because they are so value focused, concentrating on cost could be harmful: it might reduce the premium value being created.

**Other sources of cost advantage**:
- **Economies of scale**: cost advantages that grow with size; average cost per unit falls as output rises. Costco Wholesale, one of the biggest retailers in the world, has such purchasing power that it drives prices down. Larger companies benefit from lower bulk purchase rates.
- **Economies of scope**: cost advantages from a larger scope of operations; certain components support several value propositions and segments. In a large enterprise the same marketing activities or distribution channels may support multiple products. Nestlé's pod-based products: a whole range of machines producing pods, with synergies across very different businesses.

**Key questions**: What are the most important costs inherent in our business model? Which key resources are most expensive? Which key activities are most expensive? Understand what drives your costs, and whether you should be cost-driven or value-driven.

## 3. Worked Business Model Examples

These examples show how to read a business model as a whole on the canvas. Each is used again later to illustrate specific mechanics (switching costs, recurring revenues, platforms).

### Nespresso: changing the business model for coffee

**Value proposition**: the best coffee at home, as good as the coffee in the best café in Rome (a "barista experience at home"). It consists mainly of two products: the Nespresso machine and the capsules (pods).

**Front stage**:
- **Machines**: sold through retailers to households and eventually businesses. This generates revenue from machine sales, but most of that revenue goes directly to the machine manufacturers, which have been key partners for Nespresso from the beginning.
- **Capsules**: a different story. Nespresso uses its own channels: mail order, the website, and more recently Nespresso stores in most big cities in the world. These direct channels are a key resource because they give Nespresso the ability to reach customers directly.
- **Lock-in**: once you have bought a machine, you have to come back to Nespresso to buy capsules; you can only use Nespresso capsules in the machine. This lock-in strategy makes it much harder to change supplier. Nespresso reinforces it with the Nespresso Club to keep the relationship with customers, and defends it with patents (a key resource).
- **Revenue**: the repeat sales of capsules are the main revenue for Nespresso.

**Backstage**:
- **Coffee**: sourced through partnerships with coffee growers, which is normal for Nestlé's business.
- **Production**: Nespresso keeps the manufacturing activity of filling capsules; its production facilities produce millions of capsules per day, which is a cost.
- **Marketing and branding**: activities and brand resources, which have a cost.
- **Distribution/logistics**: the third key activity, because direct distribution has to reach customers; logistics is a cost.

### Google search: a business model built on free

The essence (the blueprint of the strategy), without details:

**Segment 1, users**:
- **Value proposition**: helping us find information. No value proposition is independent of a segment: Google targets users globally. (When did you last do a Google search? Probably in the last 24 hours.)
- **Channel**: Google.com.
- **Relationship**: 100% automated. You can't just call Google and ask something.
- **Revenue**: nothing. Google is free; this is a business model based on a free service.

**Backstage**:
- **Key activities**: software development and network development by developers; behind Google is a very substantial IT infrastructure.
- **Key resources**: intellectual property, something easily forgotten. Google started with very specific algorithms that let it offer better information-finding on the web.
- **Key partners** (kept simple): the telecom infrastructure providers that supply the pipes to the Internet.
- **Costs**: mainly IT and the bandwidth of the service.

This is not yet a good business model, because everything is free. So:

**Segment 2, advertisers**:
- **Value proposition**: an audience to reach and sell to through advertising (targeted search ads and access to a global audience).
- **Key resource**: a large base of users. Without it, there is no value proposition to advertisers.
- **Channel**: also the Internet, but through Google AdWords, its advertising program.
- **Relationship**: also automated, which makes the business model very scalable.
- **Revenue**: not simply "advertising" but keyword auctions: auctioning off search terms is what made Google very wealthy.

### Apple iPod/iTunes: competing with a better business model

In 2001 Apple launched the iPod, which works with iTunes software to transfer music and other content to a computer and connects seamlessly to Apple's online store, where users buy and download content. This combination of device, software, and online store quickly disrupted the music industry and gave Apple a dominant position. Yet Apple was not first to market: competitors such as Diamond Multimedia, with its Rio portable media players, were successful until Apple outpaced them.

Apple won because it competed with a better business model. On one hand it offered users a seamless music experience combining the distinctively designed iPod, iTunes software, and the iTunes store; the value proposition is to let customers easily search, buy, and enjoy digital music. On the other hand, to make that possible Apple had to negotiate deals with all the major record companies to create the world's largest online music library. The twist: Apple earns most of its music-related revenue from selling iPods, while using the integration with the online store to protect itself from competitors.

### The business model of the book *Business Model Generation*

The authors decided they couldn't credibly write a book about business model innovation without an innovative business model for the book itself.

**Origins**: 2004, Osterwalder completes the PhD with Pigneur at HEC Lausanne. 2006, the approach spreads via Osterwalder's business model blog, notably at 3M, Ericsson, Deloitte, and Telenor. At a workshop in the Netherlands, Patrick van der Pijl asks why there is no book accompanying the method.

**The model**: they ditched publishers and launched the Hub, an online platform sharing their writing from day one. Anyone interested could join for a fee (initially U.S. $24, gradually raised to U.S. $243 to keep the platform exclusive; recounted elsewhere as rising from $24 to $250). This and other revenue streams financed production in advance, so they earned before spending on design, printing, and sales. The book itself broke the format of conventional strategy books: co-created, highly visual, and complemented by exercises and workshop tips. Key audience: visionary and game-changing entrepreneurs, consultants, and executives.

**Process**: the core team first sketched the business model of the book. Content was released in "chunks" to the Hub community for feedback; the writing became completely transparent; content, design, illustrations, and structure were shared and commented on worldwide, and the core team responded to every comment. A soft launch in Amsterdam let Hub members meet; sketching participants' business models became the core exercise of the day. Two hundred limited-edition prototypes of the unfinished book were printed.

**Tools used**: strategy (environmental scanning, Business Model Canvas, customer empathy map); content and R&D (customer insights, case studies); open process (online platform, co-creation, access to unfinished work, commenting and feedback); design (open design process, moodboards, paper mockups, visualization, illustration, photography).

**The numbers**: 9 years of research and practice; 470 co-authors; 45 countries; 19 book chunks; 8 prototypes; 200 copies of a messed-up test print; 77 forum discussions; 287 Skype calls; 1,360 comments; 137,757 views of the method online before publishing; 13.18 GB of content; 28,456 Post-it notes; 4,000+ hours of work; 521 photos.

**The canvas of the book**:
- *Key partners*: the design agency, the platform provider, Amazon.com, a third-party logistics company, publishers.
- *Key activities*: content production, Hub management, guerrilla marketing and word of mouth, logistics and shipping.
- *Key resources*: the business model Hub, a powerful methodology, Hub members, the blog and web visibility.
- *Value propositions*: a visual, practical, beautiful handbook for business model innovators; co-creation of a potential bestseller; personalized books for companies and their customers; a business model event in Amsterdam.
- *Relationships*: the Hub (businessmodelhub.com), word of mouth.
- *Channels*: businessmodelgeneration.com, Amazon.com, bookstores, intermediation through publishers.
- *Segments*: visionaries, game changers, and challengers; entrepreneurs, executives, consultants, academics; companies.
- *Costs*: design, content production, printing, distribution.
- *Revenues*: Hub membership fees, advance and post-publication sales, a free giveaway of the canvas section, fees for customized versions, royalties from publishers.

**Why it works**: *Differentiation*: an entirely different format, business model, and story make it stand out in a crowded market. *Revenues*: financed through advance sales and co-creator fees, plus customized versions for companies and their clients. *Production and logistics*: everything beyond content creation is outsourced to readily available providers. *Buyers*: paying customers are not only readers but co-creators and companies wanting customized books. *Reach*: a mix of direct and indirect channels and a phased approach optimizes reach and margins; the story lends itself to viral marketing. *Community*: co-authors from around the world feel ownership thanks to attribution.

## 4. Business Model and Value Proposition Fit

### The Value Proposition Canvas

**What it is**: A tool to intentionally visualize, design, and test how you create value for customers, just as the Business Model Canvas does for how you create value for your business. It has two parts: the customer profile and the value map.

**Why it matters**: 72% of new product and service innovations fail to deliver on expectations: customers don't care about seven out of ten new products introduced to the market. It doesn't have to be this way.

**The customer profile** (the circle, for a customer segment):
- **Customer jobs**: what customers are trying to get done. Functional (getting from A to B), social (impressing friends and colleagues), or emotional (gaining peace of mind).
- **Pains**: what annoys customers while trying to get a job done. Negative outcomes they hope to avoid: dissatisfaction with existing solutions, and challenges, frustrations, risks, or obstacles related to performing a job.
- **Gains**: how customers measure the success of a job well done. Positive outcomes they hope to achieve: concrete results, benefits, and even aspirations.

Use the profile to visualize, test, and track your understanding of the people or companies you intend to create value for. It is a map that becomes clearer the more you learn about customers.

**The value map** (the square, for the value proposition):
- **Products and services** your value proposition builds on.
- **Pain relievers**: how products, services, and features eliminate, reduce, or minimize pains customers care about, making their life easier.
- **Gain creators**: how they produce, increase, or maximize outcomes and benefits customers expect, desire, or would be surprised by.

Use the value map to design, test, and iterate your value proposition until you find what resonates.

**Fit**: achieved by creating a clear connection between what matters to customers and how your products, services, and features ease pains and create gains. Great value propositions target essential jobs, pains, and gains and do so extremely well. Your profile may contain countless jobs, pains, and gains; the value map highlights which ones you intend to focus on.

**Caveat**: an outstanding value proposition can still fail if the business model is flawed. Successful companies embed outstanding value propositions in scalable and profitable business models.

### Create value for your customer and for your business

A great value proposition lives in a sustainable, profitable, scalable business model; it is not enough to focus on the value proposition alone, however innovative. The logic runs both ways: to create value for your customers you need to create value for your business model, and to create value for your business model you need to create value for customers, because without customers there is no value for the business. Embedding the value proposition in a business model means, for example, finding the right channel to reach customers, having profitable revenue streams, perhaps acquiring new resources, or forming strategic partnerships so the business model succeeds with the value proposition.

### Going back and forth between business model and value proposition

**What it is**: Zooming out from the value proposition to find the business model that delivers it profitably, and zooming in from the business model to find or adjust the detailed value proposition, repeating until both work.

**Why it matters**: A company only succeeds if it has a profitable, scalable business model as well as a value proposition customers want. Sometimes you must zoom out and ask how you will deliver the value proposition profitably; if you can't, zoom back in and change the value proposition to create a profitable model.

**Example: Hilti.**

*Original business model*: Hilti makes high-end light machine tools for builders on construction sites. It sold through a sales force and certain stores, with a one-to-one direct relationship, and earned transactional revenue from tool sales. Backstage: manufacturing, selling, and branding (relying on a great brand name), with some manufacturing partners. Resources, activities, and partners gave the main costs.

*The problem*: low-cost manufacturing. Hilti was no longer competitive.

*Understanding the customer*: the customer's main job wasn't really buying tools; it was managing, maintaining, and replacing them: having the right tool at the right place at the right time. Hilti shifted focus from construction workers to CEOs of construction companies and mapped their profile:
- **Jobs**: finding and executing contracts, respecting deadlines, which means fleet planning and scheduling to avoid delays.
- **Pains**: upfront investment in tools, broken tools, theft, and delays that lead to penalties and stress.
- **Gains**: profitable contracts, access to the newest tools, safety, uptime, and predictable costs.

*The new value proposition* (the pitch): "Have you ever wanted to just focus on your work and forget about administrative issues and managing your tool fleet? If so, Tool Fleet Management, Hilti's long-term tool usage service, is for you. We take care of your tools while you focus on the job. Hilti helps assure that your team will have the best tools, each unique and labeled using RFID and an inventory number so you can maintain control of your tool fleet. If something breaks down, no problem. Hilti has another way to make your work easier: we repair or replace your tools quickly at no additional cost. Hilti will also update your tools on a regular basis, always with the latest generation models. All this on a single monthly invoice."

*Value map*: pain relievers: no more costs for repair and replacement; subscription, so no upfront investment. Gain creators: access to a great fleet of tools, and sound cost management. A great fit.

*Zooming out to the business model*: overlay the new value proposition on the old canvas (like tracing paper) and ask which elements it affects. Hilti turned into a service-centric company:
- **Value proposition and segment**: an online fleet management system targeted at construction company CEOs.
- **Channels**: salespeople who used to sell to construction workers had to be trained to sell to CEOs; online fleet management also meant a new, online channel.
- **Revenue**: the real value to Hilti is that construction companies sign large, long-term contracts: a move from transactional sales to large, long-term contracts.
- **Key activities**: changed substantially. On top of manufacturing, Hilti must now manage a service fleet, a very different business from manufacturing.
- **Key resources**: Hilti no longer sells the tools but manages them for customers, so the inventory stays on Hilti's balance sheet.
- **Costs**: the new service component has a huge impact on the cost side.

*Result*: fleet management became a substantial part of Hilti's business.

**Lessons**: zoom out from the value proposition to look at or find the business model that makes it work; zoom in from the business model to find the detailed value proposition; and go back and forth between them until you get it right. It is not as simple as changing a value proposition.

### Multiple fits

**What it is**: When a business model needs several value propositions, each fitting a different customer, to succeed.

**Three situations**:
1. **Multi-sided platforms**: Airbnb connects travelers worldwide with people who want to rent out their flat or house for a few days. It needs a value proposition for travelers looking for a place to stay, and one for people who want to rent out their homes and list them on the site. It only succeeds with a great value proposition to both.
2. **Intermediaries**: Haier, the Chinese home appliance maker, has a clear value proposition to consumers. To reach them it sells through retail, so it also needs a clear value proposition to retailers that gets them to stock Haier appliances. Unlike the platform case, here the two value propositions go to end consumers and to direct customers (retailers); Haier needs both.
3. **Business to business**: selling to an organization means selling to different stakeholders within it, each needing its own value proposition. In B2B you need fits with end users, influencers, recommenders, economic buyers, decision makers, and maybe even the saboteurs who could undermine the purchasing decision.

**Lesson**: a business model may require several value propositions to succeed.

## 5. The Business Model Environment

### The design environment around your canvas

**What it is**: The nine building blocks are the elements you control: which customers you target, what you offer them, how you reach them, and what resources and activities you need. Those decisions are the business model design process. But no business model is designed in a vacuum; it is designed in an environment.

**The forces**:
- **Competitors** have their own business models with strengths and weaknesses. Competitors are usually a threat.
- **Customers' needs, jobs, and desires** change over time. Ever-changing customer needs must be discovered and addressed.
- **Technology trends** create opportunities and constraints; a technology trend can be an opportunity or a threat.
- **Legal trends and context** create opportunities and constraints; the legal context is a design constraint.
- **The overall economy**, the availability of capital and of skilled people, can influence the feasibility of your model. Macroeconomic forces affect costs, revenues, and more.

**Why it matters**: Understanding the environment lets you design better business models. Masters of business model design understand that it is a continuous dance between adaptations and reconfigurations of the nine building blocks and the never-ending changes of the environment. How will you adapt to yours?

### Mapping the environment with the Business Model Design Space cards

**What it is**: A workshop exercise to visualize and map everything going on in your business model's environment across four areas (Market Forces, Key Trends, Industry Forces, and Macroeconomic Forces). It is an immersion into the Business Model Design Space that prepares you for the quest for new business models. Ideally run it with a diverse group from different functions and parts of the organization.

**Outcome**:
- a visual map of your Business Model Design Space;
- deep discussions about your business model's environment leading to a shared understanding;
- a starting point to design innovative business models or redesign your current one.

**Why it works**:
- No individual alone could paint a holistic picture of the environment and design space. Only by mapping each specialist's knowledge do you develop a shared understanding.
- A visual map of markets, trends, customer needs, competitors, and more makes things tangible, lets you uncover new associations and discover new patterns, and ultimately leads to new business model ideas.
- It is ideal preparation for prototyping innovative business models.

**Logistics**:
- **Participants**: approximately 4–8 people.
- **Duration**: ongoing pre-workshop material collection; a 2–4 hour workshop (the exercise version says set aside at least two hours); ongoing post-workshop "digestion."
- **Materials**: a completed Business Model Canvas poster of your current business model (not strictly needed if you are a startup or entering a new market); a large wall; the set of Design Space cards; a collection of material on everything going on around your business model (news clippings, photos of customer interactions, spreadsheets, printouts of competitors' websites, industry statistics and graphs, etc.); Stattys (static sticky notes) or sticky notes.

**How to do it**:
1. Attach the completed canvas poster of your current business model to the middle of a large wall, leaving free space around it.
2. Decide which of the four areas to start with: Market Forces, Key Trends, Industry Forces, or Macroeconomic Forces.
3. Draw all cards of the selected area and stick the first one on the wall around the canvas. **Market Forces go to the right** of the canvas, **Key Trends above**, **Industry Forces to the left**, **Macroeconomic Forces below**.
4. Answer the questions on the card by sticking up and discussing sticky notes, photos, spreadsheets, graphs, printouts, products, magazine tear-outs, or anything else "readable," preferably from a distance. Use a lot of visual material rather than just text.
5. Continue with the remaining cards of that area, then move to the next area until you have worked through all cards.
6. You now have a visual map of your Business Model Design Space. Take a picture of it for your records.

**Next steps**: keep completing the picture after the workshop. Take time to "wander" the map to uncover new associations between elements and develop new business model ideas. Organize a follow-up workshop to (a) brainstorm innovative business model ideas and (b) prototype a selection of them on the canvas.

**Variation**: run it with four teams of about 4–8 people, each taking one of the four areas, then assemble the big picture as each team explains its findings in plenary.

#### Industry Forces (left of the canvas)

- **Competitors (incumbents)**: identifies incumbent competitors and their relative strengths. Who are our competitors? Who are the dominant players in our sector? What are their competitive advantages or disadvantages? Describe their main offers. Which customer segments do they focus on? What is their cost structure? How much influence do they exert on our customer segments, revenue streams, and margins?
- **New entrants (insurgents)**: identifies new insurgent players and whether they compete with a business model different from yours. Who are the new entrants? How are they different? What competitive advantages or disadvantages do they have? Which barriers must they overcome? What are their value propositions? Which segments do they focus on? What is their cost structure? To what extent do they influence your segments, revenue streams, and margins?
- **Substitute products and services**: potential substitutes for your offers, including from other markets and industries. Which products or services could replace ours? How much do they cost compared to ours? How easy is it for customers to switch? What business model traditions do these substitutes stem from (e.g. high-speed trains versus airplanes, mobile phones versus cameras, Skype versus long-distance telephone companies)?
- **Stakeholders**: which actors may influence your organization and business model. Which stakeholders might influence your business model? How influential are shareholders? Workers? The government? Lobbyists?
- **Suppliers and other value chain actors**: Who are the key players in your industry value chain? To what extent does your business model depend on other players? Are peripheral players emerging? Which are most profitable?

#### Market Forces (right of the canvas)

- **Market issues**: key issues driving and transforming your market from customer and offer perspectives. What are the crucial issues affecting the customer landscape? Which shifts are underway? Where is the market heading?
- **Market segments**: the major segments, their attractiveness, and new segments. What are the most important customer segments? Where is the biggest growth potential? Which segments are declining? Which peripheral segments deserve attention?
- **Needs and demands**: market needs and how well they are served. What do customers need? Where are the biggest unsatisfied needs? What do customers really want to get done? Where is demand increasing? Declining?
- **Switching costs**: elements related to customers switching to competitors. What binds customers to a company and its offer? What switching costs prevent defection? Is it easy for customers to find and purchase similar offers? How important is brand?
- **Revenue attractiveness**: revenue attractiveness and pricing power. What are customers really willing to pay for? Where can the largest margins be achieved? Can customers easily find and buy cheaper products and services?

#### Key Trends (above the canvas)

- **Technology trends**: trends that could threaten your business model, or enable it to evolve or improve. What are the major technology trends inside and outside your market? Which technologies represent important opportunities or disruptive threats? Which emerging technologies are peripheral customers adopting?
- **Regulatory trends**: Which regulatory trends influence your market? What rules may affect your business model? Which regulations and taxes affect customer demand?
- **Socioeconomic trends**: What are the key demographic trends? How would you characterize income and wealth distribution? How high are disposable incomes? Describe spending patterns (housing, healthcare, entertainment, etc.). What portion of the population lives in urban versus rural areas?
- **Societal and cultural trends**: Describe key societal trends. Which shifts in cultural or societal values affect your business model? Which trends might influence buyer behavior?

#### Macroeconomic Forces (below the canvas)

- **Global market conditions**: Is the economy in a boom or bust phase? Describe general market sentiment. What is the GDP growth rate? How high is unemployment?
- **Capital markets**: current capital market conditions as they relate to your capital needs. What is the state of the capital markets? How easy is it to obtain funding in your market? Is seed capital, venture capital, public funding, market capital, or credit readily available? How costly is it to procure funds?
- **Commodities and other resources**: current prices and price trends for resources your business model requires. Describe the markets for commodities and resources essential to you (e.g. oil prices and labor costs). How easy is it to obtain the resources needed (e.g. to attract prime talent)? How costly are they? Where are prices headed?
- **Economic infrastructure**: How good is the (public) infrastructure in your market? How would you characterize transportation, trade, school quality, and access to suppliers and customers? How high are individual and corporate taxes? How good are public services for organizations? How would you rate the quality of life?

### Where to start: trigger questions across three layers

Innovation can start in your environment, in your current business model, or in your value proposition. Zoom out from the value proposition to the business model and environment, or zoom in the other way. Ask "Could you…"

**Your business model environment**:
- Imitate and "import" a pioneering model from another sector or industry?
- Come up with a new value proposition that your competitors can't copy?
- Create value based on a new technology trend, or turn a new regulation to your advantage?
- Adapt your value proposition to a new or underserved segment, such as the rising middle class in emerging markets?
- Design a value proposition for a new macroeconomic trend, such as rising healthcare costs in the Western hemisphere?

**Your current business model(s)**:
- Come up with a new value proposition based on a new partnership?
- Build on your existing activities and resources, including patents, infrastructure, skills, user base?
- Dramatically alter your cost structure to lower your prices substantially?
- Leverage your existing relationships and channels to offer customers a new value proposition?
- Give away your core product for free, or increase your prices by a multiple?

**Your value proposition(s)**:
- Imagine a new product or service?
- Create a new pain reliever for a given customer profile?
- Create a new gain creator for a given customer profile?
- Uncover a new unsatisfied job?
- Solve your customer's most extreme unresolved pain?
- Focus on your customers' most essential unrealized gain?

## 6. Blue Ocean Strategy: Doing More with Less

### The industry standard: Sony PlayStation 2

Sketching the PS2 business model shows what "the industry standard" looked like before disruption.

- **Center**: the box, the PlayStation 2.
- **Segment**: hardcore gamers around the world.
- **Value proposition**: what attracts gamers is the quality and number of games, and a key part of game quality is graphics, which make games realistic.
- **Key activity/resource**: to deliver great graphics Sony needs the best processor in the world in the box, the fastest speed, so games are most realistic.
- **Channel and revenue**: sold through retail; revenue from console sales. But Sony loses money on every box, because it subsidizes each one.
- **Why subsidize**: Sony earns from a different revenue stream, the game developers. What attracts developers is the number of gamers they can sell to, which is therefore a key resource. The revenue stream is licensing fees from game developers for each individual game sold. That is what makes Sony money.
- **Backstage**: a great brand, meaning marketing activities and costs (Sony sponsors the UEFA Champions League); manufacturing, another main cost; and, crucially, heavy R&D to build the fastest processor with the best graphics, done in an expensive partnership with IBM. The whole industry competes on the fastest processor in the world.

### The Four Actions Framework

**What it is**: A framework proposed by W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne in *Blue Ocean Strategy* for creating a new value proposition or business model, or improving an existing one, using four kinds of action:
1. **Create**: new features of the value proposition, or new business model components, that the industry has never offered before.
2. **Raise**: existing features or components raised well above industry standards.
3. **Eliminate**: features or components the industry has taken for granted.
4. **Reduce**: features or components reduced well below industry standards.

Kim and Mauborgne originally applied the four actions only to value propositions. Here the same four questions are extended to business models in general.

**Exercise**: Ask what Nintendo did differently with the Wii compared to the industry's standard value proposition and business model (the PS2). It doesn't matter whether you know the Wii well; if you are serious about designing great business models, you need to understand how it disrupted the industry. Answer: What did the Wii add (create)? What did it raise? What did it eliminate? What did it reduce?

### How the Nintendo Wii disrupted the console industry

**The four actions applied**:
- **Created** a new kind of customer: casual gamers, or family gamers.
- **Eliminated** the hardcore gamers, the main customers of competitors at that time.
- **Reduced** processing speed and graphics capability, simplifying the device.
- **Created** a new value proposition feature: the Wii Remote's motion control based on gestures.
- **Raised** the simplicity of the gaming activity, and the fun factor, especially for families (a grandmother playing with the grandchildren).
- **Reduced or eliminated** cords, since the Wii Remote is wireless.
- **Eliminated** the console subsidies competitors had to give.
- **Reduced** R&D activity and costs, because of the simplicity, fewer subsidies, and easier production.
- **Result**: the possibility of increasing revenues very significantly.

**Consequences for the business model**:
- The big change: Nintendo targets casual rather than hardcore gamers, moving from a niche market to a larger market.
- It does so with an **inferior value proposition**: a console that's not as powerful, but more playful and suited to casual gamers.
- Less technology means R&D costs go down.
- Partnerships with state-of-the-art technology suppliers become irrelevant, lowering R&D costs further.
- Less sophisticated technology lets Nintendo use off-the-shelf standard components and original equipment manufacturers, so manufacturing costs go down.
- That lets Nintendo **eliminate the subsidies**, so it earns money on each console sold.
- Because it serves a larger market, it also earns more from licensing fees.

Recap: a larger market attracted by an inferior technology, meaning lower costs and insane profits. When did you last design a business model and strategy on inferior technology?

### How citizenM created a superior hotel experience with fewer resources

**Segment**: citizenM, a hotel chain created by Rattan Chadha and Michael Levy, focuses on "mobile citizens of the world": culture seekers, professionals, explorers, and shoppers who visit a city for one or two days.

**The four actions**:
- **Eliminate** some of the most costly elements of a high-end hotel's value proposition: the kitchen, table-seated restaurants, the fitness area, and the wet areas of the spa. Not because mobile citizens don't value them, but because they are very expensive.
- **Reduce** room space (very expensive), which gives the customer a lower price; reduce the more painful processes of the hotel experience, such as check-in; and reduce services the mobile citizen might not need.
- **Raise** very good beds and pillows, soundproof rooms, and a 24/7 bar and kiosk (coming back from a night out in Amsterdam, you find good sushi and a selection of beers).
- **Create** things that should be mandatory in the hotel experience but often aren't: free wifi, which every frequent traveler wants.

**What it looks like**:
- Self-service check-in terminals: check in simply with your credit card; if there is an issue, all-round staff come and help. Their only mandate is to make you a happy customer.
- A high-end self-service food and beverage area with good sushi, a selection of beers, and great espresso.
- A living room atmosphere on the ground floor, with design furniture from a partnership with Vitra. The reason: the rooms are the European version of a Japanese pod hotel, small, functional, but high-tech. Small means 14 square meters.
- In a partnership with Philips, a remote control lets you steer your room.
- The rooms are prefabricated and standardized.

**The business model**:
- **Segment and value proposition**: mobile citizens globally, offered "affordable luxury," in particular a very good quality-to-price ratio.
- **Channels**: rooms sold mainly through the Internet, at a much higher rate than anything else in the industry.
- **Relationships/people**: very service-oriented, all-round staff. Five people can run an entire hotel.
- **Revenue**: very traditional streams, but higher revenue per room than the industry standard.
- **Backstage**: citizenM completely changed the hotel backstage. It has a **room factory**: rooms are manufactured and then assembled into a hotel, dramatically reducing construction costs. Logistics and supplies are outsourced, so all it has to do is operations management of the hotel. It can run a hotel with over 200 rooms with only five staff, far better than anything else in the industry, and still gets very high service ratings.
- **Cost results**: dramatically lower operations costs, dramatically lower construction costs, and dramatically lower labor costs, helped by very low staff turnover (rare in this industry).

**Why it's special**: it built a scalable organization at lower cost, with more features that create value and fewer features that are costly: lower costs and higher value for the customer. The founders' intended positioning was high on quality and image but low on price: more value at lower cost, doing more with less.

## 7. Competing on Business Models

### Technology and product innovation matter, but aren't enough anymore

**Xerox, 1958**: Chester Carlson headed a research team at Haloid, which later became Xerox (a story told by Henry Chesbrough of Berkeley, who coined "open innovation"). They invented the first modern photocopier, able to make 2,000 copies a day at a time when you could make only about 20 to 30 with a smelly chemical process. The problem: the technology was seven to eight times more expensive than anything else available. Xerox asked consultants what to do; they did market research and reported that nobody would want it.

Haloid/Xerox went back to the drawing board and designed a different business model. Instead of selling the machine, it leased it to corporations for a $95 monthly fee, including 2,000 free copies per month. Beyond 2,000 copies a month, customers paid a tiny fee of $0.04 per copy. What happened: people didn't make 2,000 copies a month, they made 2,000 a day, so Xerox earned from the copies from day two onwards, plus service and support fees. That drove Xerox's incredible growth over the following decades and transformed, even created, the photocopying industry.

**Google, 1997**: Larry Page and Sergey Brin invented or popularized the first modern search engine. Google is free, so how do you earn money from free? They built a business model around it. Great search algorithms let them offer free search to users worldwide (the best search engine of the time); you can't ask people to pay for search. So they offered targeted search ads and access to a global audience to advertisers, and rather than simply selling ads they auctioned off search terms. That made Google very successful: 2012 revenues were over fifteen billion, with ten billion of net income (figures as given).

**Lesson**: in both cases product and technology innovation were important but not enough. Great products and technology are essential, but no longer sufficient. How much time and energy are you spending on enabling business models?

### The four levels of business model competition

The competitive game increasingly plays out between superior business models, in addition to the traditional battle between products, technologies, and services.

- **Level 0, "the oblivious"**: companies focused mainly on product, technology, and related services. The best build great value propositions with strong product-market fit. They are often oblivious to the fact that they could outcompete others by combining their products with a better business model.
- **Level 1, "the beginners"**: companies starting to understand the importance of competing on business models. They use the canvas to ask which customers they target, how they reach and acquire them, how they earn money, what resources, activities, and partners they need, and what the cost structure is. They look at the whole model but use the canvas more like a checklist. They have taken their first step.
- **Level 2, "the masters"**: companies that build outstanding business models. They know covering all components isn't enough; their business models have a story in which every piece reinforces the others. Nintendo Wii: beyond an innovative product, a model with lower costs, more customers, and superior margins; it outcompeted rivals on the business model. Dell disrupted the entire PC market with its model. Nespresso: at the center is a machine for single-portion espresso, but what gets customers to pay six to eight times more for coffee, outperforms competitors, and produces insane profits is the configuration of the business model.
- **Level 3, "the invincible"**: companies that compete on a superior business model and already think of new ones while successful. They build new business models before a crisis or an industry or technology change forces them to, sometimes self-disrupting or cannibalizing their existing model while still successful. Very hard to beat, practically unstoppable. Examples: Apple and Amazon, which continuously innovate their business models despite their success.

You decide where to compete. Level 0 is like competing on training wheels; level 3 is like a Ducati Monster motorcycle. Guess which one outcompetes the other.

### Exercise: assess the competition level of different companies

Classify each company as oblivious, beginner, master, or invincible, then compare with the debrief.

- **3M**: best known for Post-it notes; famous as one of the world's most innovative companies; extremely diversified, generating $30 billion in sales from more than 55,000 products; mission: transforming new ideas into thousands of ingenious products. *Debrief: Level 0.* Probably one of the greatest product innovators in corporate history, which has served it well. Yet if it mastered business model innovation, it could probably double or triple revenues in a few years.
- **Google**: the poster child of 21st-century innovation, disrupting one industry after another: Google Apps is making Microsoft's life very hard, free Google Maps put several map makers out of business, and Android became the leading mobile operating system. *Debrief: Level 2.* Its business model is a gold mine built around search. To date it hasn't produced substantial business model innovation, though it is a formidable product and technology innovator; most successful innovations (e.g. Android) support the original search business model. No other business model has challenged it yet, but that might happen faster than expected.
- **GoPro**: a high-profile California camera company that carved out a big niche in action sports. Launched in 2004; around 500 employees worldwide; contract manufacturer Foxconn bought 8.88% for $200 million in late 2012, valuing the company at $2.25 billion. *Debrief: Level 0.* Very successful since about 2009 with incredible revenue growth from its cameras, but still mainly focused on technology and products. Can its model survive growing competition from product innovators like Sony with similar value propositions? Time will tell.
- **Your company**: only you know the answer, but you could now become an "invincible" business model competitor.

## 8. Seven Questions to Assess Your Business Model Design

**What it is**: A scorecard for the "business model mechanics" of a design. Great value propositions should be embedded in great business models. Some business models are better than others by design: they produce better financial results, are harder to copy, and outperform competitors.

**How to do it**: For each question, circle how you perform on a scale from 0 to 10, using these anchors:

| # | Question | 0 means | 10 means |
|:-|:-|:-|:-|
| 1 | Switching costs | Nothing holds my customers back from leaving me | My customers are locked in for several years |
| 2 | Recurring revenues | 100% of my sales are transactional | 100% of my sales lead to automatically recurring revenues |
| 3 | Earning vs. spending | I incur 100% of my costs of goods sold (COGS) before earning revenues | I earn 100% of my revenues before incurring costs of goods and services sold |
| 4 | Game-changing cost structure | My cost structure is at least 30% higher than my competitors' | My cost structure is at least 30% lower than my competitors' |
| 5 | Others who do the work | I incur costs for all the value created in my business model | All the value created in my business model is created for free by external parties |
| 6 | Scalability | Growing my business model requires substantial resources and effort | My business model has virtually no limits to growth |
| 7 | Protection from competition | My business model has no moats, and I'm vulnerable to competition | My business model provides substantial moats that are hard to overcome |

### 1. Switching costs

**What it is**: How easy or difficult it is for customers to switch to another company.

**Example, the iPod**: When Steve Jobs pitched the iPod, the one thing he repeated again and again was "1,000 songs in your pocket." That was the value proposition, a great product innovation sold through retail to the mass market, backed by real technology innovation (it was hard to fit 1,000 songs in a pocket then). What most people don't realize is that it was a very intelligent strategy to create switching costs. Once you have 1,000 songs on one device, it is pretty difficult (unless the company makes it easy) to move them to a device from another brand. You are locked in. For Apple, that meant recurring transactional sales: locked into the Apple system, you are likely to buy Apple products later. On top of that, iTunes software and the iTunes store created recurring content revenues. What most people don't know is that for a long time content revenue wasn't that important: Apple built the system with switching costs mainly to create recurring revenue from selling devices, from hardware.

**Low end example**: in the hygiene sector, toilet paper has practically zero switching costs; customers can switch brands very easily. That is why these companies invest heavily in marketing and brand.

### 2. Recurring revenues

**What it is**: Is every sale a new effort to sell (transactional revenue), or does it produce follow-up revenues and purchases (recurring revenue)?

**Example, Nespresso**: Nespresso created switching costs that led to recurring revenue. The value proposition, a barista experience at home, is composed of machines and pods. Nespresso pushes machines into retail channels to reach households, because once the machine is in your home you are locked in: you can only use Nespresso pods, defended by patents. (Some of those patents ran out in 2012, making lock-in much harder.) With the machine, you must go to Nespresso's direct channels to buy pods, and because you are locked in, pod revenue recurs. The machine is a one-off transactional sale; the pods bring repeat sales again and again.

**Score**: only transactional revenues puts you on the left; recurring revenues you can even project over years put you on the right.

### 3. Earning before spending

**What it is**: Are you spending money before money comes in, for example on producing products or building platforms?

**Example, the PC industry before and after Dell**: PCs were manufactured (large manufacturing costs) and sold through retail to the mass market. Modeled correctly, the PC maker's real customer is the retailer, who pays for the computers. The problem: manufacturing costs occur long before sales, if sales ever happen, a big time gap between spending and earning. It also leads to inventory depreciation, which can be a huge cost. Dell entered in the 1990s selling PCs directly to the mass market, and perfected just-in-time manufacturing: it only started manufacturing very close to the moment of sale, so expenses came just in time, much closer to revenue, and inventory depreciation fell. These business model mechanics were put in place in an industry that worked very differently.

**Example, the book *Business Model Generation***: before a sentence was written or a page designed, people paid to join and co-create the book, first $24 and at the end $250 (the book's own account gives $243), so the authors earned money before spending on design, printing, and sales.

### 4. Game-changing cost structure

**What it is**: Every company looks at costs, but some have cost structures substantially different from and better than their competitors'.

**Example, telecom operators versus Skype**: Telecom operators sold calls through their own channels, often physical stores, and installed lines in a local market. Local because telecoms could only offer service where they had networks (for a long time). Revenue came from calls, per minute or a fixed fee. To make this happen they invested in developing and maintaining local networks: huge capital expenditure. Offering calls required a very expensive network built first and monetized later.

Skype, built by Niklas Zennstrom with Janus Friis, offered free calls, even free video calls, Skype to Skype. It needed voice-over-IP software to route calls over the Internet, so it required no network of its own; as an Internet company, the global market was its market, targeting users globally. Users needed Internet access, which was essential to the model, but that cost was not borne by Skype, so it could offer calls for free. The difference: no capital expenditure in the telco sense. Software requires lower capital expenditure than a network; there are fixed costs (great developers, much intellectual property), but it is a very different and better cost structure for offering calls.

**Example, Bharti Airtel**: at a time when everyone considered the network the core asset, Bharti Airtel outsourced all IT and network infrastructure because that wasn't its core; the core was offering services to customers. It got rid of the most expensive part of the cost structure and performed extremely well.

**Score**: a cost structure similar to most others (maybe slightly better) is the left; a substantially better one than competitors' is the right.

### 5. Getting others to do the work

**What it is**: How much does your business model get customers or third parties to create value for you, ideally for free?

**Example, Facebook**: Facebook offers a platform (facebook.com) to connect with friends, targeting users globally, over a billion today. What you really go to Facebook for isn't just the platform but the content you and your friends update all the time. So a key resource is constantly changing content, and Facebook must make sure users keep changing it. It isn't Facebook creating that content but users globally, for free. One of the most valuable things in the business model is generated for free; Facebook largely just creates the platform. (Its valuation was huge, though it didn't perform extremely well when it went to the stock market; the business model is still very powerful.)

**Score**: doing everything yourself at high cost is the left; getting others to do the work, and even to become passionate about it, puts you near a 10.

### 6. Scalability

**What it is**: How rapidly and easily can you grow the business model without hitting roadblocks in infrastructure, customer support, and so on? Facebook's model is extremely scalable.

**Example, Diners Club** (the first credit card usable in different places): it offered credit via a card accepted at several restaurants to salesmen who invited their customers to restaurants in New York. The salesman paid a $3 annual fee (not much today, worth more then). Diners Club first had to get a series of restaurants to accept the card, then expand to other areas. It attracted restaurants, the other segment, with access to clients, very loyal ones, and earned money from transaction cuts. This double-sided market is extremely scalable: once you have acquired customers on one side and accepting merchants on the other, you can scale very easily.

### 7. Protection from competition

**What it is**: How much your business model protects you from competition: how hard it is for competitors to enter your market or copy your model. This is more an accumulation of all the other questions.

**Example, Apple iPhone and iOS**: At launch, technology gave the iPhone its early lead. Today you might argue it's not even the best phone. It isn't the technology or the phone itself keeping Apple ahead; it's the business model. On one side, 400 million iPhone/iOS users come to Apple partly because they like the technology. On the other side, a huge number of applications, developed by app developers who come because there are so many iPhone users. This double-sided market reinforces itself in both directions and is very hard to copy. Windows and Nokia together might catch up on technology, but can they get hundreds of thousands of developers and hundreds of thousands of apps onto their platform? That's the hard part. The result: Apple earns about 70 to 75 percent of all profits in the industry with only about 7 percent market share.

### Design is not enough without traction

Even a perfect 10 on all seven questions means a great business model design, not that customers will love your value proposition. You also need market traction. The best business models have both: a great business model design and market traction because customers love the value proposition. Together they mean wild financial success. And with a great design you are likely to outcompete competitors who have only market traction and perform poorly on these seven questions.

**Exercise**: find one additional example that illustrates each of the seven questions (switching costs, recurring revenues, earning vs. spending, game-changing cost structure, others who do the work, scalability, protection from competition based on business model). These skills help in the ideation phase when you aim to create a great model for your business.

## 9. Playing with Numbers

### Prototyping business models with ballpark figures

**What it is**: Throwing rough, ballpark figures at a business model to learn whether it is a big opportunity and how its financial dynamics work.

**Why it works**: When people hear "numbers" they think of spreadsheets, but spreadsheets are for detailed, accurate financial reports. That is not the goal here. Numbers are an outcome of how you create and deliver value. The value side (front stage, as in the theater analogy) creates revenues; the infrastructure side needed to create that value produces costs; revenues must exceed costs. You are not trying to establish whether the idea is 100% correct or the numbers accurate in detail. You want rough models, because in prototyping you need to explore different business models with different financials; detailed spreadsheets take too long and stop you from finding the best idea. You can apply this to improving existing business models or to inventing new ones. (A digital tool like Strategyzer lets you attach numbers and formulas to canvas elements, faster than spreadsheets.)

**How to do it**:
1. Sketch the business model on the canvas.
2. Attach rough market-size numbers to the segments (quick research or industry reports).
3. Add each revenue stream as a formula: market size × share reached × purchase frequency × price, minus any intermediary cut.
4. Add costs as formulas (variable costs tied to volume; fixed costs as estimates).
5. Read off the profit. Then change the business model, not just the numbers, and compare.

**Example: a medical technology startup in genetic testing**

*Prototype 1, transactional device sales*: The company has intellectual property and plans a medical diagnostic device, sold through a third-party sales force it hires and pays a transaction fee, generating one-time transactional sales. Infrastructure: R&D, manufacturing, sales and marketing, working with original equipment manufacturers.
- Market: 110,700 primary care doctors in the US (a quick search of reports).
- Reach: one in fifteen doctors, 6.667% (also findable in industry reports).
- Frequency: doctors buy once.
- Price: research showed doctors value the device at $5,000 but are only willing to pay $1,000.
- Sales force cut: 50%.
- Revenue: $7.4 million per year; the sales force cost is $3.7 million (50% of transactions).
- Manufacturing: about $225 per device (variable), at one-in-fifteen, one-time purchase over a year: $1.7 million.
- Sales and marketing: about $900,000 to get that market share (pure assumption).
- Equipment: about $75,000 (known more accurately).
- Profit: approximately $1 million.

These are assumptions, but you get the size of the opportunity and the model's dynamics. The team felt it probably wasn't worth it and looked for a better model.

*Prototype 2, Nespresso-style recurring revenues*: Nespresso turned coffee from a transactional business into one with recurring revenue with a base product plus a consumable. The team imagined disposable cartridges and testing strips as the consumable, like Nespresso pods. There is precedent in medical technology: glucose testing devices for diabetes use disposable cartridges and strips. Add a disposables revenue stream sold through the Internet, and add production of the disposables to the infrastructure.
- Reach: still one in fifteen (about 6%).
- Frequency: five cartridges/strips per doctor per month.
- Price: an average of $75.
- Revenue: about $33 million, with no intermediary taking a cut.
- Production: about $1.8 per unit, variable; roughly $800,000.
- Profit: shown as $33 million in the tool's estimate, described as an opportunity of about $30 million, thirty times bigger than the first prototype.

Will it work? Unknown yet, but the team is on the path to a better business model. Play with the numbers the next time you prototype an idea.

### How Nike substantially improved the numbers of its running shoe business

**Existing model**: Nike sells running shoes through distributors to global retailers, earning a wholesale fee of approximately $50 per shoe. Backstage: heavy R&D, demand creation, supply chain management, manufacturing and distribution. Key resources: its magnificent brand and many patents. Key partners: manufacturing contractors, supply chain management partners, and athletes (Michael Jordan, Tiger Woods, Kobe Bryant, and so on). Cost structure: R&D, cost of sales, selling and admin, promotion and advertising.

**Numbers**: 52,000 retailers worldwide sell Nike; that gives a running-shoe revenue stream of about US $3 billion and a profit of about $380 million for running shoes alone.

**The change**: the model relied on low-cost manufacturing with a lot of manual labor in low-cost countries such as China, Cambodia, and Vietnam. Flyknit technology changed this. Nike calls it micro-level precision engineering: proprietary software instructs a knitting machine to knit the entire upper of a shoe in a single piece, which is then attached to the tongue and sole. No more need for big factories with manual labor. CEO Mark Parker called it a complete game changer: Nike could manufacture shoes anywhere in the world.

**Numbers after the change**: cost of sales is cut in half. Profit becomes 3.7 times higher. Imagine applying the technology to Nike's other shoes: the whole company would become substantially more profitable.

## 10. The Design Attitude and Prototyping

### From a decision attitude to a design attitude

**What it is**: Business people typically take a decision attitude: it's easy to come up with alternatives (A, B, C) and hard to choose between them, so the work is choosing among existing options. Designers take a design attitude: create new alternatives, design possibilities of what could be rather than just what is, and only then choose.

**Why it matters**: Yesterday's dominant business models have expired, and in that context a decision attitude isn't enough. The music industry learned painfully that its business model had expired and is only now experimenting with new ones. The pharmaceutical industry's blockbuster-drug model expired. Publishing hasn't really acted on its expired model: it thinks ebooks are the solution, yet that is just a technology; it needs new business models. Has your industry's dominant business model expired? Business models expire like a yogurt in the fridge, so start working on new ones before it's too late.

### What prototyping is

Prototyping is a simple process of building quick, cheap, rough study models: models you won't hesitate to throw away, because you know they are not the final solution. The point is to learn about the desirability, feasibility, and viability of alternative solutions, here business models.

**Learning from architects**: Frank Gehry says, "If I knew where I was going, I wouldn't do it." Architects are comfortable with the unknown and explore possibilities until they know what they will build; business people are not. A building like Gehry's Guggenheim Museum doesn't come from heavenly inspiration. The team makes tons of study models, sketches, and prototypes in very different materials, going down one path, throwing it away, trying another, with models cheap enough to throw away and start over. Only when they can say "this is the mature design" do they refine and build. Prototyping is not making a refined picture of your first idea; it is exploring alternatives and what could be. Rather than falling in love with your first idea, explore many possibilities; only then will you find a business model that outcompetes others.

**Play with difficult questions**: Could you build a business model with recurring revenues? Will it be based more on fixed or variable costs? Could you transform your business into variable costs? Distributed or centralized logistics? Don't just brainstorm and discuss (remember blah blah blah): quickly sketch out one idea, then several, on the canvas.

**Prototyping as conversation**: "Prototyping is the conversation you have with your ideas" (Tom Wujec). Without prototyping, ideas can't evolve. Designers are comfortable with a very messy beginning (the "design squiggle") and see the design mature over time, throwing many ideas away along the way.

**Share rough models early**: designers and architects share rough models very early to gather feedback. Business people refine models, build thick Excel spreadsheets, and lose time on something they might throw away, and then won't throw away because they've fallen in love with it. Gehry presents deliberately crude models "to make people nervous," just to understand their thinking and how the design needs to change. Would you ever present a business model you know you won't build just to get reactions?

**Keep early prototypes short**: early business model prototypes should take a maximum of five to fifteen minutes and contain very little detail; otherwise you can't do many, can't explore possibilities, and won't be able to throw them away.

**Lessons**:
1. Don't just choose from existing business models; design new alternatives, asking what could be, using prototypes.
2. If you fall in love with your first models and refine them early, you won't be able to change anymore. Sketch rough, different models to explore possibilities.
3. Don't present only final, refined models you worked on for weeks. Make very rough prototypes to collect feedback early, then iterate and pivot towards better business models.

"I begin with an idea and then it becomes something else" (Pablo Picasso). Designers and artists are comfortable with that and have a process for it. Think like a designer: imagine what could be and prototype to learn quickly.

### What the Marshmallow Challenge teaches

**What it is**: A design exercise introduced by Peter Skillman. Teams of four must build the tallest freestanding structure from 20 sticks of spaghetti, one yard of tape, one yard of string, and a marshmallow, which must be on top. It seems simple but is hard because it forces people to collaborate very quickly. It has been run in about 70 design workshops worldwide with students, designers, architects, even CTOs of the Fortune 50.

**What usually happens**: teams orient themselves to the task, talk about it, figure out what it will look like, and jockey for power. Then they plan and organize, sketch, lay out spaghetti. They spend most of their time assembling sticks into ever-growing structures. Just as time runs out, someone takes out the marshmallow, gingerly places it on top, they stand back, and "ta-da." But most of the time the ta-da turns into an "uh-oh": the marshmallow's weight makes the whole structure buckle and collapse.

**Who does worst and best**: among the worst are recent business school graduates (they lie, cheat, get distracted, and produce really lame structures). Among the best are recent kindergarten graduates, who not only build the tallest structures but the most interesting ones. Why? None of the kids spend time trying to be CEO of Spaghetti, Inc., jockeying for power. More importantly, business students are trained to find the single right plan and execute it; when the marshmallow goes on at the end and they run out of time, it's a crisis. Kindergartners start with the marshmallow and build successive prototypes, always keeping the marshmallow on top, so they have multiple chances to fix things along the way. Designers recognize this as the essence of the iterative process: each version gives instant feedback on what works and what doesn't. The capacity to play and prototype is essential.

**Performance by group**: the average for most people is around 20 inches; business school students about half that; lawyers a little better, but not much; kindergartners better than most adults. The very best are architects and engineers (thankfully); the tallest structure seen was 39 inches, because they understand that triangles and self-reinforcing geometrical patterns are key to stable structures. CEOs do a little better than average. But add an executive admin to a team and it gets significantly better: they have facilitation skills, they manage and understand the process. Any team that manages and pays close attention to the work improves significantly. Specialized skills plus facilitation skills lead to strong success. Out of ten teams, typically about six have standing structures.

**The effect of high stakes**: when a $10,000 software prize was offered to the winning team of design students, not one team had a standing structure; anyone who had built even a one-inch structure would have won. High stakes have a strong impact. When the same students did it again, now understanding the value of prototyping, they went from the very worst to among the very best, producing the tallest structures in the least time. There are deep lessons here about incentives and success.

**Why it matters**: every project has its own marshmallow. The challenge helps teams identify hidden assumptions and provides a shared experience, a common language, and a common stance for building the right prototype. (Step-by-step instructions, world records, and examples are at marshmallowchallenge.com.) Design is a contact sport: it demands all our senses and the best of our thinking, feeling, and doing. Sometimes a small prototype of the experience is all it takes to turn an uh-oh moment into a ta-da moment.

### Exercise: three business models in six minutes (the bicycle)

**How to do it**: You have exactly six minutes to sketch three alternative business models for the same starting point: a business built on two key resources, a bicycle and $1,000. Each of the three models must be built around the bicycle and the $1,000.
1. Have a canvas and sticky notes ready.
2. Set a timer for six minutes.
3. Sketch your first model.
4. When done, throw it away by removing all the sticky notes.
5. Sketch your second model. Repeat.

**Debrief, what the exercise teaches**:
- Most first-timers focus on product innovation and use the canvas mainly to fill in pieces around the product or new segments. Instead, think about the story of the business model: three very different alternatives, built on different business model mechanics (switching costs, recurring revenues, and so on).
- When you get good at it, you'll generate three, four, five, ten, twenty alternative business models, each going down a different path with different mechanics.
- Play with difficult questions and address each with different prototypes: Can you create recurring revenues? Tailor-made value proposition or mass production? Physical or virtual? Explore what could be rather than trying to find the best solution directly.
- Exploring many alternatives is time-consuming, but if you keep the early prototypes very rough you can explore many and find the best solution. That beats iterating along a single path.
- The crudeness of early models is deliberate. Spend five to thirty minutes on early prototypes. Use a timer and stick to short time frames, or every prototype turns into a one-hour discussion.
- Don't refine too quickly. As Jim Glymph (of Gehry's team) says, if you refine too early you become attached to the idea, and then it's hard to explore and look for better. How often has someone said after days or a week of work "I don't think this is the right direction," only to hear "We've spent so much time and money on this, there's no way we can go back"? Explore rough, different alternatives at the beginning so you can keep looking for better, and don't fall in love with your first ideas.

## 11. Generating Alternative Business Models

### The business model design and innovation process: search, then execute

**What it is**: A messy process with two phases. First, the **search** for the right business model; only once you have figured it out do you move to **execution**. Search is the hard part. The search phase has two components: **business model design**, where you design many different possibilities of what you could do, and **business model testing**, where you quickly test in the market to find out what you should do, using a process called customer development (section 12).

**How it works**: At the start you make many canvas prototypes, exploring as many possibilities as you can, very quickly. Then you get out and test your ideas fast to find out whether the market is really interested. Only once you have really nailed it do you execute. Drawn as a graph it looks linear, but it isn't: there is a lot of back and forth until you learn what you should be doing. Companies don't like this, because they like linear processes. But following it minimizes your risk and maximizes the likelihood of outcompeting competitors with better business models. Testing means failing a lot and improving a lot, and the process will be full of doubts.

### Improving versus inventing business models

**What it is**: A continuum from improving existing businesses to inventing new ones. The rules of the game are almost totally different at the two ends.

- **Improve (left)**: one or several existing business models that the company knows well; the whole organization is trimmed to execute and incrementally improve them. The environment is known, with existing customers and good market understanding, so relatively accurate sales forecasts and growth predictions are possible. The work is executing and incrementally improving known models. Rules: business plans with performance projections; people evaluated on meeting those projections; failure is not an option.
- **Invent (right)**: one or several new business models the company is trying to develop, far from proven, still being experimented with, in an unknown environment. Sales forecasts and growth numbers are practically impossible at early stages; market understanding is still being built. The work is the search for business models that work. Rules: detailed plans are worthless; projections don't make sense for unproven models. Instead, run many small experiments to test the hypotheses of the unproven model, learn from these continuous small bets, and improve, adapt, or pivot until you are sure it will work.

Existing and new business models need completely different thinking, rules, and organizational structures. Can you play both games in parallel?

### Trigger questions

**What it is**: A technique for generating business model ideas, created because people were very focused on products, technologies, value propositions, or simply expanding to new markets. That's a start, but you want business models with better business model mechanics. Trigger questions move people from a product focus to a business model focus.

**How to do it**:
1. Ask one question at a time and have people answer individually with as many answers as possible.
2. Go for quantity, not just quality. Nobody should hold back ("a five-year contract to subscribe to shoe buying," whatever). People must not stop at one reasonable answer.
3. Across the eight questions, aim for at least 40 ideas.
4. Retain some of the ideas and synthesize them into a business model prototype.
5. With several teams, each synthesizes a different prototype, so you explore several alternatives, just as designers do.

**The eight trigger questions**:
1. How could we earn revenues if we had to offer our best-selling product or service for free?
2. Around which assets could we build a platform business? (Think of the Nintendo Wii console and Apple's App Store.)
3. How could we generate more recurring revenues? Transactional revenues are good; recurring revenues are definitely better.
4. How could we get others to create value for us for free? (Facebook got more than one billion people working for it for free.)
5. Which strategic partners could we work with to leverage or expand our business model? (Nike and Apple with the iPod: you go running, you listen to music, a perfect partnership.)
6. How could we achieve more scalability in our business model?
7. How could we dramatically increase switching costs? You don't want customers to leave, so explore all the ways to weave switching costs into the model.
8. For which segments, other than our usual ones, could we develop a value proposition and expand our business model?

**Pitfall**: the "new product, new segment" trap. Generate business model alternatives. It's hard, but trigger questions get you started.

### Napkin sketches and dotmocracy

**What it is**: The quickest, cheapest business model prototype: a sketch of one idea, synthesized from the answers to the trigger questions.

**What goes in a napkin sketch**:
- a **headline**;
- a **metaphor drawing**;
- a **mini text** explaining a few core elements of the idea.

**Rules**: Don't describe how it works; use a metaphor and a very quick explanation. Don't describe processes: if you have arrows in your drawing, you are probably describing "how," which belongs on the canvas later. Focus on one idea only.

**Why it works**: It gets people to focus quickly on a single idea. Most importantly, it is the quickest and cheapest business model prototype imaginable, so you can throw it away. Even after five to ten minutes on an idea, people find it hard to let go; cheapness counters that.

**How to run it**:
1. Everyone makes napkin sketches of their ideas.
2. Everyone pitches their napkin sketch very quickly, in 15 seconds. (You couldn't do that with a canvas; that comes later.)
3. Check for diversity. If all ideas look alike, or are all just product ideas, throw them away and start over. That's what designers do.
4. When you're happy with the alternatives, run **dotmocracy**: give everyone ten dots to vote on the ideas they are interested in, distributing them however they like (five on one, one on another, two on another...). This gives immediate visual feedback on favorite ideas: where the love is.
5. Drop some prototypes and continue with those that have the most traction. This is not a decision-making mechanism, just a way to move on with the ideas that have the most traction.
6. Turn the top ideas into business model canvases, taking another ten to fifteen minutes each. Do it for the second and maybe third; skip those with few or no votes.

**Example**: a team working on household products produced multiple prototypes; one got 53 votes and clearly won. That doesn't mean pursuing only that one: from ten to twenty ideas, about five with the most votes were pursued.

**Informal version**: it doesn't need a big formal setting. In a meeting, quickly sketch some napkins, vote on what gets people excited, then do a canvas prototype.

"Prototyping and iterating is part of the job. If there's something I've learned in the last four years, it's the power of iteration: rapidly create, test, and change" (Scott Cook). There's nothing faster than a napkin sketch. Quick prototypes let you let go of ideas; make sure you also have diversity.

### Constraint prototyping with the constraint cards

**What it is**: A technique for thinking outside the box, invented because it's very hard for people to think beyond the business models they see every day in their organization or industry. You apply a constraint, copying the mechanics of a known business model, to your own business or a case study, and are forced to design models you wouldn't otherwise think of.

**How to do it (exercise)**:
1. Retrieve the existing business model you have been working on.
2. Pick one constraint card.
3. Take a clean copy of the canvas.
4. Set a timer for 10 minutes (the whole step is about 15 minutes).
5. Prototype an alternative business model that follows the constraint.
6. To keep practicing, pick another card and repeat.

**The five constraint cards**:

1. **Nespresso's recurring revenues**. Nespresso (owned by Nestlé) turned a transactional industry, selling coffee, into one producing recurring revenues, built around well-designed coffee machines and aluminium pods of premium coffee. *Constraint*: copy the mechanics into your model: a **base product** (the coffee machine) and a **consumable** (the pods), with **transactional revenue from the base product** and **recurring revenue from the consumable**. Figure out what the base product and the consumables are in your business model, and how you will create recurring revenues from the consumables. Other examples: printers, razor blades.
2. **The iPhone's platform advantage**. The iPhone's competitive advantage comes not just from its phones but from almost 300,000 external iOS developers who contributed 850,000 apps to the App Store, an ecosystem that is hard to copy. *Constraint*: design a platform business model like Apple's App Store: a **connecting platform** that mediates between **two or more essential customer segments**, each with its own value proposition. Apple attracts smartphone buyers to the iPhone, and attracts application developers because there is a very large user base willing to pay for apps: one segment comes because the other exists, and vice versa. You need **revenue streams from all segments**, though one or more can be subsidized or even free. Apple's are hardware sales on one side and a 30% transaction cut on every app sold on the other. Figure out what the connecting platform is in your business model.
3. **Red Hat Linux's free value proposition**. Red Hat built a billion-dollar company on free software, the freely available Linux operating system. *Constraint*: the **core value proposition is free**. Find one value proposition you give away for free, and still build a profitable business model around it. Don't go straight to advertising; that's too easy. Try something harder. Other examples: Skype, Facebook.
4. **Tupperware's "free" sales force**. Tupperware pioneered direct marketing and became famous for Tupperware parties, where women sold products to friends and neighbors (this was before emancipation, women selling to other women). They weren't employed and didn't draw a salary, though they earned a commission. *Constraint*: a **sales force that is passionate about your value proposition but isn't employed by you and draws no salary** (company or individuals). How can you attract a free sales force that partners with you? Other examples: Avon, Living Goods (Uganda).
5. **The venture capital fund**. VC funds invest in early-stage, high-growth, high-risk startups and mitigate risk by investing in several companies at once, knowing several will fail while some will deliver outstanding returns. *Constraint*: instead of designing the right business model yourself, design the business model of an **investment fund that invests in five to ten startups** who will figure out the right business model for you. The **key resource is capital**, which is also a main part of the value proposition: **funding and advice**. You need the ability to attract and invest in only the very best, most promising entrepreneurs in a given area. And unless you're already filthy rich, you must figure out where the capital comes from (hint: you might need another customer segment).

### Example: a three-day business model workshop

**Caveat**: this structure is designed for a specific context; use it as inspiration or a starting point for your own workshops and processes.

**Context**:
- Rather large organizations, starting with one business line with revenue between US $500 million and a couple of billion.
- Objective: create new growth engines.
- 40 to 60 participants, split into groups of about five people (a very good group size from experience).
- Participants come from all over the world, which is why it's one three-day workshop rather than three separate ones (too expensive to fly people in three times).
- Participants from different functions and backgrounds, so all aspects of the canvas are covered.
- Canvas knowledge: some, but very limited.

**Structure** (morning and afternoon sessions):
- **Day 1, morning**: introduce the canvas. Go through the building blocks very quickly (the interest is the big picture), and spend much more time on business model mechanics, so people think in business models rather than just new products or technologies.
- **Day 1, afternoon**: sketch the as-is business model of the business line as it exists now. Don't let them weave in future ideas; that's day two. In the evening, get everyone to agree that this is the as-is model. This seems quick, but conversations about who the customer segments really are and what you really offer them are intense and take time to reach consensus. It's important to start day two with a shared understanding of the current model.
- **Day 2, morning**: sketch the business model environment (technology trends, competitive trends, market trends, where customer needs are going) in groups of five using the environment cards, then share in plenary to create a shared understanding. Then assess the strengths and weaknesses of the as-is business model specifically, not in general: value proposition, channels, backstage, front stage. A thorough assessment is a great starting point for the afternoon.
- **Day 2, afternoon**: design and prototype alternative growth engines, alternative canvases, what could be, using the whole arsenal of design thinking tools. At the end of the day, each group draws a story of its business model to pitch the next morning.
- **Day 3, morning**: groups pitch their business model prototypes and get feedback, using a particular feedback methodology to avoid the typical political tensions of large organizations.
- **Day 3, afternoon**: next steps, in particular testing business models before building them, and explaining the customer development process. At the end of day three, everyone must understand this was just a starting point: all the energy in the room is the start of getting out of the building, testing, and evolving the prototypes.

**Frequently observed difficulties**:
- **"Let's do it with five to ten leaders."** Insist on larger groups. They integrate the organization's knowledge and people's opinions, which facilitates implementation: even if the decision isn't made in the room, people who participated understand what you're talking about.
- **"We have very many different business models in our business line."** Once they sketch, the core business model in a business line usually turns out to be pretty homogeneous; it may just be at different stages from country to country.
- **"Our people already know the canvas; go fast on the introduction."** They may use it, but their understanding of business model thinking is usually very basic. They use the canvas as a checklist and don't understand that the same product or technology can have very different business models, and that business models can outcompete even better technologies.
- **Participants struggle to break with existing models** in their organization or industry. After trying many methodologies, the conclusion is that you need the entire arsenal of design thinking tools and processes, or people can't think outside the box.
- **The dangerous one: assuming you're ready to execute after three days.** That's how the traditional business plan approach works: pick an idea and implement it. Make it crystal clear that the workshop is only the beginning and that people must get out of the building and test their ideas. Participatory workshops generate stellar enthusiasm and energy; carry that energy out of the room into testing, and you'll see spectacular results.

## 12. Why Business Models Fail and How to Test Them

### The four types of business model failure

1. **Solving an irrelevant customer job.** The business model is built around a value proposition that helps customers get a job done they don't care about, or don't care about enough. It sounds trivial, but entrepreneurs and executives often get deeply excited about a value proposition removed from customers' reality. Examples: the infamous Segway personal transporter, the Apple Newton (an early PDA), and FLO TV, Qualcomm's portable device with a mobile TV service. All three were very expensive flops; one cost over a billion dollars.
2. **A flawed business model design.** Even with a great value proposition for a relevant job, you can fail. You are guaranteed to fail when your customer acquisition cost is higher than what you can earn from customers directly or indirectly over time (their customer lifetime value). Real case: Kodak, now bankrupt, earned the lion's share of its income from high-margin camera film and employed 145,000 workers at its peak. Despite helping invent and commercialize the first digital camera back in 1975, it failed to design a business model for digital cameras that would compensate for evaporating film revenues. Its new digital business model was fundamentally flawed.
3. **External threats from the environment.** Great value proposition, relevant job, sound design, and you can still fail. Competitors might offer a similar value proposition at a much lower price because their business model has different economics. Customers might like your value proposition but think you aren't environmentally friendly enough. New technology might undermine your model. A stock market crash might dry up your funding.
4. **Poor execution.** Even with all fundamentals in place, you can screw up the best business model if you can't build the required infrastructure, assemble the wrong team with the wrong skills, or lack the leadership to turn the concept into reality.

**Questions to avoid them**: Are you helping customers do a job they care strongly about? Do you have the right business model, profitable and scalable? Is today's environment the right context for your business model, and is your model designed to excel in that context? Does your team have the right skills to execute it and make it reality? Nobody said building a business was easy.

### Customer development

**Why it matters**: Some expensive stories:
- **Color**, a startup that went bankrupt, described by the New York Times as "a $41 million party and nobody came."
- **Better Place**, a very ambitious startup aiming to change the world by making electric vehicles extremely popular. It didn't test its business model to make sure it would work. Cost: $850 million.
- **FLO TV** at Qualcomm, a corporate venture: a service to watch TV on a mobile device (you could also make calls). Official loss: $850 million; insider number more like $1.6 billion. It recovered a little by selling the spectrum license, but it was still a spectacular flop.

This happens when you think very hard about a problem but don't test. As Steve Blank, the father of customer development, puts it: business plans don't survive the first contact with customers. Symbolically, burn the business plan. It isn't about writing business plans, or just thinking hard, or just designing great business models; it's about testing them, in addition to understanding business model mechanics.

**What it is**: The underlying idea is that there are no facts inside the building. Get out and talk to customers, partners, and channel partners as quickly as possible to evaluate whether your idea could work and adapt based on what you learn, starting with very quick tests and ending with a more expensive pilot.

**How design and testing fit together**: Within the search phase, design and testing are not sequential but parallel: design, test, design, test.
1. Start by designing. You can sketch several canvases quickly, even before talking to customers. Don't fall in love with the first ideas; you'll throw them away anyway, either because you think they won't work or because testing kills them.
2. Select one, set the others aside for now, and accept that however smart it seems, it is still a series of guesses.
3. Get out of the building and talk to customers to see whether you address real jobs, pains, and gains. You'll learn what doesn't work and what does.
4. Redesign: what did I learn, how could I improve? You might produce one, two, three, or more new canvases. Then select one again.
5. Go beyond asking: run real tests to see what customers will do. Purchasing tests (would they actually buy?), getting them to sign a document or agreement. These bring hard facts that go beyond asking.
6. Keep designing and testing until you can say you've nailed it, with a lot of proof from the market. That minimizes the risk of failure, and you go into execution with much less uncertainty.

**The four phases of customer development** (Steve Blank):
- *Search*
  1. **Customer discovery**: understand the customer: discover jobs, pains, gains, the channels they want, and so on, by getting out of the building. You'll learn what doesn't work and iterate until you think it could work and you understand the customer's problem.
  2. **Customer validation**: go further with tests that bring facts, such as purchasing tests or getting customers to sign a document. Based on the facts you learn what works and what doesn't, and you might have to revise the canvas completely. That is a **pivot**: a substantial change to one or several blocks of the canvas, which sends you back to customer discovery. The process moves back and forth between phases, not sequentially. Once you've proven it could work and have a lot of proof of traction, the search phase ends.
- *Execution*
  3. **Customer creation**: marketing plans and scaling up customer acquisition. You may still learn what does and doesn't work.
  4. **Company building**: once it's really figured out, hire people and set up the right organizational structures.

**On the canvas**: the process is a series of canvases. Get out, talk to customers, test, learn, throw away the old canvas, design a new one, and repeat, probably many times. The distance between successive canvases grows over time, because the more facts you have, the more certain you are the model will work, so you need fewer tests. At the end you have a proven canvas; when building a company, you want to be pretty sure you're investing in something that works. Customer development minimizes your risk of failure; business plans maximize it. Your choice.

### Increase validation spending as confidence rises

**What it is**: A rule for how much to spend on testing over the search phase, relative to uncertainty.

**How it works**: At the start, uncertainty is at its maximum: you have no idea whether your business model or value proposition will work. The aim is to decrease uncertainty over time through customer development. When uncertainty is highest, spend small amounts on very quick, cheap tests to see how the market and partners react. Each test teaches you what works and what doesn't, which raises your confidence and lowers uncertainty, so you can increase spending: more expensive tests, talking to more people, setting up websites that simulate transactions, and so on. Only when you really think it will work do you run pilot tests (what's usually called market testing), which are already very expensive and in large companies can run into the millions. Once you've eliminated most uncertainty and are really sure, spend the full amount to implement and execute the business model.

**Pitfall**: spending big early, while uncertainty is at its maximum, wastes money. Start cheap with rapid tests.

### Why cycle times matter

**What it is**: The speed at which your tests produce insights. Each test runs through a cycle:
1. **Hypothesis**: what you believe must be true for the idea to work. Example for a consumer goods company: "Consumers in Sweden will find our value proposition useful" (a very broad hypothesis).
2. **Test**: set up a landing page and measure how many people sign up for the release date.
3. **Outcome**: say only 1% of visitors sign up, but they come from very high-income households.
4. **Insight**: interest isn't very significant, but there is a promising customer segment paying attention.
5. **Adapt**: the insight makes the hypothesis more specific and changes the next test, and the cycle continues.

**Why it works**: Cycle time is how quickly you can learn from your tests. You want the maximum amount of learning in the minimum amount of time. That is very different from the business plan approach: you are decreasing uncertainty and increasing facts, and the faster, the better.

### From idea to hypotheses, tests, and insights: four steps

1. **What's your idea?** Formalize it with a Business Model Canvas and a Value Proposition Canvas.
2. **What needs to be true for the idea to work?** Extract the hypotheses underlying your canvases.
3. **How can you verify each hypothesis?** Decide which tests or experiments will show whether each is true.
4. **How do the insights change your idea?** Ask whether what you learned changes the original idea and how to adapt it.

Iterate and pivot through these four steps until you have proof that the idea will work. Do you prefer maximizing risk with well-thought-out business plans, or minimizing it through experimentation?

### Testing library: techniques for the search phase

Testing is usually associated with expensive pilot projects at the end of product development, when it's too late to make major changes. These techniques can be used during search and design, as early as the moment you have an idea, to generate market facts rapidly and cheaply while you are still trying to understand customers and design the value proposition and business model. Testing goes beyond interviews and observation: it gets potential customers to perform actions that prove their interest. The facts should continuously inform your search. Three categories:

#### Testing interest and relevance

*What you measure*: Do potential customers show interest in your ideas? Are they relevant? Are customers interested enough to act? You can start before you even have a concrete value proposition, by testing whether customers care about the jobs, pains, and gains you intend to address.

- **Ad tracking / "fake" ads**: advertisers use ad tracking to measure the effectiveness of ad spend; use it to track interest (or lack of it) in a value proposition that doesn't exist yet. If few or no people respond, they either don't have the job, pain, or gain you want to address, or don't see a fit with the advertised value proposition.
  - Tear-off ads or flyers: a low-cost, low-tech way to learn from a local campaign.
  - Google ads: a very scalable way to test reactions to certain search terms. With a small budget on Google AdWords, you only pay when people click through to your landing page.
  - Billboard, magazine, or online banner ads: a very rich but usually more expensive way to test, provided the ad has a call to action that lets you measure interest.
  - Link tracking: when you email people a URL to your offer, track whether they click (easily done with link services such as goo.gl). If they don't click, they aren't interested or have more pressing issues.
- **Landing pages**: a very quick, low-cost way to test interest; originally for online services but just as applicable to physical products. Attract traffic, then test calls to action:
  - Email sign-up to be notified at launch: a small "investment" of time and an email address that proves interest.
  - Surveys: a valuable source of information but not a reliable source of facts; the main fact is that people were willing to invest their time.
  - Click activity: with several elements on the page, heat maps show where visitors click most, often surprisingly.

#### Testing willingness and ability to pay

*What you measure*: Are potential customers interested enough in your value proposition's features to buy? Will they put their money where their mouth is? Proving relevance is not enough; you want proof they will pay you to address those issues.

- **"Fake" sales**: there's a big difference between people saying they are interested and pulling out their wallet or credit card, or unlocking a budget in B2B. That's the difference between information and facts. Simulating a sale of an unfinished or non-existent value proposition gets you facts.
  - Manage what happens after the "purchase": be transparent, explain it was a test, and offer a future discount or another goodie. This usually turns them into great advocates.
  - Simplest method: a "buy now" button on your website or landing page. You can go as far as collecting credit card details (then destroying them immediately) or just count clicks. Works for digital and physical products.
  - In fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG), an established technique is a "fake" sale in a single retail location for a very limited time. Don't confuse it with a pilot sale, which is more extensive, more expensive, often covers a whole market, and comes much later.
- **Pre-sales**: selling before the product fully exists. Standard in large industrial investment goods and now very common in mass-market consumer goods.
  - Kickstarter, one of the most popular pre-selling platforms: advertise a project and people pledge money; projects only get the money if they reach their funding goal.
  - Collecting credit card details on your own platform and charging later: more complicated than an off-the-shelf platform, but you retain full control over legal and commercial aspects.
  - Pledges, letters of intent, and signatures on non-legally binding documents: a powerful test of willingness to buy, even if not a binding pre-sale.
- **Minimum Viable Product (MVP, a form of prototyping)**: a strategy for fast, quantitative market testing of a value proposition, product, service, or feature, first used for web applications and popularized by Eric Ries. An MVP is not always a smaller or cheaper version of the final product; often a cheaper proxy tests the underlying assumptions faster and more cost-effectively. Ask what you really want to learn and what the cheapest possible hack is to test that assumption.

#### Testing preferences and priorities

*What you measure*: Which features do potential customers prefer? What do they really value and prioritize? Facts about preferences beat prioritizing on assumptions, and these techniques also reveal customers' take on alternative prices.

- **Split testing (A/B testing)**: compare two or more identical versions of a value proposition, MVP, or landing page with one variation each. Even marginal changes can have a major impact: in e-commerce, the location of a purchase button or the color of a website can strongly affect buying. The goal is to find changes that increase or maximize an outcome of interest. Landing pages are the most common vehicle; for example, several titles for a book were split-tested this way. Split tests also work for physical products and services using split groups: FMCG retailers test the best shelf and aisle placement, and you can offer services with slightly different features and measure behavior.
- **Innovation Games®**, popularized by Luke Hohmann: collaborative play with customers, online or in person, to unearth, describe, and prioritize value propositions and features. Three of them:
  - **Prune the Product Tree**: using the metaphor of a gardener pruning a tree, customers place index cards with features on a drawing of a tree to shape how they'd like the product to grow. Cards on the outer edges are longer-term features (the future); cards placed closest are near-term additions.
  - **Buy a Feature**: customers prioritize features rather than just debating them. Give them a list of features, each with a price (based on development cost, customer value, or something else), and play money to buy the features they want in the next release.
  - **Product Box**: customers imagine selling your product at a trade show, retail outlet, or public market. Give them a few cardboard boxes and ask them to literally design a product box they would buy, with the marketing slogans they find compelling, then use it to sell the product to you. You learn what they appreciate most and find most important.

### Case: testing interest in an iPad app before spending money

**The setup (exercise)**: On January 27, 2010, Steve Jobs presented the iPad for the first time. At that moment, Alex Osterwalder was running a business model training with Hortis, a Geneva software company specialized in iOS development, and the idea of a business model app for the iPad came up. Before spending money on development, the team (in close contact with Steve Blank) decided to test two hypotheses:
- **H1**: People are interested in using an iPad app to sketch out business models digitally.
- **H2**: Buyers would be willing to pay approximately $29 for such an app.

*Exercise*: take five minutes to come up with at least 10 different ways to test these hypotheses with a maximum budget of $1,000, before spending any time or energy on software development.

**What they actually did**: a very simple, static landing page.
1. A fake screenshot of the iPad app, which didn't exist yet.
2. On the left, a description of what the app would do, without saying it didn't exist yet or was in development: (1) sketch your business model with virtual digital sticky notes; (2) add ballpark figures such as customer numbers and revenues; (3) find out whether your idea could be profitable and how big it could be.
3. A request to sign up with an email address to be notified of the release. (If 10,000 people visit and only 20 sign up, you know there isn't much interest.) In their case, a very large proportion of visitors signed up.
4. After sign-up, one more question: "Would you pay $100 for this app?" They actually wanted to charge $29. Why ask $100? A trick learned from Steve Blank: ask an insanely high price, and people will say "You're crazy, but I'd pay this," revealing their highest price.

**Results**: many email sign-ups (usable at launch) and pricing information. 50% said, in effect, "You're criminals, that's way too much." 30% named their highest price: $20, $10, $50, $80. And, very surprisingly, 20% said "$100? Where can I send the money? You're solving a problem for me." All from a landing page that cost nothing but some time and design work. The app turned out to be a great success. Do you systematically test your assumptions before spending money?

### Case study: Owlet baby monitor, from idea to validated business model

This case (from the winners of the 2013 International Business Model Competition at Harvard) shows how different a design-and-test pitch is from a traditional business plan pitch. Each test is reported with what it cost and how long it took.

**Test 1: wireless pulse oximeter for hospitals (invalidated)**. Tanner worked in a hospital with pulse oximetry, the little red light clipped to your finger that monitors vital signs. He saw a pain: the cord. Patients didn't like it, nurses didn't like it, it tangled and got in the way. Assumption: other nurses feel the same, so a wireless pulse oximeter used by nurses could be sold through hospitals. He talked to 58 nurses: 93% wanted it over the existing technology. But hospital administrators told a very different story: it wasn't a pain for them, it was about money, it wasn't cost-effective, and they said no. Lesson: **your user is not always your customer**. The users loved it but had no purchasing power, so the hypothesis went back to unvalidated. Cost: nothing; time: about a week. Time to pivot.

**The pivot: in-home infant monitoring**. Kurt Workman had experienced in his own family the pain of losing a child to respiratory failure. Idea: use the same technology at home for infants, monitoring vital signs (blood oxygen, heart rate) and alerting parents if there's an emergency during the night, giving them peace of mind. Supporting facts: SIDS is the number one cause of infant death in the United States and is often respiratory-related, which raises parents' anxiety a lot; four million babies are born in the US every year. Assumption: a lot of parents feel this pain. Assumed solution: a smart anklet on the baby, sending data wirelessly to a relay station that alerts and alarms parents in an emergency and pushes information to a smartphone. A complete pivot to a new segment, parents at home, with a peace-of-mind value proposition.

**Two risks**: market risk (will someone buy it?) and technology risk (can we build it?). They tackled market risk first because it was easier to test and more important.

**Test 2: is there a pain? (validated)**. A survey of 105 mothers gave an average of 8.5 out of 10 on wanting the product; 96% said they would use it; many said "This is awesome, take my money now." Very early validation, but pointing the right way. Real validation came from a class project: a website and a video that was pure smoke and mirrors showing what the product could do. It leaked out and was picked up by 40 news agencies in 13 countries, producing over 500 emails, mostly parents begging for the product ("We're not sleeping well at night," "We'll buy a prototype if that's all you have"). Fifteen distributors worldwide reached out; one from a major competitor offered to drop an account worth $2 million a year to take theirs; the fastest-moving wanted $50,000 in presales to get into its market quickly. All from building virtually nothing. Cost: $220; time: about two weeks.

**Test 3: channel and price (assumption overturned)**. Channel: they first assumed hospitals, but pediatricians and mothers showed retail was the best place to sell. Price: production cost implied a retail price around $200, and they doubted they created that much value, so assumed they'd have to rent the product; their initial survey pointed the same way. At Babies R Us they were told products span a huge price range, and the best sellers are in the $200 range. They didn't believe it, so they called 33 other baby stores, who said the same. They were completely wrong: 71% of baby monitors purchased today are at the $200 to $250 price point. Lesson: **surveys can lie if worded badly**. Asking "What would you expect to pay?" gave misleading results; asking "What's the maximum you would pay?" changed them completely. They also A/B tested on their website: people clicked a "reserve" button, believing they were buying, at different price points. That revealed demand elasticity and the demand curve, with a profit-maximizing price of $299. Cost: $30; time: three weeks.

**Test 4: can we build it? Wireless transmission (validated)**. They started with wireless transmission because it was easier. The proof of concept did everything they wanted, even though it looked ugly. Cost: $300; time: seven weeks.

**Test 5: infant pulse oximetry (validated with redesign)**. Back at the hospital they interviewed nurses and watched them use current pulse oximeters, and realized their design wouldn't work; for technical reasons they switched to a sock-like design. They tested it for over 120 hours in 26 homes, including 7 overnight tests with parents, and found they could build it. Cost: $500; time: twelve weeks.

**Test 6: regulation (validated, but sobering)**. They learned late that the product needed FDA clearance. The first person they asked said it would cost over a million dollars. They talked to 16 professionals and the labs they'd need, and established it would cost at most $200,000 and take 13 months. Cost of this validation: $200; time: three weeks. But it was bittersweet: $200,000, 13 months, and all that liability before getting a product into customers' hands is hardly running lean.

**Test 7: a lower-risk first product (validated)**. What triggered FDA clearance was the alarm: alarm plus pulse oximetry requires clearance. What if they dropped the alarm? The same product without it could still do many interesting things with the data: an "infant health tracker." This was unvalidated, so they revalidated. With clipboards, they interviewed 81 people in person at retail locations, showing four sheets of paper and asking which they would buy: theirs or three other products at the same price points. 20% chose theirs. They also learned the market isn't one-size-fits-all: a spectrum from the less-worried ("hippie-type") mom to the very worried. The less-worried kept saying "I wouldn't want an alarm on this; it would drive me crazy," which floored them: the alarm version wouldn't even capture that part of the market. Starting with the health tracker would address a completely separate market, avoid cannibalizing their own sales, and be much less risky. The flagship alarm product for the worried parents would launch a few months later, after learning and avoiding some risk with the first product. Cost: nothing; time: about three weeks.

**Still to validate**: some subscription-like revenue streams, and key partners with operations and retail experience.

**Totals**: all this for just over $1,000 and 24 weeks, with hundreds of one-on-one interviews and attempted fake sales, not just surveys. Plan: launch the non-alarm health tracker (no FDA needed, lower risk) first, then the flagship alarm product about seven months later.
