## 1. The Owner Mindset: From Operator to Owner

### The question every CEO asks

The most common question business leaders ask is "How do I grow my business and make more money?" That is a CEO's question. Founders ask a different one: "How do I make my product better?" This material is built for the CEO question: doing a better job of running the business end of the business. For most people that means a transition from operator to owner.

### Operator vs owner

**What it is**: Two different ways of relating to a business. In the operator world the business runs the owner. In the owner world the owner runs the business into the business.

**Why it matters**: The operator world looks like being tired, playing whack-a-mole and pin the tail on the donkey, with your hair on fire. There are so many fires that there is no time to figure out who has the book of matches: somebody is lighting all these fires. Business owners struggle financially because they are too busy growing and operating the business and not running the business into the business. It is not that they do not want to; nobody ever taught them how. There are skills and tools required to make the transition, and financial optics is the first of them.

A common symptom: owners who have been in business five or ten years and say, "I used to be passionate about my business, but now I hate it." The reason is usually that profits are going down or staying flat, the business is not growing, and it is running them instead of them running it.

On a bumper sticker: **Great operators get tired. Great business owners get rich.**

A second bumper sticker: **You cannot create sustainable success in business by becoming a better operator.** There is no way to do it.

### Structure and leverage

**What it is**: The two things that differentiate an operator from an owner. Operators tend to have very low leverage and virtually no structure.

**Why it works**: **Opportunity without structure is chaos.** When most owners look at their business with an owner's hat on, they see lots of chaos and drama, and **drama costs money**: every time drama goes up, you have to get out your checkbook. Minimizing drama and chaos means baking in structure and leverage, and that is what creates the opportunity to grow. Without leverage and structure you are a hand crank, and if you try to grow a hand crank, you wind up with more crankers.

### Growth is strategic, not tactical

**What it is**: Most people approach growth tactically: "I need search engine optimization, a digital media strategy, a rebuilt website." All of those might be important, but every one of them is tactical. What is missing is the strategic thinking about what has to happen for a business to grow sustainably.

**Why it works**: It does no good to grow a tumor. Having a bigger tumor does not eradicate the tumor. No doctor has ever said, "All your problems would be solved if you could just gain fifty more pounds." The key is not "How do I grow it?" but "What foundation do I need to lay?", and that foundation has to do with structure and leverage.

**Example: Southwest Airlines.** The founder sketched the idea on a napkin in a restaurant: three dots, Dallas, Houston and Austin, joined in a triangle. When the people at the table said, "There are lots of people in the airline business. How are you going to make money?", Herb answered: **"A plane on the ground makes no money."** That was the whole strategy. Southwest has made more money in the last ten years than the rest of the airline industry cumulatively since Orville and Wilbur Wright. Not because the planes are new, the crews attractive, the food good or the seats wide. Everything about how Southwest is organized is about keeping ground time very low and turning the plane around. It turns out that to make a lot of money in the airline business you have to get very good at one thing: getting bags on and off the plane as quickly as possible. That is strategy, and it has nothing to do with SEO or a new website.

### It is never what you do, it is how you do it

**What it is**: Business success is not about the product.

**Why it works / Examples**:
- **McDonald's.** Asked to name a favorite restaurant, nobody names McDonald's, yet nobody in the history of mankind has made more money in the restaurant business. The cheeseburgers are poor (the French fries are spectacular), and yet they out-earned everybody.
- **Southwest Airlines.** The seats are not roomy, and yet they made more money than everybody else.
- **Apple vs Microsoft.** Apple got there first with the better operating system and was beaten by Microsoft: roughly ninety percent Microsoft, ten percent Apple in adoption. The one who got there first with the better product lost.
- **Any product.** Name any product or service (cheeseburgers, chewing gum, seminars, television sets, houses). Given one week, you can find at least one person who has made millions and at least a hundred people who have gone broke doing the exact same thing.

### Founder vs CEO

**What it is**: Two entirely different jobs. As founder, your job is to create. As CEO, your job is to execute. On the day a founder starts a company, they looked at every other one of the eight billion people on the planet and decided they were the most qualified to be CEO, and put on that hat.

**Why it matters**: Founders get confused about which hat to wear and spend an inordinate amount of time tweaking the product as if tweaking the product would somehow equal more sales. That is the founder's trap: "it's all about the product."

**Example**: It is extraordinarily rare for the scientist who created the space shuttle to also be the astronaut. Founders build a vehicle for a certain environment: fast, on a straight line, in smooth conditions. Then the environment changes, potholes and detours appear, and the driver takes the Ferrari off the road into a pasture, where it was never built to go. Founders and CEOs are trying to do something very hard: create a product or service and simultaneously learn to drive the car. Driving the car well means reaching the destination most efficiently and effectively, with the fewest wrecks, consuming the least amount of gas. That is a skill set.

### The better question: what did the big guys do?

"How do I get big so I can get rich?" is the silent saboteur of small businesses. A better question: **What did the big guys do that caused the profits and cash flow that allowed them to get big?** The answer is not one thing. Business is complex, and anyone who says it is not is either stupid or trying to sell you their flavor-of-the-month Kool-Aid. You cannot create and sustain business success without business skills and tools; when you learn the tools, you get the results that are possible.

The target for the financial part of this material: given the financial statements of a disguised Fortune 100 company, with no company name and no years shown, you should be able to tell from the numbers alone whether you do or do not want to own the stock, and why.

### The four hats

**What it is**: Four hats available to wear in business. They are wardrobe, not identity. You are not "an artist" or "an operator"; you are a human being with a comfort zone and a sweet spot you gravitate to, and with the capacity to learn, grow and build new muscles. You can decide at different points in time which hat to put on, but you have to know which hat you have on.

**The artist hat**
- Focus: my passion; excited about my idea. This is where founders usually start.
- Plan: create something, and it is going to be fun.
- Strategy: I love my idea. That is the whole strategy.
- Outcome: fulfillment. It is a hobby.
- Motto: If it's not fun, I'm not going to do it.
- When to wear it: whenever something has to be created, such as writing a book, creating new content or starting a new business.

**The operator (technician) hat**
- Focus: value comes from time and effort. The more time invested and effort exerted, the better we will do.
- Plan: I have to break a sweat; I have to control it.
- Strategy: react. Every person whose hair is on fire is in operator mode, reacting all day long. You get to the end of the day and say, "I'm so tired, but I didn't get anything done."
- Outcome: the business runs them instead of them running the business.
- Motto: If it's going to get done, I'm just going to have to do it.
- This is the operator trap: "I have to break a sweat in order to have progress."
- When to wear it: after something is created, if it is going to get traction, someone has to deliver it.

**The owner hat**
- Focus: value comes from structure and leverage.
- Plan: monetize it.
- Strategy: lead it and coach it.
- Outcome: you run your business instead of your business running you.
- Motto: **Measure results, change activities.**

**The board or investor hat**
- Focus: What don't I see? (WDIS). What you do not see about yourself are the assumptions you are making.
- Values: thinking. It is an intellectual sport.
- Plan: keep it; avoid doing something dumb.
- Strategy: sustain it. A board is about defense; an owner is about offense. You need both.
- Outcome: being really thoughtful about **priorities, resources and risk**.
- Motto: "Whoa. Slow down. Not so fast, cowboy." Have we thought about second-order consequences? Are there assumptions or a story in here causing us to think the way we are thinking?

**Why the board hat matters**: Over the last thirty years of Super Bowls and NBA finals, in about ninety-five percent of them the team with the best defense beat the team with the best offense. **Defense wins games. Offense lights up the crowd.** Most people spend the majority of their time operating or creating offense. You do have to put points on the board, but defense is at least as critical, and most people do not have a board and do not have this hat. So they take risks they should not take, get excessively optimistic, and make assumptions and tell stories that simply are not true.

**Example of switching hats**: Creating an event is artist work. Preparing for it is owner work. Delivering it is operator work. A business partner can say, "I need you to put on your owner hat for a minute," and both people know what kind of conversation is about to happen. In artist mode a partner's input is rarely needed; in owner mode it is mandatory.

### Getting rich by doing fewer dumb things

**What it is**: A question asked of more than a hundred thousand people: **How much money would you have right now if you could unwind any three financial decisions you have ever made?** Most people answer two times, five times, ten times more. The key to getting rich is to do fewer dumb things, which is exactly the opposite of what most people think.

**Why it works**: All problems started out as a good idea. Nobody wakes up and says, "Today I'm going to use only my third-best ideas." Nobody loads a shotgun, points it at their foot and pulls the trigger, and yet people blow their own feet off all the time. The board hat exists to prevent this.

**How to do it: the board's three questions**
1. What's the upside?
2. What's the downside?
3. Can I live with the downside?

Most people are really good at question one. If they have to, they can do question two. Only the pros do question three.

### Priorities: majors, minors and threshold effort

**What it is**: A board thinks in priorities, meaning what gets elevated off the to-do list. Per quarter you can have **two majors and three minors**. That is it.

**Why it works**: The concept of **threshold effort** comes from the book *Good Strategy, Bad Strategy* by Rumelt (R-U-M-E-L-T). Threshold effort is the amount of effort it takes to actually move something, as opposed to the amount it takes to touch it. Most people spend their day touching many things, and very few things move, because the right amount of prioritization or resources was never allocated to get the outcome. **An owner who touches but doesn't move** sabotages both financial success and growth.

**How to do it**:
1. Choose the quarter's priorities: two majors, three minors. There cannot be twenty.
2. Give each priority incremental calendar time, so you can do more than touch it: you can move it.
3. For each priority, ask what resources must be exerted to actually move it, and what the risks are.

### The three questions that take courage

**What it is**: Three questions every CEO and leader should ask, and asking them takes courage.

1. **What am I missing?** These are blind spots. The person with the fewest blind spots always wins.
2. **Where am I wrong?** We usually go wrong in our assumptions and the story we tell ourselves. We are very good at making assumptions and very good at telling a story. The problem is that **when assumptions meet facts, facts always win.** We let our assumptions and story become a substitute for the facts, and we mistakenly believe that a description of the destination can substitute for the road map: that if we visualize it enough it will somehow turn into success. The result is people who are very good at visualizing but have no clue how to pick up the business end of a shovel, or where to dig.
3. **What do I need to learn?**

**Why it works**: Every failure of leadership, without exception, has at its root a lack of courage: courage to make the hard decisions, to say what needs to be said, to ask for help, to admit "I don't know." One of the most powerful phrases an effective CEO can say is **"I don't know yet."** Instead of saying it, we dream something up, tell a story, create assumptions, then do something stupid and pay a dumb tax.

**The dumb tax**: The root of every dumb tax is three things: **excessive optimism, unexamined assumptions and ignored risk.**

### Five core beliefs about mastering business

1. **There is no such thing as a natural business owner.** Nobody is born good at business or investing. The people who are good at it have practiced, have a coach, study it, execute, correct, execute again and correct again. It is a never-ending process. Nobody won a gold medal by subscribing to the four-hour work week, which is a strategy for lifestyle, not success. Like every course, success depends on what you do after you leave. The common failure is **shelf help, not self help**: a shelf of binders full of terrific information you forgot to use. Keep a running page of VIPs (very important points) for ideas you must follow up on; you only get the benefit if you execute on what you learn. Repeating material you have seen before is not wasted: it is another trip to the gym that makes the muscles more solid, and people who repeat the same course six or eight times report a new insight every time, because they come back at a different place in their business.
2. **Trial and error is a pathetic way to learn anything.** Pin the tail on the donkey is a stupid game: a blindfolded, dizzy twelve-year-old with low self-esteem, spun around ten times, trying to pin a tail on a picture of a donkey's rear with no idea where it is. That is how many people run their businesses. When they tire of that game they switch to whack-a-mole, and spend their time blindfolded, dizzy and worn out because they are playing the wrong game.
3. **Practice does not make perfect. Practice makes permanent.** Practicing the wrong thing gives you a bad habit. Example: a golfer who had their first lesson after twenty-five years of playing, and had spent those years practicing hard and doing the wrong thing. Hitting balls on a range next to the professional Tom Kite, the golfer watched Kite's helper stand a hundred and eighty yards out in the middle of the range catching Kite's balls in a sack without moving his feet. Worried aloud about hitting the kid, the golfer was told: "I've watched your swing. All you've got to do is aim at him. If you aim at him, there is zero probability you're going to hit this kid." Figure out what you should be practicing, then practice the right things.
4. **The person with the fewest blind spots always wins.** Surround yourself with people who will **smell your breath and then offer you a mint.** Nobody can smell their own breath. You need people who help with your blind spots (scotomas), the things you cannot see. It takes courage to ask and courage to listen.
5. **The people with the best choices have the best life.** People with poor choices tend to have poor lives. The purpose of this learning is to improve the quality of your choices.

## 2. Financial Optics: Why You Need Dials in the Cockpit

### The cockpit and the dials

**What it is**: Every business has been handed a dashboard at some point, usually by its accountants. The financial statements are dials in a cockpit. Financial optics means being able to read those dials.

**Why it matters**: One of the main reasons businesses struggle to make more money is that the person flying the plane cannot read the dials. If you cannot read the dials, you are flying by the seat of your pants, and the seat of your pants is not a successful strategy for flying an airplane or a business. Without dials (or with dials you cannot read) you do not know which direction you are headed unless you happen to see a road or river out the window, you do not know how much fuel is left, and you do not know where the airport is. You wind up off course.

**Example: the pilot at the cockpit door.** Imagine boarding a plane and the captain, greeting passengers at the cockpit door, looks over her shoulder and says, "Holy cow. Look at all those dials." You would run off the plane. Nobody wants to fly with a pilot who does not understand the dials.

**Example: the helicopter crash that killed Kobe Bryant.** The pilot decided to fly the helicopter when he could not see, did not have (or could not read) the dials, and instead of stopping, pushed on and flew at a hundred and eighty miles an hour. When you go a hundred and eighty miles an hour and cannot see, somebody is going to die. That happens in businesses all the time: owners struggle, the team struggles, customers struggle, employees struggle, the financial statements are weak, and it is because nobody in the cockpit understands the dials. If you cannot see, slow down and hover. Do not stomp on the gas.

### Profits but no cash

**What it is**: The classic symptom of missing optics. The reports usually exist (balance sheet, income statement, sometimes statement of cash flow), but owners look at them once a year, at tax time, and the only thing they pay attention to is how much tax they are paying.

**Why it happens**: Looking at the tax return, the owner sees how much the business made and asks, "Where's the money? How can I have this much profit and nothing in my bank account?" Or the business is profitable this month but struggling to meet payroll and debt service. The going-out-of-business signs seen in front of shops are there not because those businesses had no profits and not because the owner lacked passion, but because they had no cash. Something went wrong, there was a tumor somewhere in the business, and the owner could not see it.

**Example: the doctor and the lab test.** You go to the doctor, they draw blood, you give a urine sample, the lab results come back, and the doctor looks under the hood for problems nobody can see. Looking at someone, you would not know they have a pacemaker for atrial fibrillation (AFib), but a doctor needs to know. You can be terminally ill and not feel bad; you can be terminally ill and not even know it. Businesses can be too.

On a bumper sticker: **What you can't see can kill you.**

The owner's job is to ask: what dials and levers do I need in my cockpit so I can see what is happening? Financial statements are one part. The other part is an operating **dashboard**, which measures critical drivers. The financial statements measure the accumulation of activities in the business that resulted in a monetary gain or loss.

**Example: bowling with a curtain.** Imagine bowling with a curtain between you and the pins. Only a crazy person would play: you have no idea what the score is, where to aim or how to win, because you have no optics.

### Business is a game, and the language is accounting

**What it is**: Business is a game. Every game has a language and a scoreboard. The language of business is accounting (Warren Buffett's line). You cannot play a game if you cannot speak its language.

**Why it works**: A person who knows a game can hear a single word from its commentator and visualize exactly what just happened. A person who does not speak the language hears nothing useful and cannot play.

**Example: cricket.** Arriving in Sydney after about twenty-five hours of travel and waiting in a hotel lobby, a visitor who had never seen cricket watched it on every television. The commentators used words that meant nothing to him: an over (with no under), a six, a four, a wicket, a wicket keeper, a batsman, a slip, out for a duck, a golden duck, a googly, a silly mid on, a silly mid off, a silly point, a wrong'un, a flipper, a yorker, a wiki, a frog, rubbing the seam. Someone who knows cricket can see something happen with each word. Someone who does not, gets nothing. The explanation a friend offered did not help either: "You have two sides, one out in the field and one in. Each man that's on the side that's in goes out, and when he's out he comes in and the next man goes in until he's out. When they're all out, the side that's out comes in and the side that's been in goes out and tries to get those coming in out. Sometimes you get men still in but not out. When both sides have been in and out, including the not outs, that's the end of the game." Rugby, American football, golf, darts, cooking, astrophysics and music all have languages too. **If you can't speak the language, you can't play the game.**

### The scoreboard gives optics, and optics drive strategy

**What it is**: All games have a scoreboard. A scoreboard provides optics, and optics let you develop a strategy.

**Example**: A basketball scoreboard tells anyone who knows the game the score, the time left, the period, fouls and possession. On an American football scoreboard, if you are the home team in the fourth quarter, ahead 27 to 3 with four minutes left, your strategy should probably be to pull your best quarterback so he does not get hurt, because you have already won. If you are the visiting team, your strategy might be exactly the same, pulling your best quarterback, for the opposite reason: there is no way you are going to win. Same strategy, different why. On a cricket scoreboard, a person who does not speak cricket cannot even see who is playing, let alone who is winning, despite all the data.

Warren Buffett: **"If you can't read the scoreboard, you don't know the score. And if you don't know the score, you can't tell the winners from the losers."**

### There is no value in a number

**What it is**: A typical balance sheet shows cash, receivables and fixed assets; a typical income statement shows revenue, cost of goods sold and gross profit. The natural reaction is "So what? What am I supposed to do with this?" **There's no value in a number. The value is in knowing what to do about it.**

**Why it works**: Knowing there is a certain amount of accounts receivable, cash or debt is interesting. What is critical is whether, seeing that number, you know what to do about it. That requires a different kind of treasure map. The map most owners get has no X. A treasure map has value because there is a red X that says "dig here, dummy," not over there.

An effective treasure map gives you optics on:
- profit drains and how to plug them
- how to maximize cash flow
- how to increase returns
- how to anticipate a crisis
- how to spot danger signals
- how to optimize performance
- how to drive results
- how to maximize enterprise value

That is what accounting, the cockpit and the dashboard are for: optics, so that you can make different decisions going forward than you made in the past.

**Business mastery** is not just growing a business. It is understanding the dials and levers: what results am I getting, and which dials and levers let me produce different results in the future. **Measure results, change activities.**

**Example: the bathroom scale.** If you step on the bathroom scale in the morning and do not like the number, it does no good to take a baseball bat to the scale. The scale is just reporting the number. What is critical is what you need to do about it, and what you need to measure and change to get a different result.

### Decisions become activities, activities become numbers

**What it is**: Management makes decisions. Decisions get converted into activities. Activities get converted into numbers. Accountants, all day long, every day, convert activities into numbers and record those numbers on three pieces of paper: a balance sheet, an income statement and a statement of cash flow.

**Why it works**: If understanding accounting were the key to getting rich, all accountants would be rich. They are not, so it is not. The key is the reverse direction: **can I look at the numbers and reverse engineer back to the activities that caused the poor numbers?** That gives you the chance to change the activities and so get different numbers. **You are not going to change the results unless you change the activities.** What an owner needs is a mechanism, a Rosetta Stone, that converts the numbers back into the activities that caused them.

This is also why private equity firms and investors pay very close attention to management: they know management's decisions drive the activities that drive the financial results.

### Accounting is a report card, not a scoreboard

**What it is**: Accounting is not a dashboard and not a scoreboard. It is a report card.

**Why it matters**: A school report card shows an A, a B or a C, but you cannot tell what you got right or wrong from the report card alone; you only see the grade. Owners get their accounting, and if they like the numbers they go have a drink, and if they do not like the numbers they go have two drinks, without ever understanding which activities caused the problem.

### Learn the language, skip the jargon

**What it is**: Debits, credits, adjusting journal entries and trial balances are the jargon and the mechanics of accounting, not its language. Ask an accountant how a result happened and you may hear, "We debited this and credited that, then did an adjusting journal entry and posted the whole thing to a trial balance, and poof." It is like listening to someone from a small Scottish village: you know it is English, but you do not know what they are saying. The goal is to learn the language and avoid the jargon.

**Accounting is simply a transaction recording mechanism.** There are roughly seven hundred and fifty accounting systems available. QuickBooks is one of the most popular for small businesses in the United States; MYOB is popular in Australia. All they do is capture the activities as numbers and record them on a balance sheet, income statement or statement of cash flow. They are very good at that. What they do not tell you is what you got right or wrong.

### The four steps of business intelligence

**What it is**: The path from raw data to action.

**How it works**:
1. **Collect the data.**
2. **Organize the data into information.** QuickBooks and similar systems are very good at this step.
3. **Analyze and evaluate the information to turn it into knowledge.** Information is not knowledge. This step needs a tool built to analyze and evaluate. (CFO Scoreboard is one such tool: it takes QuickBooks or MYOB output and converts it into knowledge. Other people use other tools.)
4. **Use the knowledge for insight, a decision and action.**

**Why it works: clarity equals power.** Power is the ability to act. Every time you are unclear, you take either no action or tentative action, and **tentative action begets tentative results.** When you lack clarity, have made up a story, or are running on wild assumptions, you act tentatively and without insight.

### A template beats memorization

**What it is**: Accounting's jargon makes it feel like learning a new numbering system. The fix is a template, a way to hold the numbers in your mind, rather than memorizing.

**Example**: Imagine a new numbering system: 1 is drawn as a backwards L, 2 as a U, 3 as a forward L, 4 as a backward C, 5 as a box, 6 as a C, 7 as a 7, 8 as an upside-down U and 9 as a backward 7. Study it, then try to write 489. You are stumped, not because you have not seen it, but because you have not memorized it. Study harder for 489, and then you are asked for 286 and you are stumped again. Now draw a tic-tac-toe grid and place 1 to 9 in the squares: each symbol is simply the shape of the lines around its square. Write 286 now and you can do it immediately, because you did not have to memorize anything. You had a template. The balance sheet "box" used throughout this document works the same way: by the end you are doing accounting in your head, without paper.

### Better optics, better decisions, more money

On a bumper sticker: **If I have better optics, I make better decisions. If I make better decisions, I make more money.** Poor optics lead to poor decisions, and poor decisions lead to a dumb tax. It happens to everybody who lacks optics.

**Example: blindfolded to the grocery store.** Everyone can visualize the route from home to their usual grocery store: backing out of the driveway, the stop sign, the right turn. Put someone in a car blindfolded and ask them to drive it, and a hundred percent of people would have a wreck, with no exceptions, even though they know the way. Knowing the way does not mean you can get there safely. You have to be able to see. If you can see, you might not know where you are going, but you will not die getting there.

### Time is always now

A small closing idea: an investment banker who admired watches never wore one. Asked why, he said he always knew what time it was. Challenged to prove it, he answered: "Now." Whatever your watch says, the time to act is now.

## 3. Trends, Relationships and Measurement

### The key to financial optics: trends and relationships

**What it is**: You cannot get optics from a single number. Optics come from **trends** (what is happening to a number over time) and **relationships** (how one number compares with another).

**Why it works**: A number without context tells you nothing. If a business has a million dollars of cash, you do not know whether to applaud or boo, buy or sell. If last month it had ten million, you are nervous. If last month it had zero, at least cash is moving the right direction. The same is true of a million dollars of profit or of accounts receivable.

**Example: the three-hundred-and-fifty-pound patient.** Walk into a new doctor's office weighing three hundred and fifty pounds and the doctor does not know whether to applaud or scold you. If you weighed nine hundred pounds a year ago, you get a standing ovation. If you weighed a hundred and fifty pounds a year ago, you get a scolding. It is all about the context of the number.

**The problem**: What most accountants deliver is one column of numbers. Sometimes two (this month and year to date), sometimes a third column of percentages, and that is it. One column on a balance sheet or income statement is useless for optics.

### Multiple time periods on one page

**What it is**: The single most important format change. Write this in your VIPs: **I need multiple time periods on one page, not on multiple pages.** A pile of January financials, then a separate pile for February, then another pile, does nothing for you.

**How to do it**:
1. Put periods side by side in columns on one page, for example 2018, 2019, 2020 and 2021, with revenue, cost of goods sold, gross profit and each operating expense running down the rows. Comparing one column with the next shows the trend immediately.
2. Do the same for the balance sheet: cash, accounts receivable, accounts payable and inventory, period against period.
3. For **cyclical businesses**, compare like periods, not consecutive ones. A retailer that struggles for ten months and makes most of its money in November and December learns little from comparing November with October, but a lot from comparing November this year with November last year. The same applies to businesses with a summer bump or a holiday bump.
4. Add **year-to-date comparisons**: year to date this year against year to date last year.
5. Add **annual performance year over year**: are we getting better or worse?

**Multiple time periods is your friend.** Without them you have no ability to measure.

### Relationships

**What it is**: One number compared with another number within the same time period, then tracked over time.

**How to do it**:
1. Pick a pair that belongs together, for example cost of goods sold and revenue.
2. Calculate the relationship for each period (2018, then 2019, 2020 and year to date this year).
3. Watch how the relationship changes over time.
4. Do the same with critical relationships on the balance sheet.

Relationships exist on both the income statement and the balance sheet, and both need monitoring. You only see them if the report is formatted to show them.

### What gets measured

**What it is**: Three linked statements:
- What gets measured is what gets done.
- What gets measured is what gets managed.
- What gets measured and reported can improve exponentially.

**How to run a business on it**: A group of businesses, each eight, ten or fifteen million dollars a year in revenue, each making twenty to twenty-five percent profit with no debt, was run (not operated) this way:
1. When you put on your owner hat, **decide what you are going to measure.**
2. **Announce it.** Be crystal clear with the team: "Here is what I'm measuring. Make sure you are managing the things I'm measuring, so we get the results I want."
3. Measure **critical drivers**, and do not scatter attention. If the leader looks in too many places, or distracts the team with too many new ideas, the team does not execute on the critical drivers.

**Why it works: wherever the leader looks, the team looks.** Point at something on the ceiling in a room full of people and everyone turns to see what you are pointing at. Your team does the same thing all day. If you are not pointing at anything, and have not been crystal clear about the standards that need to be hit, they have nothing to look at.

### Standards, not goals

**What it is**: Goals and targets tend to be wishful thinking. Stepping on the scale and weighing five pounds more than you want, the first thing you say is "I need a new goal," which really means "I wish I weighed five pounds less." A **standard** is a performance metric you expect to be hit. It is not a target and not a goal; it is an expectation.

**How to do it**:
1. Set the standard (the expectation).
2. Design and build the **yellow brick road** that gets you from Munchkinville to Oz. If all you do is visualize Oz without designing the road, you have a goal of reaching Oz with no path to get there. For most businesses the gap is not an inability to visualize Oz; it is that they have not built the road.
3. Expect trouble on the road: flying monkeys, the wicked witch, a field of poppies where you lie down and go to sleep like Dorothy. The road still gets you from where you are to what you want.
4. Measure along the way. Measurement creates sustainability.

### What measurement does

Measurement does two things:
1. **It focuses management's attention on what is most important.** What gets measured is what gets managed.
2. **It tells you what to improve and whether you are making progress.** The only way to know is with financial optics. "I want to get healthier" gives you nothing to do; "I want to lose five pounds" gives you something to measure.

**Pitfall: fuzzy priorities.** Every quarter, someone announces priorities such as "we're going to improve our sales," "improve our culture" or "hire some A players." Nobody knows what those mean. Fuzzy, hand-waving, glossy priorities need to be tightened until there is a target on the wall, so you know whether you are getting closer to what you said you wanted.

**Example: the marksman.** Expert marksmen put the target on the wall, aim, pull the trigger and see where the hole is. Amateurs shoot a hole in the wall, then paint a target around the hole and say, "I'm pretty good. I'm a natural." It does no good to paint the target after the hole is already in the wall.

### Alignment and the loop that is "running your business"

The trick is alignment: can you get your team aligned with you, the CEO and owner, on what is going to be measured? Without that alignment you have no shot at improving financial results or growing sustainably. The foundation is what you measure; measurement focuses attention and tells you whether you are improving.

**The loop**: measure results, change the activities, measure the results, change the activities, measure the results, change the activities. **That is called running your business**, and it is the CEO's job, not the founder's. A founder's job is to create something; a CEO's job is to execute on it. You need both.

## 4. The Three Accounting Report Cards

Accountants have three report cards. They are the primary dials in your cockpit.

### Report card 1: the balance sheet

**What it is**: A record of the "things and stuff" a business has, and whether the business owes or owns them.

**Things and stuff** (assets) include:
- **Cash.**
- **Accounts receivable** (money customers owe you).
- **Inventory** (things and stuff you buy to turn around and resell later; many businesses have none).
- **Property, plant and equipment** (PP&E): a machine, a truck, a computer.

**What you owe** (liabilities) are payables:
- **Accounts payable**: money owed to your vendors.
- **Notes payable**: money owed to your lenders.
- **Taxes payable**: money owed to the government.

**What you own** (equity). A business owns only two things:
- **The investment** made by the shareholders or partners.
- **Its earnings.** A business can distribute its earnings, in which case it pays a **dividend**, or retain them, in which case they stay on the balance sheet as **retained earnings**.

**Why it is called a balance sheet**: Things and stuff equals what you owe plus what you own. The two sides always balance.

**The equation**: Accountants do not say things and stuff, owe and own. They say **assets = liabilities + equity**. If you know any two, you can work out the third.

**Examples**:
- Things and stuff of 100, owing 40: you own 60.
- Equity of 10, liabilities of 5: assets must be 15, because the left side always equals the right side.

**A snapshot**: A balance sheet is a picture of one moment in time, like taking a photo with your phone. That is why the top of a balance sheet usually reads "as of" a date, such as "as of December 31, 2020" or "as of March 31, 2021."

**Where things sit**: Cash is at the top left of the balance sheet; earnings are at the bottom right. They could not be placed any further apart, as if to make sure you never confuse earnings with cash.

### Report card 2: the income statement

**What it is**: Also called the **P&L**, **profit and loss** or **statement of operations**; all the same thing. It records:
- **Sales**, also called revenue or turnover.
- **Expenses and costs** incurred to produce those sales.
- Whatever is left over: **profits**, also called net income, earnings or the bottom line.

**Why the bottom line alone misleads**: Owners see the bottom-line number first and are happy or sad, then look at revenue and are happy or sad about that. But one business with revenue of 10 and expenses of 6 has 4 left over, and another with revenue of 1,000 and expenses of 996 also has 4 left over. Same bottom line. The bottom line is just the result of the two things above it. Only when you know the revenue and the expenses, and whether they are in the right relationship, do you know whether to be happy or sad about the 4. Without that context it is meaningless.

**A movie, and a theory**: Unlike the balance sheet, the income statement is not a snapshot; it is a movie, covering a month, a quarter or a year. And it is a **theory**, a critical and important theory, because **you can't spend your profits. You can only spend cash.**

**Income statement structure** (see the worked example in section 7):
- Revenue
- minus cost of goods sold (if the business has it) = **gross profit**
- minus operating expenses such as general and administrative (G&A) and selling = **EBITDA**, which is operating income (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization)
- minus depreciation and amortization = **EBIT** (earnings before interest and taxes)
- minus interest = **EBT** (earnings before taxes)
- minus taxes = **net income**

**Why break it into layers**: Each subtotal is a point of comparison. With several subtotals, compared against the last three, four, six or ten periods (last month, last year, year to date against last year to date), you can locate the problem: is it in operating expenses or in cost of goods sold? You can zero in and figure it out.

### What counts as an expense, and what counts as cost of goods sold

**The rule**: **Anything that reduces earnings is an expense.** Cost of goods sold is one type of expense. Others include G&A, marketing, depreciation, interest and taxes. The only reason cost of goods sold is deducted separately at the top is so you can see gross profit.

**How to spot cost of goods sold**: Whenever there is a sale, ask whether it was free. If you sold eggs, you used eggs, and the cost of those eggs is cost of goods sold.

**How cost of goods sold behaves**: In theory, if you have no revenue, you have no cost of goods sold. When revenue goes up, cost of goods sold goes up; when revenue goes down, it goes down.

**Classifying payroll**:
- **Commissions** paid only when there is a revenue event can be included in cost of goods sold if you wish.
- **Salaried people paid whether or not there are sales**: be careful. You could put them in cost of goods sold, but in a poor revenue month you could end up showing cost of goods sold at a hundred and ten percent of revenue.

**Pitfall: cost of goods sold above revenue.** One business showed fifty thousand dollars of revenue and sixty thousand dollars of cost of goods sold, and the owner insisted it was on accrual accounting. Under accrual accounting, cost of goods sold cannot exceed revenue. That cannot happen. Items had been miscategorized, and it destroyed the owner's optics. The aim is not what is right for the accountant; it is optics that support better decisions.

### Report card 3: the statement of cash flow

**What it is**: The dial for cash. There are three kinds of cash, and the sum of the three is **cash flow**.

**O cash: operating cash.** Cash generated or used in the operations of the business.
- Good for operating cash: making a sale for cash, collecting accounts receivable.
- Bad for operating cash: paying bills, making payroll, paying off an account payable.

**I cash: investing cash.** Cash generated or used in investing activities, which are **always and only** buying or selling property, plant and equipment.
- Buying a new computer or a piece of real estate is bad for investing cash.
- Selling a truck or a piece of real estate is good for investing cash.

**F cash: financing cash.** Cash generated or used in financing activities.
- Borrowing from the bank is good for financing cash.
- Repaying the bank is bad for financing cash.

**Cash flow** for a period is how much operating, investing and financing cash went up or down. You start the month with a certain amount of cash, cash flow happens, and that gives you the amount at the end of the month.

**A movie, and a fact**: Like the income statement, the statement of cash flow is a movie covering a period. Unlike the income statement, it is a **fact**, not a theory, because you can spend cash. **Profits are a theory and cash is a fact.**

**Not all cash is created equal.** One of the three kinds of cash is far more important than the other two: operating cash, the cash the business itself generates from its operations (see sections 8 and 9).

If you want to sound like a pro to your accountant, ask: "How's my O cash doing?" That is literally how it is referred to.

**Why most owners have never seen one**: Fewer than five percent of businesses under twenty million dollars in revenue have ever seen a statement of cash flow. Accountants do not tend to prioritize it, because most small-business accountants are tax accountants, not management accountants. The person doing the books is also the person doing the tax return, which helps explain why owners are not getting all the report cards they need.

### Profits and cash are different things

Because you can only spend cash, you can end a month with a profit and still struggle to make payroll or repay the bank, or end a year showing a profit with no cash.

- Could a business have a million dollars of cash and lose fifty thousand dollars? Yes.
- Could a business have five million of profits and only a hundred thousand of cash? Of course.

Profits and cash have a little to do with each other, but very little.

### How the three report cards fit together

**What it is**: The three papers are linked by two shared numbers.
- **Earnings** on the income statement is exactly the same number as earnings on the balance sheet.
- **Ending cash** on the statement of cash flow is exactly the same number as cash on the balance sheet.

**The world atlas and the street maps**: The balance sheet is the **world atlas**. It says there is such a thing as cash and such a thing as earnings. The income statement and the statement of cash flow are **street maps** that show how you got to those numbers.

**Example**:
- The balance sheet shows earnings of 4. How did you get to 4? The income statement (street map) says: revenue of 1,000, expenses of 996, leaving 4.
- The balance sheet shows cash of 100 in the upper left. How did you get to 100? The statement of cash flow (street map) says: you started the month with 40 and had cash flow of 60, so you ended with 100. It also shows how much of the 60 came from operations, how much from investing and how much from financing.

### The sequence: assets to revenue to profits to operating cash

**What it is**: The reason there are three dials. What every owner wants is to **turn assets into sales, sales into profits, and profits into cash**, specifically operating cash. The sequence bounces around the three papers: from assets (balance sheet) to revenue (income statement), revenue to profits (income statement), profits to operating cash (statement of cash flow).

**How to use it**:
1. **Minimize the assets it takes to produce a dollar of revenue.** You want revenue per dollar of assets going up.
2. **Minimize expenses** so you maximize profits.
3. **Convert profits into operating cash**, because you cannot spend profits.

**Pitfall: pimping your stuff.** Owners know they should minimize the assets it takes to produce a dollar of revenue, yet often do not act like it. If you reframed every purchase as "Does this drive incremental revenue?", you might choose differently. It is unclear how a twenty-five-hundred-dollar Mac produces more revenue than a seven-hundred-dollar Dell, yet most people own the Mac. That is fine for one computer, but do it throughout the business and you end up with far too many assets per dollar of revenue.

**Examples**:
- **Walmart.** Visit Walmart's headquarters in Bentonville, Arkansas to make a sales call and you wait in a reception area furnished with twenty-dollar folding lawn chairs. Walmart figured out that spending more on reception furniture does not drive incremental revenue, so it spends on things that do.
- **Warren Buffett.** He wrote in an annual letter that his cumulative spending on headquarters furniture and equipment over sixty years was two hundred and fifty thousand dollars in total. He understood that every dollar spent on office stuff is a dollar he cannot invest and compound at twenty percent.

**The principle: wealth creation and lifestyle are incompatible.** When you are trying to accumulate wealth, jacking up your lifestyle sabotages the wealth. It is as obvious as the fact that eating a dozen Krispy Kremes every day and being skinny are incompatible. This is not an argument for or against nice things; it is an argument for being thoughtful, because the sequence runs from assets to revenue to profits to operating cash.

## 5. Learning Accounting by Running an Egg Business

### The method

**What it is**: Accounting learned by running a small business for three weeks and recording every activity on a balance sheet, without debits, credits or journal entries. The story is true: a twelve-year-old's egg business in Houston, condensed from a whole summer into three weeks. Every activity really happened except one (the insurance policy in week three, used to teach prepaid expenses).

**Why it works**: You convert words (activities) into numbers and place those numbers on the balance sheet, again and again, until you can do it in your head. Then you can run the process in reverse: numbers back into activities.

**How to do it**: For the three weeks, suspend any attempt to apply the lessons to your own business. Just be a twelve-year-old in the egg business. Work each transaction yourself rather than watching, and check your answer with a neighbor ("coaching is a high form of cheating").

**Three habits used on every transaction**:
1. **Look at the prior balance sheet. Don't memorize.** Every new balance sheet starts from the last one: "I used to have X; this happened; now I have Y."
2. **Draw the box.** Before calculating, sketch a simple balance sheet box and mark where the transaction lands: cash near the top left, receivables and inventory below it, equipment lower on the left; payables at the top right, earnings near the bottom right. Say what goes up and what goes down, then check both sides moved by the same amount.
3. **Check the balance.** Assets must equal liabilities plus equity after every transaction.

### The origin story

At twelve years old, on a hot summer lunchtime in Houston, the next-door neighbor, Mrs. Chapman, came through the unlocked back door mid-cake, out of eggs, and borrowed a dozen from the boy's mother. The next day she returned a dozen: the cake was spectacular, and she wanted to know where the eggs came from. They came from Mamaw, the boy's grandmother, who had a five-to-ten-acre ranchette in Rosenberg, about thirty miles from Houston, with a garden, chickens, a cow, a couple of goats and a donkey. The family of six drove there every Saturday in a station wagon and brought back six dozen eggs a week.

Mrs. Chapman asked the mother to bring back extra eggs for her. The mother said no. The boy asked:
- "How many dozen a week do you need?" Five dozen.
- "Are you willing to pay, or looking for a freebie?" Willing to pay.
- "What does the grocery store charge?" Sixty cents a dozen.
- "If I bring you five dozen every week, will you pay me the same as the grocery store?" Yes.

He then called Mamaw on the family's one rotary-dial phone. It was a long-distance call, which in those days meant somebody had died, so she answered, "Who died?" Nobody had. Did she have extra eggs? Yes. Could he buy them? She offered to give them to him; he insisted on buying, and she charged thirty cents a dozen. A customer at sixty cents, a supplier at thirty: he was in business.

Mamaw had more than five dozen a week, so he went door to door around the neighborhood with a carton, offering farm-fresh eggs from Mamaw's at sixty cents a dozen, and handed each neighbor one egg to try. A week later everybody wanted eggs. Within three weeks he had outgrown Mamaw and was buying from the same place the grocery store did (without telling customers). Within six months he was selling five or six hundred dozen eggs a week and clearing about two hundred dollars a week, making station-wagon trips to pick up huge crates.

His father, an engineer who knew little about business, saw the money and gave him a ledger book. On its cover was a picture of a three-masted English sailing ship with the caption: **"A small leak will sink a great ship."**

Notation below: **Cash, AR** (accounts receivable), **Inv** (inventory), **Equip** (equipment), **Dep** (accumulated depreciation), **Prepaid**, **AP** (accounts payable), **NP** (notes payable), **Orig** (original investment), **APIC** (additional paid-in capital), **RE** (retained earnings), **EWTD** (earnings week to date).

### Week 1

**Slide 2: Invest $5.** From his cigar box of money earned mowing yards and doing odd jobs, he took five dollars and put it into a new cigar box labeled "egg business." That is an investment in the company. Cash (things and stuff) goes up by 5. Nothing is owed. The business owns the original investment of 5.
- Balance sheet: Cash 5 = Liabilities 0 + Orig 5. **5 = 5.**

**Slide 3: Borrow $10 from dad.** On the way to Mamaw's he realized he was about ten dollars short: all his marketing had paid off. His father offered two ways to help:
1. **Invest** ten dollars and become a partner, taking a share of the profits: half. ("That doesn't seem fair, I'm doing all the work." "Yes, but I'm putting up most of the money.")
2. **Lend** ten dollars: no share of the profits, but the boy pays rent on the money, called **interest**, and pays it back.

He chose the loan. "What happens if you don't pay me back?" "Sucks to be you." "Not good enough. I need collateral." "Dad, I'm twelve." "You've got a bicycle. If you don't pay me back, I take your bike." He took the loan.
- Cash was 5, add 10: Cash 15. Owed was 0, now 10. Orig 5. **5 + 10 = 15. 15 = 15.**

**Is collateral on the balance sheet?** No. Collateral given to a bank (or a father) never appears on the balance sheet. It lives in the note agreement, which here said the father was lending ten dollars, would charge interest, and could take the bike if not repaid in a certain period.

**Slide 4: Rename.** Things and stuff become **assets**; owe becomes **liabilities**; own becomes **equity**. Assets 15 = Liabilities 10 + Equity 5.

**Slide 5: Buy 50 dozen eggs at 30 cents a dozen, paid cash.** 50 x $0.30 = $15. Cash was 15 (look at the prior balance sheet), used 15: Cash 0. Inventory was 0, now 15. This is **trading one asset for another asset.** Nothing happens to earnings when you buy inventory.
- Cash 0 + Inv 15 = 15. NP 10 + Orig 5 = 15. **15 = 15.**

**Slide 6: The sales cheat sheet.** Sold 40 dozen at 60 cents a dozen, collected $24 cash.
- Sales: $24.
- The eggs were not free. 40 dozen at 30 cents = $12 of inventory used. That is **cost of goods sold** (negative 12).
- Gross profit so far: 24 minus 12 = 12.

**Slide 7: Record the sale on the balance sheet.** Cash was 0, add 24: Cash 24. Inventory was 15, used 12: Inv 3. Earnings week to date: 12.
- Assets: 24 + 3 = 27. NP 10. Orig 5 + EWTD 12 = 17. **17 + 10 = 27. 27 = 27.**

**Slide 8: Expenses.** Counting his cash, the boy was told by his father that the business had expenses: two dollars of gas for the drive to Rosenberg ("We go every week!" "It's two dollars."), and one dollar of rent for the garage refrigerator he was storing eggs in, the one that had sat unplugged and unused for five years until he cleaned it up. He paid three dollars. **Expenses reduce earnings.** Inventory is unaffected by paying dad.
- Cash 21 + Inv 3 = 24. NP 10. Orig 5 + EWTD 9 = 14. **14 + 10 = 24.**

**Slide 9: Repay the loan plus interest.** Annoyed by the interest, rent and gas, he repaid the ten dollars to unwind the arrangement. His father asked for the interest: one dollar. ("Ten percent a week? I could have gone to Guido.") **Interest is an expense, and expenses reduce earnings.** Total out: $11 ($10 principal, $1 interest).
- Cash 21 minus 11 = 10. Inv 3. Total assets 13. NP 0. Orig 5 + EWTD 8 = 13. **13 = 13.** End of week 1.

**Slide 10: The C / S / E cheat sheet.** Accountants do not use this page, but it is how to learn. Three columns: **C** (cash), **S** (sales), **E** (expenses). For each transaction, mark only what it did to cash, sales or expenses. The columns do not have to balance. The C column becomes the statement of cash flow; the S and E columns together become the income statement.

| Transaction | C | S | E |
|-|-|-|-|
| Original investment | +5 | | |
| Loan from dad | +10 | | |
| Buy 50 dozen at 30 cents | -15 | | |
| Sell 40 dozen at 60 cents | +24 | +24 | |
| Inventory used (cost of goods sold) | | | +12 |
| Gas and refrigerator | -3 | | +3 |
| Repay dad's loan | -10 | | |
| Interest | -1 | | +1 |

Key points from the sheet:
- **Buying eggs is not an expense.** When the eggs were bought (slide 5), cash went down 15 and inventory went up 15; nothing happened to earnings. The eggs sat on the balance sheet as inventory.
- **Selling eggs creates the expense.** When the eggs were sold, the $12 of eggs used did not touch the cigar box (it was already hit when they were bought), but expenses went up by 12.
- Back-of-the-napkin: sales 24, expenses 16 (12 + 3 + 1), profit probably 8. Cash: +5 +10 -15 +24 -3 -10 -1 = **+10**. Both match the last balance sheet.

**Slide 11: Week 1 income statement.**
- Revenue 24
- Cost of goods sold (12)
- Gross profit 12
- Gas (2), refrigerator (1), interest (1) = (4)
- **Earnings 8**, exactly what the slide 9 balance sheet shows. The balance sheet is the world atlas; the income statement is the street map to the 8.

**Slide 12: Week 1 statement of cash flow.**
- Collected from customers +24
- Bought inventory -15
- Borrowed +10
- Repaid -10
- Original investment +5
- Expenses and accounts payable paid -4 (3 + 1)
- Beginning cash 0, ending cash 10 (slide 9), so **cash flow +10**, which is what the column adds up to.

### Week 2

**Slide 13: Beginning balance sheet.** The beginning balance sheet of week 2 is exactly the same as the ending balance sheet of week 1. At 11:59 PM the photo of week 1 is taken; thirty seconds later, the photo of week 2 is taken, and nothing has happened in between. Likewise the balance sheet at the end of December 2020 is the beginning balance sheet for 2021. Balance sheets are bookends.

**Slide 14: Retain or distribute.** Week 2 cannot carry "earnings week to date" from week 1. The owner decides whether to leave the $8 in the business (retain it) or distribute it as a dividend. He retains it: RE 8, EWTD 0. (Accountants do this annually, not weekly.)
- Cash 10 + Inv 3 = 13. Orig 5 + RE 8 + EWTD 0 = 13. **13 = 13.**

**Slide 15: Borrow $30 from the bank.** Short of cash again, the boy asked his father whether he borrowed from his own dad. "No, I go to the bank." "I need a bank." His father offered to lend again; the boy declined ("this deal has gotten too expensive"). The father quietly called his banker. Told to walk in and say he wanted to "establish a relationship," the boy rode his bicycle to the bank, showed his financial records, and the banker, Gary, pulled a twenty and a ten from his billfold and slid them across the desk with a sticky note reading "I owe you thirty dollars," which the boy signed.
- Cash 10 + 30 = 40. Inv 3. Assets 43. NP 30 (was 0 on slide 14). Orig 5 + RE 8 = 13. **13 + 30 = 43. 43 = 43.**

**Slide 16: Sell leftover inventory at cost.** Unsure whether the leftover eggs had been kept refrigerated the whole time, he sold them to his brother for what he paid: $3. Cash 43, Inv 0. Total assets unchanged at 43: one asset traded for another. No profit (sales 3, cost of goods sold 3), so EWTD 0. **43 = 43.**

**Slide 17: Buy 60 dozen, half on credit.** Cash had become very important to him, so he asked Mamaw whether he could pay part now and part later. Of course. 60 dozen at 30 cents = $18 of inventory: $9 cash and a $9 IOU. The IOU creates a new category, **accounts payable** (money owed to Mamaw or another supplier). No earnings impact: nothing has been sold.
- Cash 43 minus 9 = 34. Inv 18. Assets 52. AP 9 + NP 30 = 39. Orig 5 + RE 8 + EWTD 0 = 13. **13 + 39 = 52.**

**Slide 18: The wagon, and capitalize vs expense.**

The delivery problem: eggs came in thirty-dozen crates. A plastic mop bucket with a wire handle held six dozen; hanging one on each handlebar grip, he could deliver twelve dozen at a time on his bicycle. Better: put a whole thirty-dozen crate in a wagon and tow it behind the bike.

At the store there were three wagons:
- A cheap, basic wagon (about seven dollars): regular wheels, regular paint.
- A medium wagon: nicer wheels, nicer handle, better paint.
- The top wagon (twenty dollars): sparkly paint, ball-bearing wheels, big fat rubber tires, an ergonomically correct handle.

He bought the twenty-dollar one. He pimped his ride.

**What capitalize vs expense means**: There are two ways to record a purchase like this.
- **Expensing** the wagon: cash goes down 20 and earnings go down 20 (the purchase goes on the income statement). Good for taxes, because lower profit means lower tax. **Horrible for optics.**
- **Capitalizing** the wagon: cash goes down 20 and equipment (property, plant and equipment) goes up 20 (the purchase goes on the balance sheet). One asset traded for another.

Cash is hit either way. The only question is what happens to the other half of the transaction.

**How to decide: the three-part test.** Capitalize when all three are true:
1. Will it last longer than one year?
2. Is it a significant amount of money?
3. Does it have future economic value? (He could sell the wagon the day after buying it for ten dollars. It is worth something; it is not consumed.)

The wagon meets all three, so the correct treatment is to capitalize it. That avoids polluting the income statement with an expense that would hit the week's earnings and give an inaccurate picture of how the business is really doing.

**Two sets of books**: Most people prioritize taxes, not optics, and expense everything. There is a way to do both: one set of books that is right for management optics, and another for reporting to the government. Google is described as keeping two sets of books, with a building larger than a conference venue full of management accountants and another building just as big full of tax people, on the same campus, that do not have each other's phone numbers, because what management needs is very different from what the government needs. Facebook and Apple do the same. Two sets of books (one for management, one for the government) is appropriate. Three sets of books is what Enron did; steer clear of that.

**The real mistake**: He paid twenty dollars instead of seven. He was always going to buy a wagon and would be more efficient either way, but not one extra egg would be sold because the wagon was fancier. **He bought an asset that would not increase revenue, and that is a mistake.**

- Cash 34 minus 20 = 14. Inv 18. Equip 20. Assets 52. AP 9 + NP 30 = 39. Orig 5 + RE 8 + EWTD 0 = 13. **52 = 52.** Only cash (down) and equipment (up) changed.

**Slide 19: Sales cheat sheet.** Sold 50 dozen at 60 cents a dozen = $30 of sales. 30 dozen were paid in cash ($18); 20 dozen were on an IOU from customers ($12), which is an **account receivable**. Inventory used: $15. Gross profit and profit so far this week: 15.

**Slide 20: Record the sale.** (Question from a participant: why is EWTD 15? It was 0 on slide 18, and this sale produced $15 of profit: $30 of sales minus $15 of eggs used. It is the only profit so far this week.)
- Cash 14 + 18 = 32. AR 0 + 12 = 12. Inv 18 minus 15 = 3. Equip 20. Assets 67.
- AP 9 + NP 30 = 39. Orig 5 + RE 8 + EWTD 15 = 28. **28 + 39 = 67. 67 = 67.**

The point is not the accounting; it is what you do with it.

**Slide 21: Depreciation.** The wagon is expected to last four years, so it is depreciated at five dollars a year.

**What depreciation is**: The government and the accountants agree an asset will not last forever, so its recorded value is reduced each year, and **depreciation is an expense, and expenses reduce earnings.** Each year: equipment value down 5, earnings down 5. Both sides fall by the same amount, so the balance sheet stays in balance.

**Why it reconciles with expensing**: Compare two balance sheets. Expense the wagon: cash -20, earnings -20 on day one. Capitalize it: cash -20, equipment +20, then over four years equipment falls 5 a year to 0 and earnings are reduced by a total of 20. **Four years from now, the capitalized balance sheet looks exactly like the expensed one did on day one.** It just takes four years to get there.

**Pitfall: depreciation is not real value.** It does not mean the wagon is actually worth less. It might be worthless the day after you buy it, or last a hundred years; the balance sheet will still say it is worth nothing after four years. Mastering depreciation is unlikely to make you rich.

**Why capitalize and then depreciate?** (A participant's question.) Buying the wagon used **investing cash**, not operating cash; it was not a twenty-dollar operating loss that week. Expensing it would distort the optics. And either way the cash was hit: the real difference from buying the fancy wagon is that cash took a twenty-dollar hit instead of a seven-dollar one, which is not what you do if you are thinking about getting rich.

**The problem: negative cash.** You cannot have negative cash in a cigar box. It can only exist in accounting, not in the real world. Balance sheets from Australia and New Zealand very often show negative cash somewhere. ### The decision review: money left on the table

**What it is**: Management makes decisions, decisions become activities, activities become numbers. So a business with seven dollars of profit and zero cash after three weeks is the product of specific decisions. Go back through them.

**The decisions**:
- **Pricing at sixty cents a dozen.** That was dumb. Eighty cents was easily achievable and would have added **$30 of profit and $30 of cash.**
- **Selling leftover inventory at cost** at the end of week 1 instead of at the usual markup forfeited **$3 of profit and $3 of cash.**
- **The wagon** consumed **$9 of profit** ($5 depreciation, $3 tires, $1 insurance) and **$24 of cash** ($20 wagon, $1 insurance, $3 tires).
- **Extending credit to a customer who didn't pay** cost $3. (Maybe don't ding yourself for this one: better receivables tracking might have helped, but she moved away.)
- **The rainy weekend**, when it just wasn't convenient to go out and sell ("nah, I don't feel like it"), cost **$15 of profit and $15 of cash** compared with every other week.
- **The woman with the five-dollar bill**: he could have given her the eggs, taken the five and said "you still owe me a dollar." That cost **$1 of profit and $1 of cash.**
- **The dropped eggs**, from getting sloppy and moving too many at once, cost the eggs, the profit and the cash: a **$6 swing**.

**The total**: **$67 of profit left on the table.** Instead of $7 of profit, the business could have had $73 (as stated; $7 plus $67 computes to $74), roughly **ten times** as much. Instead of zero cash, it could have ended with **$82 of cash**. Not every item was necessarily avoidable: back out the bad debt and the dropped eggs and it could still have been fifty-five or sixty dollars. He could still have had a wagon, just not the fanciest one, which alone would have saved **$13 of cash**.

**The lesson**: In every business, some decisions get made that do not support outstanding financial results. Take a hard look at the decisions you are making.

### Accounting in your head

No week 3 income statement or statement of cash flow is needed, because you can now do it without paper. Picture an empty balance sheet box and place each of these:
- A business borrows $5 from the bank.
- The same business has $10 of sales and uses $3 of inventory, so it has a $7 profit; of the $10, $1 was cash and $9 was on account.
- A business buys a $30,000 car and pays with $30,000 of its cash.
- A business buys a $30,000 vehicle using none of its cash, borrowing the money from the bank.
- A business buys $50 of inventory with $50 of cash.
- A business buys $100 of inventory, pays $30 cash and owes the vendor $70.

Translating words into numbers and placing them in the balance sheet is accounting in your head, done without debits, credits or adjusting journal entries, which are the mechanics of accounting, not its language.

**The conclusion**: No matter what the financial transaction is, it impacts the balance sheet. The piece of paper most owners spend the least time looking at is the **repository for every single thing that happens in a business.** Accountants convert activities into numbers. What an owner wants is a scoreboard that converts the numbers back into activities.

## 6. Cash Accounting vs Accrual Accounting

### The two systems

**What it is**:
- **Accrual accounting** says: **when you earn it, you owe it, or you use it, then you record it.** If you had 30 of sales and collected only 9 in cash, you still record the full 30 of revenue. Inventory is expensed when it is used, not when it is bought.
- **Cash accounting** says: if you had 30 of sales but collected only 9, your sales were 9. Revenue is the cash you received, and "cost of goods sold" is the cash you spent on inventory, whether or not you used it.

The egg business above was run on accrual accounting.

### Example: egg business week 2 both ways

**Accrual**:
- Revenue 33
- Cost of goods sold (18)
- Gross profit 15
- Expenses (8) (depreciation 5, bad debt 3)
- **Profit 7**

**Cash**:
- Revenue = cash collected from customers: 21 (18 + 3)
- Cost of goods sold = cash paid for inventory this period: (9) (not the 18 purchased or the 18 used)
- Gross profit 12
- No depreciation and no bad debt under cash accounting
- One dollar of expense for the cash repaid to Mamaw
- **Profit 11**

(A participant pointed out, and it was accepted, that in the pure cash view the dollar paid to Mamaw would not appear if Mamaw had been paid only in cash, but any cash payment to anyone else, such as fixing the wagon or a rent check, would count. Both readings are right.)

**How deadly cash accounting can be**: Change two assumptions. Suppose every sale that week was on account, so no cash came in: cash revenue is 0. Suppose the $18 of eggs had been paid to Mamaw in full in cash. Cash-basis revenue 0, cost of goods sold (18), gross profit (18), and net profit **-18** instead of +7 (or -19 if the one-dollar payment went to someone other than Mamaw). Same business, same activities, completely different picture.

### Why accrual, if cash is what matters most?

**The question**: If cash matters most, why not use cash accounting?

**The answer**: Accrual accounting gives you optics on both profitability and cash. Cash accounting only tells you about cash. You can have cash and no profits, or profits and no cash, so you need all three pieces of paper for a balanced view.
- **Accrual accounting has three papers**: an income statement, a balance sheet and a statement of cash flow.
- **Cash accounting has two papers**: a balance sheet that is inaccurate, because under cash accounting there is no such thing as inventory, accounts receivable or accounts payable; and what is effectively a statement of cash flow. Under cash accounting the cash you receive is called revenue, but for most businesses the cash received is not the same as revenue. **What cash accounting is missing is a real income statement.** Most people on cash accounting have never seen a real income statement; all they have seen is a statement of cash flow, which is important, but so is an income statement.

**Example**: In a week with $33 of revenue but only $18 of cash collected, suppose the business bought $18 of inventory but used only $10 of it. Under accrual accounting, profit is $23. Under cash accounting, gross profit is zero. The cash view is accurate for cash; the accrual view is the accurate reflection of profit, which is why accrual adds the income statement.

### Who can stay on cash accounting

Very few businesses: those with no accounts receivable, no accounts payable and no inventory, where a sale is collected immediately. Example: a significant real estate firm in New York that receives a commission and pays its bills more or less simultaneously; for it, the difference between cash and accrual is basically nothing. The only other business that fits, said half in jest, is a crack cocaine dealer: no AR, no inventory, no AP. For most businesses, which carry some receivables and some payables, the way to better optics is accrual.

### Tax vs management reporting

**Why owners end up on cash accounting**: In the United States (not necessarily in Australia), the government likes you to report taxes on a cash basis. Most small-business accountants are very familiar with cash accounting because that is how the taxes get filed, so many owners get their financial statements on a cash basis and end up with goofy optics.

**How to fix it**:
1. Put this in your VIPs: **check whether your management reports are on cash or accrual accounting.**
2. **Keep reporting to the government on cash accounting if you like, but get your management reports on accrual.** In ninety-eight percent of the businesses this has been applied to, the owner needs accrual management reports.
3. Migrate at **year end**. Nothing bad is likely to happen between now and year end if you stay on cash, but plan the move.
4. The cost is not great: you are already keeping track of who owes you money (AR), who you owe (AP) and, hopefully, your inventory (and when you do not track inventory, it bites you and gets expensive).

**Evidence**: There are sixty-three thousand publicly held companies in the world and nine thousand in the United States, all trading on stock exchanges. The number using cash accounting for management reports is **zero**. The largest and most successful companies on the planet use accrual.

**The QuickBooks trap**: QuickBooks has a button that switches between cash and accrual. It only works if the books were entered on accrual; then you can switch to cash. If the books were entered on cash, you cannot push the button and get back to accrual.

**Pitfall: who is hit hardest.** Cash accounting is particularly deadly for doctors, dentists and anyone with large receivables. On cash accounting with large receivables, you have no idea how you are doing month to month.

**When your accountant objects**: Expect to hear, "No, you don't need that. It's too expensive and too hard." That is usually a tax accountant who understands cash accounting and wants to keep doing the books that way. The response: **"Not counting today, how long have you worked for me?"**

**Size threshold**: Above a certain size, a business cannot use pure cash accounting for government reporting. A participant cited the threshold as about twenty-four and a half million dollars of gross receipts, "ten and a half million" was also floated, and the figure agreed on was about twenty-five million dollars (stated as "twenty-five million, I think"; unverified which figure holds in 2026). Above it you must move to a different method: not full accrual, but you must, for example, count inventory.

## 7. The Financial Rosetta Stone: Campus Products

### What the Rosetta Stone is

**What it is**: A single page that records every transaction as a row and every balance sheet account as a column. Rows used here: equity sale, bank loan, adjust interest (two rows), PP&E, depreciation, inventory purchase, sales, cost of goods sold, operating cost, loan repayment, income taxes. Columns: cash, accounts receivable, inventory, property plant and equipment, accounts payable, taxes payable, notes payable, shareholder investment, earnings.

**Why it works**: When you add up the columns, the totals across the bottom are the balance sheet. The earnings column, read down, is the income statement. The cash column, read down, is the statement of cash flow. One piece of paper holds all three report cards, which lets you read the numbers back into the activities that caused them. Ask an accountant whether a balance sheet, income statement and statement of cash flow can be put on one page and you will be told it cannot be done. It can.

### The sign rule

Do not think "I owe it, so it's negative." Ask one simple question of every column: **did this number go up or down?** If it went up, write a positive number; if it went down, a negative number. That applies to cash, receivables, inventory, taxes payable, notes payable, everything. Borrowing money increases notes payable, so it is a positive number in the notes payable column.

### The finding technique: where did that cash come from?

When cash goes up, walk the columns: did it come from accounts receivable? From liquidating inventory? From selling PP&E? Do I owe a vendor, or the government? Did somebody invest? Is it earnings? Eliminate columns until one is left.

### The Campus Products case

Campus Products was started on January 1, 2020, in a small Texas town. Its first three transactions took place in the first week; the rest summarize the first year. Required: record the transactions and prepare the balance sheet, income statement and statement of cash flows as of December 31, 2020.

**Transaction 1: Equity sale.** Sold 35,000 shares of capital stock at $1.00 per share = **$35,000**. Cash +35,000; shareholder investment +35,000.

**Transaction 2: Bank loan.** Borrowed $10,000 from a local bank; the note is due in 5 years and carries 5% interest, paid December 31 each year. **Interest = $500.**
- Bank loan row: cash +10,000; notes payable +10,000.
- Interest row: cash -500; earnings -500 (interest is an expense; expenses reduce earnings).

**Transaction 3: Equipment, with a second loan.** Bought $40,000 of PP&E, paid cash, financed by borrowing $20,000 from the bank (recorded separately). The loan is for 3 years at 10% interest only, paid annually December 31. The assets have an expected life of 5 years, no salvage value, straight-line depreciation for financial reporting. **Interest = $2,000. Depreciation = $8,000.** Four parts:
- Bank loan row: cash +20,000; notes payable +20,000.
- PP&E row: cash -40,000; PP&E +40,000 (one asset traded for another).
- Interest row: cash -2,000; earnings -2,000.
- Depreciation row: PP&E -8,000 (the value is reduced, so it is negative); earnings -8,000.

**Transaction 4: Inventory (FIFO cost flow).**

4.1 Purchases (all on account; by year end all orders fully paid):
- 100 units at $10 = $1,000
- 100 units at $20 = $2,000
- 200 units at $30 = $6,000
- 400 units = **$9,000**
- Inventory purchase row: cash -9,000; inventory +9,000. No earnings impact: it has not been used yet.

4.2 Sales (all on account; by year end, collections received only for the first 100 units sold):
- 100 units at $150 = $15,000 (collected: cash)
- 170 units at $200 = $34,000 (accounts receivable)
- 270 units = **$49,000**
- Sales row: cash +15,000; accounts receivable +34,000; earnings +49,000.

4.3 Inventory sold (first in, first out) = [100 x $10] + [100 x $20] + [70 x $30] = $1,000 + $2,000 + $2,100 = **$5,100**.
- Cost of goods sold row: inventory -5,100; earnings -5,100.

**Transaction 5: Operating expenses.** $20,000 incurred, $17,000 paid. **A/P = $3,000.** Mentally draw the box: operating cost row: cash -17,000; accounts payable +3,000 (it went up, so positive); earnings -20,000 (the full expense).

**Transaction 6: Loan repayment.** On December 31, repay $7,500 of the $10,000 loan. Cash -7,500; notes payable -7,500.

**Transaction 7: Income tax.** Tax for the year is $3,000, to be paid March 31 of next year. Taxes are an expense, so earnings -3,000. No cash was used; the business gave the government an IOU, so taxes payable +3,000. Liabilities up, equity down.

### Tagging O, I and F on the Rosetta Stone

The only thing the Rosetta Stone does not show on its own is whether each cash line is operating, investing or financing. Tag them:
- Equity sale: **F** (money raised from investors, not earned and not from selling assets).
- Both bank loans: **F**.
- PP&E purchase: **I** (investing in assets).
- Inventory purchase: **O**.
- Sales: **O**.
- Cost of goods sold: no cash line (the cash was spent when the inventory was bought).
- Operating cost: **O**.
- Interest: **O**. It is tempting to call interest financing, but think of interest as rent: you are renting someone's money, so it is operating cash.
- Loan repayment: **F**.
- Income taxes: **O** (none paid this year).

Totals: **O cash -13,500. I cash -40,000. F cash +57,500.** Starting from zero, cash flow is +4,000 and ending cash is 4,000.

### Reading numbers back into words

This is the reason for the exercise. Ignore everything else and read only the Rosetta Stone:
- Line 1: cash up 35,000. Where did it come from? Shareholder investment up 35,000. Somebody invested $35,000 in the company.
- Line 2: cash up 10,000. Where from? Notes payable up 10,000. The company borrowed $10,000 and deposited it.
- The PP&E line: cash down 40,000. Where did it go? PP&E up 40,000. The company traded $40,000 of cash for equipment: one asset for another.

That is converting numbers back into words, possible because the balance sheet, income statement and statement of cash flow are on one page. It is the first step. The goal is to reverse engineer statements even without a Rosetta Stone, well enough to look at a disguised Fortune 100 company's statements and say whether you like it, and why. If you do not like your own numbers, the Rosetta Stone is a starting point for figuring out what is going on.

## 8. Profits vs Operating Cash: The Three Balance Sheet Rules and the Blueprint

### Reading Campus Products' first year

At the end of year one, operating cash is -13,500, investing cash is -40,000 and financing cash is +57,500, for cash flow (and ending cash, since the company started at zero) of +4,000.

Apply the sequence from section 4. The only reason to want a wagon (assets) is to increase revenue; the reason to want revenue is profits; the reason to want profits is to convert them into operating cash. Campus Products had:
- **Assets of $73,900**
- that produced **revenue of $49,000**
- that produced **profits of $10,400**
- which the business converted into a real, out-of-the-bank **operating cash loss of $13,500**.

If the person who had the entrepreneurial seizure to start this business had had a V8 instead and simply not gone to work all year, they would have had $13,500 more cash in the bank. But they had to go to work, and turned $10,400 of theoretical profit into a $13,500 operating cash loss. The Rosetta Stone shows how.

### Rule 1: When inventory goes up, that's bad for cash

The first operating cash line is the inventory purchase: $9,000 of cash used, but only $5,100 of inventory used by year end, leaving $3,900 of inventory on hand. Had the company bought only the $5,100 it used, it would have spent only $5,100 of cash and had zero inventory left. That $3,900 of extra inventory cost $3,900 of operating cash.

### Rule 2: When accounts receivable go up, that's bad for cash

Only $15,000 of cash came in on $49,000 of revenue; accounts receivable ended at $34,000. With zero receivables, cash would be $34,000 higher. Receivables are consuming cash.

### Rule 3: When accounts payable go up, that's good for cash

Operating costs were $20,000, of which $17,000 was paid in cash and $3,000 was an IOU. If every operating cost had been an IOU (not realistic; it only makes the point), no cash would have been used and cash would be $17,000 higher. Higher payables mean less operating cash used.

### The primary difference between profits and operating cash

**What it is**: **The primary difference between profits and operating cash flow is three things on the balance sheet: accounts receivable, inventory and accounts payable.** The one other item is depreciation, which reduces profit without using cash. So the full statement is: the only difference between profits and operating cash flow is depreciation plus AR, AP and inventory.

**Why it matters**: Campus Products' cash flow was destroyed by inventory and accounts receivable, and slightly more accounts payable would have helped. It does not take a lot: buy a little less inventory or stop paying cash for all of it, collect receivables a little better, manage payables slightly better, and the result is very different from $10,400 of profit and -$13,500 of operating cash.

**How to improve operating cash**:
1. **Off the income statement**: raise profits by increasing revenue while holding expenses flat, or by holding revenue and reducing expenses.
2. **Off the balance sheet**: manage receivable days, inventory days and payable days.

Higher profits help operating cash only if AR, inventory and AP are not moving the wrong direction. If you do not get a statement of cash flow, you have to watch the balance sheet to see what is happening between profit and operating cash. If you do get one, the accountants have done it for you. Managing both the income statement and the balance sheet gives you the chance not only to make more profit, but in many cases to make more cash.

### The blueprint (the one-page summary)

**What it is**: One page that captures several hours of teaching; worth starring in your VIPs.

**How it works**:
1. **Assets into sales.** How many assets does it take to produce a dollar of revenue? Assets can be defined three ways:
   - dollars on the balance sheet;
   - number of employees;
   - payroll dollars.
   An asset-light business, with little physical stuff on its balance sheet but a lot of people (perhaps part-time or subcontract), cannot use balance sheet dollars and may not be able to use headcount, but can use payroll dollars: how many payroll dollars does it take to produce a dollar of revenue? With that metric you can see how well you convert assets into sales.
2. **Sales into profits.** The difference between sales and profits is expenses.
3. **Profits into operating cash flow.** The difference is depreciation and three things on the balance sheet: AR, AP and inventory.

The specific tactics for each step (driving operating cash off the income statement or off the balance sheet) are covered at length in the book *The Ultimate Blueprint for an Insanely Successful Business*, which is designed as an owner's manual for business and financial optics. Some owners will want the tactics; others will say, "I'm good. I'm happy destroying capital."

### The tool

Activities into numbers is the accounting part. Numbers back into activities is where optics come from. The Rosetta Stone is step one. A scoreboard tool (CFO Scoreboard is one such software) takes the balance sheet and income statement you already get and puts them in a format that shows what is actually happening, so you can reverse engineer the activities.

## 9. Case Study: Enron, Profit Is a Theory and Cash Is a Fact

### The income statement story

A real, publicly held company reported:
- **1999**: theoretical profits of **$827 million**.
- **2000**: theoretical profits of **$896 million**.
- **Q1 2001**: **$405 million**, about forty-five percent of the entire prior year in one quarter.
- **First six months of 2001**: almost **$800 million** (**$788 million**), compared with about $900 million for all of the previous year.

Earnings per share told the same story. These people were "tearing it up."

### The cash balance story

Ending cash from the balance sheet:
- End of 1999: **$288 million**.
- End of 2000: **$1.3 billion**.
- End of Q1 2001: **$1.1 billion**.
- End of the first six months of 2001: about **$850 million**, still roughly three times the (about $300 million) cash on the balance sheet eighteen months earlier.

The stock was **$61 a share**.

### What the smartest people on Wall Street said

- **Lehman Brothers**, March 12, 2001: strong buy.
- **Merrill Lynch**, March 21: near-term buy (good in the short term; no view on the long term).
- **Goldman Sachs**: recommended at **$55**.
- **Bear Stearns** (no longer in existence): attractive at **$51**.
- **JP Morgan**: buy.
- **AG Edwards**: accumulate.
- **Bank of America**: strong buy.
- **Goldman Sachs**: if you liked it at $61, you will love it at **$40**; recommend.
- **Bear Stearns**: still good looking; buy.
- **Goldman Sachs**: recommends it again at **$33**. (Apparently the more money you lose, the more Goldman likes it.)
- **AG Edwards**: buy. **First Albany**: strong buy.
- **Merrill Lynch**: now a long-term buy.
- **CIBC**: buy. Long-term buy, strong buy. **First Albany**: buy.
- **Bank of America**, at **$16**: market perform. ("Market perform" means: we don't have a clue. If the market goes up, this goes up; if it goes down, this goes down.)
- **Smith Barney**, October 2001: buy.
- **Merrill Lynch**: neutral. ("Neutral" means: if you don't own it, for goodness' sake don't buy it; if you do own it, for goodness' sake don't sell it. You have already lost about seventy percent of your investment; don't change horses.)
- **CIBC**: you need to own it at **$11.99**.
- **Prudential**, at **$9.24**: hold. ("Hold" means it's always darkest before the dawn; markets move in cycles and this is bound to come back.) Maintain your position: another way of saying neutral, or "don't do anything stupid."
- **Goldman Sachs**, November 2001, at **$5.01**: market perform.
- **CIBC, UBS, Credit Suisse**: hold.
- **November 2001: $0.36 a share.** From March to November 2001, about **ninety-nine percent** of the market value was destroyed, and the advice was still "don't sell now."
- **December 2, 2001: zero.** Enron filed for bankruptcy.

Some of the smartest people on the planet (not all of them) forgot that **profit is a theory and cash is a fact.**

### What the third piece of paper showed

Every publicly held company gives shareholders all three papers, every quarter: a balance sheet (the world atlas), an income statement (the street map to earnings) and a statement of cash flow (the street map to cash, broken into O, I and F). In the figures below, a positive number is good for cash and a negative number is bad for cash.

**O cash**:
- **1999**: a great job of converting profits into operating cash.
- **2000**: "hit it out of the park": $896 million of theoretical profits converted into billions of operating cash flow (the figure is given once as $1.8 billion and once as $4.8 billion; unverified which is correct). How? Look at what AR, inventory and AP did. The only difference between profits and operating cash is those three things on the balance sheet: AR down is good for cash, AR up is bad for cash. Mystery solved.
- **Q1 2001**: **$405 million** of theoretical profits and operating cash flow of **negative $464 million**. Had Enron's employees stayed home for ninety days, the business would have had an incremental $405 million of cash in the bank, as stated. Instead they went to work and destroyed capital in ninety days.
- **First six months of 2001**: profits of **$788 million**; operating cash flow of **negative $1.3 billion**. They destroyed $1.3 billion. It was on the piece of paper; all you had to do was pull out the third piece of paper. What it was saying, in June, was **"Run."** Wall Street was saying "good looking."

**I cash** (negative = used cash to buy PP&E; positive = sold PP&E):
- **1999**: used **$3.5 billion** of cash to buy more stuff.
- **2000**: sold an enormous amount of property, plant and equipment. That cash came from selling assets, not from operations.
- **Q1 2001**: sold more.
- **First six months of 2001**: used about **$1.1 billion** to buy PP&E.

**F cash** (positive = raised money from financiers; negative = repaid debt or distributed money to financiers): raise money, raise money, raise money, raise money. **The only reason Enron was still in business was that somebody was very good at raising money.**

### The human cost

An Oregon newspaper article at the end of 2001: as 2001 started, **Tim Ramsey** felt like a million bucks. A **fifty-five-year-old** electrical worker, he was ready to take early retirement after **thirty-three years with Portland General Electric**. His retirement nest egg, fed by more than thirty years of steady savings, had grown into a golden goose worth more than **$1.25 million**. As 2001 ended, it had collapsed to **less than $10,000**, and his retirement plans were nothing more than wishful thinking. "I was a big sucker," he said. "For some reason, I don't know why I listened to those people. I should have my butt kicked."

He did not know there were three pieces of paper: three major dials in the cockpit.

### The lessons

- Your accountants supply all three papers, but if they are on cash accounting, your optics are fuzzy.
- If you get the accounting and do not look at all three papers, you are choosing to fly at a hundred and eighty miles an hour without seeing where you are going. It is only a matter of time before you and a mountain say hello. You cannot keep flying by the seat of your pants and expect financial success, let alone avoid catastrophe.
- **What you can't see can kill you.** Like a blood test, the statements show what is going on under the hood when everything looks fine from the outside.
- The going-out-of-business sign is not the result of a lack of revenue; it is the result of something going wrong with cash. **You only go broke when you run out of cash.** Yet most owners are slurping the Kool-Aid of profits, getting most excited about the income statement. The income statement has very useful information, but if all you see is profit, without understanding how you are generating operating cash, it may be only a matter of time before you see a sign you do not like.

## 10. Reporting Cadence, Report Cards vs Critical Drivers

### How often to get the three papers

**What it is**: Get the income statement, balance sheet and statement of cash flow **at the end of every month**, all three at the same time. Watch the bank account daily or weekly if you like, but in the normal financial reporting world the statements happen once a month. Trying to produce them more often generally causes wonkiness in the numbers.

### Report cards are an autopsy

**What it is**: Income statements and balance sheets are key performance indicators (KPIs). They are an **autopsy**: you are looking at what happened after the patient has already died. You need those optics, but to manage the business into your business you need something **predictive** about what is about to happen to your financial statements. That means monitoring **critical drivers**, and it requires a different tool (an operating dashboard) to manage between month ends.

**Example: the bathroom scale prescription.** If you weigh five pounds too much, a doctor gives you a prescription, a recipe: cut your caloric intake by this much and exercise by that much. Those are the critical drivers of your weight. If you execute the recipe consistently, you can throw away the bathroom scale; the only reason anyone has to step on the scale is that they did not execute consistently on the diet and exercise. The same is true in business. Looking at a balance sheet is stepping on the scale: it tells you that you had one too many Krispy Kreme donuts. The question is what to do about it. Measure the report cards monthly, and manage the critical drivers in between.

### Where this leaves you

You started with "things and stuff" and can now do accounting in your head by picturing a box. The next step is to use that base to reverse engineer statements without a Rosetta Stone, make better decisions and make more money. There is so much needless pain in business owners' relationship with financial information; the purpose of simplifying it onto one page is optics, so that nobody has to play pin the tail on the donkey or whack-a-mole with their business.
