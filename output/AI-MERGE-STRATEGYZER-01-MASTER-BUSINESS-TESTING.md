## 1. Foundations: Why Business Ideas Need Testing

### The Four Ways Business Models Fail

**What it is.** Business models fail for many reasons, but there are four big ones to avoid: solving an irrelevant customer job, a flawed business model design, external threats from the business model environment, and poor execution.

**Why it matters.** Each failure type sits in a different part of the business, which is why testing has to cover the value proposition, the business model, the environment and the team, not only the product.

**Type 1: The value proposition helps customers with a job they don't care about (or don't care about enough).** It sounds trivial, but entrepreneurs and executives routinely get deeply excited about a value proposition that is removed from the customer's reality. Illustrations: the Segway (a personal transportation device), the Apple Newton (an early PDA), and FloTV (a portable device by Qualcomm that came with a mobile TV service). All three were very expensive flops, and one cost over a billion dollars.

**Type 2: The business model design is flawed.** Your model can fail even with a great value proposition that addresses a relevant job. You are guaranteed to fail, for example, when your customer acquisition costs (CAC) are higher than what you can earn from customers directly or indirectly over time (their customer lifetime value). Real case: Kodak earned the lion's share of its income from high margin camera film and employed 145,000 workers at its peak. Despite helping invent and commercialize the first digital camera back in 1975, it failed to design a business model for digital cameras that would compensate for evaporating film revenues. Its new digital business model was fundamentally flawed, and the company went bankrupt.

**Type 3: External threats from the business model environment.** Even with a great value proposition that solves a relevant job and a sound business model, you can still fail. Competitors might offer a similar value proposition at a substantially lower price because their business model has different economics. Customers might like your value proposition but think you are not environmentally friendly enough. New technology might undermine your model, or a stock market crash might dry up your funding.

**Type 4: Poor execution.** Even with all fundamentals in place, you can ruin the best business model if you cannot build the required infrastructure, assemble the wrong team with the wrong skills, or lack the leadership to turn the concept into reality.

**How to check yourself.** Ask these questions:

1. Are you helping customers do a job they care strongly about?
2. Do you have the right business model that is profitable and scalable?
3. Is today's environment the right context for your business model?
4. Is your business model designed to excel in that given context?
5. Does your team have the right skills to execute your business model and make it reality?

### Not Every Idea Carries the Same Risk: Search vs. Execution

**What it is.** New ideas sit at different points on a curve that runs from high uncertainty to high certainty. The left side is **search** (high risk, then medium risk); the right side is **execution** (low risk). Uncertainty falls from left to right while spending rises. It doesn't matter whether you are a startup or an established company: a new business idea puts you on the explore side of the continuum, which is high risk.

**Examples of placing ideas on the curve:**

- **High risk: Travis Kalanick's new startup.** A startup is high risk even if helmed by an experienced CEO. Kalanick operates with a lot of uncertainty because it is a new type of food-delivery service, which is a new value proposition.
- **Low risk: Apple's move to India.** Efficiency improvements are low risk because the product and the market remain the same, so they operate in a known environment. The company Apple uses for iPhone production also remains the same (Foxconn), which lowers the risk of failure further. However, efficiency improvements might bring up operational costs or other types of risks, which still have to be tested.
- **Medium risk: Disney's new streaming service.** Disney operates in a known environment (its current market) but is launching something new. It likely understands its market quite well, but still needs to run experiments and collect evidence to prove the new product is feasible (can they build it?) and viable (does it make more money than it costs to create?).

**Why it works.** Testing exists to move an idea from high uncertainty to high certainty before spending climbs. The more uncertain the idea, the more testing it needs before large investments.

## 2. The Testing Process at a Glance

**What it is.** Testing follows the design phase, in which you shaped your ideas with the Business Model Canvas and the Value Proposition Canvas. The process runs through five steps:

1. **Hypotheses.** Extract hypotheses to make the risks underlying your business idea explicit, then prioritize them to find the most critical risk.
2. **Experiments.** Test the most critical hypothesis with an experiment.
3. **Evidence.** The experiment produces evidence that either supports the hypothesis (it is valid) or refutes it (it is invalid).
4. **Insights.** Study all evidence produced by your experiments and learn from it, gaining insights so you can take more informed decisions.
5. **Actions.** Act on the insights: keep testing (pick the next hypothesis and next experiments) or go back to the drawing board and reshape your ideas because your initial hypotheses were not right.

**Why it works.** Each step is tightly linked. A good experiment has a strong connection between the hypothesis you started with and the evidence you collect, and a good action is one clearly connected to that evidence. The whole chain starts and ends with the quality of your hypothesis.

**Pitfalls.** The faster you test, the better. Don't get stuck in analysis paralysis. And remember that the goal is not learning for its own sake: the goal is to take action and move from idea to validated business case, or even better, towards a real business.

## 3. Hypotheses: Extract, Prioritize, Refine

### What a Hypothesis Is

**What it is.** A hypothesis is an assumption that your value proposition, business model or, more generally, your strategy builds on. It is what you need to learn to understand whether your business idea might work or not, and it relates to the desirability, feasibility, viability or adaptability of the idea. Put generally: a hypothesis is everything that would have to be true for your idea to work.

**Why it matters.** Understanding your hypotheses is how you go from high uncertainty to high certainty. You shape your idea, make the hypotheses explicit, and then test them. Your hypotheses must cover all the uncertainties of your business idea, not just product-related ones, because for you to succeed you need to test them all. That is how you reduce risk and uncertainty.

**Where hypotheses come from:**

- **The value proposition, customer side.** Example: "We believe that executives are struggling with good meeting planning."
- **The value proposition, solution side.** Example: "We believe that our technology can help increase the quality of meetings."
- **The business model.** Example: "We believe that executives are willing to pay a subscription fee of $20."
- **The business environment.** Hypotheses related to competitors, changes in the regulatory environment, or the infrastructure underlying your business idea.

### The Four Risk Categories: Desirability, Feasibility, Viability, Adaptability

**What it is.** The idea comes from IDEO, which tests product designs for desirability, feasibility and viability. Overlaid on the Business Model Canvas, you get something very similar, plus a fourth category for the environment:

- **Desirability: Do customers want it?** The front stage of the business model and the value proposition (customer segments, value proposition, channels, customer relationships, customer profile).
- **Feasibility: Can we deliver it?** The back stage of the business model (key resources, key activities, key partners).
- **Viability: What's it worth?** Can we capture more value than it costs us to produce that value? Can we make more money than we spend? (revenue streams, cost structure).
- **Adaptability: Is the timing right?** Aspects of the business model environment: competitors, market forces, trends, regulation, macroeconomics.

**Pitfall: the product myth.** Many think all you need to do is figure out the product and everything else will fall into place. If you only ask "can we build it?", you only pay attention to the backstage of the business model and feasibility. The purpose of extracting hypotheses is to find all underlying assumptions that will make the idea successful: if we build it, how do we sell it? Will people buy it? Is the market timing right? You need hypotheses for all four risk categories.

### Step 1: Extract Hypotheses

**What it is.** Making every assumption behind your business model and value proposition explicit, by asking "What would have to be true for our idea to work?" for each of the four risk categories.

**Why it works.** The first step to start derisking a business idea is making its hypotheses explicit. Ideas that rely on a new business model and a new value proposition for a new audience operate from a lot of unknowns, and you cannot test what you have not written down.

**How to do it:**

1. Create a business prototype of your idea with the Business Model Canvas and the Value Proposition Canvas.
2. Ask "What needs to be true for our idea to work?" for each risk category, starting with desirability: what are the underlying assumptions about your value proposition, channels, customer relationships and customer profile?
3. Write each hypothesis on its own sticky note and place it around the Business Model Canvas and Value Proposition Canvas, next to the building block it relates to.
4. Repeat for feasibility, viability and adaptability.
5. Step back after the first round and check: do you have hypotheses for each of the four risk categories? There is no right number of hypotheses, but every category must be covered.

**Best practices:**

- Use a different sticky note color for desirability, feasibility, viability and adaptability hypotheses.
- Begin every hypothesis with "We believe that..."
- One hypothesis per sticky note. Don't use bullet points; single notes make prioritizing easier.
- Keep hypotheses short and precise. No blah blah blah.
- Discuss and agree as a team when writing.

**What you need (about 40 minutes for the full extract, prioritize and refine exercise):** your business idea; a Business Model Canvas and Value Proposition Canvas; sticky notes in 4 colors; a big white space or wall; a flip chart and markers. Alternatively, work in a virtual real-time project in the Strategyzer App. To learn more about business design before this step, read *Business Model Generation* and *Value Proposition Design* or use the free online material.

**Example (LifeTree Bank).** Desirability: "We believe that Generation Z prefers efficiency and mobility over traditional banking experiences" and "We believe that automations will increase our conversion rates." Feasibility: "We believe that schools and coworking spaces will be interested in partnering with us." Viability: "We believe that we can save $500 million over three years by switching from retail branches to pop-up stores." After stepping back, they noticed a gap and added an adaptability hypothesis: "We believe that our idea complies with banking regulations in all countries the bank operates in."

### Step 2: Prioritize Hypotheses

**What it is.** Ranking hypotheses by how much risk they represent for the success of your idea, using a 2x2 matrix of evidence against criticality.

**Why it works.** You can't test everything at once, so you test the most important hypotheses first. The goal is to find a business model that works, so you focus on the things most likely to kill your idea. If a fundamental hypothesis turns out to be wrong, everything else doesn't matter.

**The two axes:**

- **Horizontal: evidence vs. no evidence.** What evidence do you already have about this hypothesis from existing business data, research or experience? Place a hypothesis on the left if you can produce relevant, observable and recent evidence that supports it (for example, you have experience working with a technological procedure). Place it on the right if you have no evidence and are operating on pure assumption (for example, your idea targets a customer segment you never dealt with before). Beware: we often believe we know our customers based on our own beliefs, but in reality we often just assume.
- **Vertical: critical vs. non-critical.** Is this hypothesis critical for your business model and value proposition to succeed? At the top go hypotheses that would kill your idea if wrong (for example, your idea relies on a specific customer behavior; ideas that didn't fulfill a customer need are business killers). If that hypothesis is proven wrong, the idea fails and all other hypotheses become irrelevant. At the bottom go less critical ones, which are not the first things you'd go out and test (for example, partnering with an existing vendor: you can likely find another partner).

**How to do it:**

1. Draw a large 2x2 on a flip chart.
2. Start with the horizontal evidence line. Take each hypothesis from all four risk categories and place it on the line: some evidence to the left, no evidence to the right.
3. Without changing the relative positions on the horizontal line, move each hypothesis up or down according to how critical it is for the survival of the idea.
4. Focus on the top right quadrant: the most critical hypotheses with the least evidence. These are your riskiest hypotheses and define your near-term experimentation.

**Example (LifeTree Bank).** Four hypotheses: "Generation Z will spend more time in temporary pop-up stores than retail branches"; "We can build automations with our existing systems"; "Generation Z prefers efficiency and mobility over the traditional banking experience"; "We can partner with our existing software vendors." Because LifeTree Bank is building a new business model for a new customer segment, desirability is high risk; because the idea builds on existing infrastructure, feasibility is lower risk. The top right quadrant held a couple of desirability hypotheses, plus an adaptability hypothesis right at the top: even if customers want it, the lifestyle bank may not comply with banking regulations around the world.

**Should desirability always come first?** If you are operating with a new customer segment and don't understand their needs, it is often advisable to start with desirability. But other categories can be equally important, as the regulation example shows. It is also possible that you already have evidence on desirability: had LifeTree Bank already served Generation Z, it would understand their behaviors, which would reduce that risk.

**Parallel testing.** You shouldn't test everything at once, but you can test more than one type of hypothesis in parallel. LifeTree Bank can test desirability and adaptability at the same time because both are very risky for the idea, especially at the beginning of the journey when risk is high and the team wants to move quickly with cheap experiments.

### Step 3: Refine Hypotheses (Testable, Precise, Discrete)

**What it is.** Before designing experiments, put on your scientist's glasses and reshape each priority hypothesis so it is testable, precise and discrete. This creates a clear connection between the hypothesis and the results your experiments produce.

**Why it works.** Different people understand different things by vague terms. A well-formed hypothesis makes sure everybody on the team tests exactly the same thing, so you get better data and learn together about exactly the same thing. Don't make the mistake of starting with badly shaped hypotheses: they give you a bad start to the entire testing process.

#### Testable

**Definition.** A hypothesis is testable when it can be supported (validated, shown true) or refuted (invalidated, shown false) based on evidence and guided by experience. In science this is called falsifiable; business doesn't need to be as strict because you just want to learn about the underlying hypothesis.

**Example 1.** Weak: "Millennials prefer companies with a purpose." It is unclear what "prefer" and "purpose" mean. Better: "Millennials prefer working for companies with an explicit purpose statement on the job offer." It may seem cumbersome, but it makes everyone precise about what they want to test.

**Example 2 (LifeTree Bank).** Weak: "We believe that Generation Z prefers pop-up stores over branches." Unclear what preference means, how Generation Z is defined, what a pop-up store is and where it is. Better: "We believe that young adults between 18 and 24 will spend more time in temporary pop-up stores that are placed in coworking spaces compared to traditional branches."

#### Precise

**Definition.** A hypothesis is precise when you know what success looks like. Ideally it describes the precise what, who and when of your assumption.

**Example 1.** Weak: "Partygoers spend a lot of money on the weekend." Who are partygoers, when does the weekend start, and what is a lot of money? Better: "Adults between 18 and 32 who go out at least once a week for drinks and dancing spend more than $400 on Friday and Saturday evenings."

**Example 2 (LifeTree Bank).** Weak: "We believe that young adults don't plan for their future." We don't know what they would save for, the age range, or what "future" means. Better: "We believe that the majority of young adults between 18 and 24 don't save more than $100 per month for their retirement."

#### Discrete

**Definition.** A hypothesis is discrete when it describes only one distinct, testable and precise thing you want to investigate.

**Example 1.** Weak: "We can produce widget X at a lower cost than today and make it look better than our competitors' solutions." This mixes things one experiment probably couldn't test together. Split it: "We are able to produce widget X at less than $2" and "Our current customer base will pick our design over that of our direct competitors." Maybe one experiment tests both, but you now know these are two different things, and very likely you will need two experiments.

**Example 2 (LifeTree Bank).** Weak: "Our digital platform helps us increase conversion rates and save money in call centers." Split it: "We believe that our digital platform will help us increase conversion rates by 25%" (a desirability hypothesis, because it addresses channels) and "Our digital platform will help us save $200 million in call centers over three years" (which remains a viability hypothesis). You might use one experiment for both, but based on their nature you likely need two different experiments.

### Practice: Refine the iPod Hypothesis

**Setup.** Imagine you are on Apple's innovation team in 1999, working on the new iPod and iTunes. Refine this hypothesis into a testable, precise and discrete one: "We believe that people will pay to download music which they have been getting for free."

**Why the original fails.** Hidden inside it are assumptions about why consumers would pay for something they used to get for free. You need to identify these jobs, pains and gains (desirability) and make them discrete to understand consumer behavior. If the original hypothesis were invalidated, the insight would be inconclusive: you would not know why. Because it's too expensive? Because consumers don't care about low quality? Because you tackled the wrong audience? Because consumers prefer free music over an easy-to-use service?

**Sample solution (one of many valid answers):**

- Desirability (customer profile): "We believe that consumers (20-40 yrs) will indicate a preference for good quality music files over free music."
- Desirability (customer profile): "We believe that consumers (20-40 yrs) will indicate a preference for easy-to-use service over free music."
- Viability (revenue streams): "We believe that consumers (20-40 yrs) will pay at least $1 per downloadable music file."

### Practice: Extract Hypotheses From the Owlet Case (15 minutes)

**Directions.** Watch Owlet's first-iteration story. Sketch the first business model on a Business Model Canvas. On Value Proposition Canvases, sketch the two customer segments they identified (nurses and hospital administrators) and how they intended to create value for each. Extract one hypothesis per customer segment.

**Debrief.** Nurses monitor patients with pulse oximetry. The device is attached to a cord, which is a pain for both patients and nurses: it tangles and restricts movement. Owlet's idea was a wireless pulse oximetry monitor worn on the ankle to remove the cord pain; it also addressed the gain of setting up and getting a patient's results in the shortest time. Value map: pulse oximetry monitor (product), wireless (pain reliever), ankle-worn tracking (gain creator). Customer profile (nurses): job "monitoring patients", pain "cord between device and monitor", gain "shortest time".

- Nurse hypothesis: "We believe that nurses find wireless pulse oximetry more convenient than traditional pulse oximetry." Validated: interviews with 58 nurses, 93% said the idea was better than the original technology.
- But nurses don't purchase the product. It would be distributed through hospitals, so the buyer is the hospital administrator, and devices would be sold by Owlet's sales force. Owlet assumed the same hypothesis for administrators ("hospital administrators find wireless pulse oximetry more convenient..."). Administrators have completely different jobs (buying materials, managing budgets), pains (cost) and gains: they want to run hospitals cost efficiently. The cord and saving time aren't in their interest; cost saving is. Ankle-worn tracking and a wireless device don't save them money, so their pain remains unaddressed and there is no fit. The hypothesis was invalid.

**Lesson: your user is not always your customer.** A user who loves the idea but has no purchasing power is not your customer. The interviews cost nothing and took one week. Had Owlet built a prototype right away, it could easily have spent thousands of dollars. Choosing a cheap and quick experiment allowed a quick pivot without spending money.

## 4. Experiments: Design With the Test Card

### What an Experiment Is

**What it is.** An experiment is a procedure that reduces the risk and uncertainty of a business idea. It produces weak or strong evidence that supports or refutes a hypothesis, and it can be fast or slow, and cheap or expensive, to conduct.

**Two families of experiments.**

- **Experiments where you listen to what people say.** Simple interviews and field research; more sophisticated interviews using storyboards and card sorting so people reveal their priorities; even more sophisticated interviews where you show a spec sheet or brochure of your products and services.
- **Experiments where you get people to do something.** Quickly set up a website and get people to sign up with their email or even make a purchase. In business to business this is harder, so you can ask people to sign a piece of paper that may not be legally binding but gives them skin in the game. Or produce a technological prototype, a so-called minimum viable product (MVP), and get people to interact with it.

**Example: the medical device manufacturer.** Hypothesis: "I believe that surgeons prefer our new medical device over older technology." Test: get surgeons to sign a recommendation letter to their hospital administrators. Metric: the percentage of surgeons who sign. This goes beyond hearing "yes, I'm interested": signers have skin in the game. Threshold: the percentage of surgeons who sign is as high as the percentage of surgeons who already purchased an older device from us.

### The Hypothesis, Experiment, Evidence Relationship

**What it is.** It all starts with your highest-risk hypothesis. You define how to test it in the form of an experiment. After running the experiment, there are four possible outcomes:

1. The evidence validates your hypothesis.
2. The evidence invalidates it.
3. The evidence is inconclusive and you need to keep testing.
4. You learn something new.

**Why it matters.** A good experiment has a strong connection between the hypothesis you started with and the evidence you collected, so the evidence is a clear indicator of the hypothesis's status. The experiment is the binding element in this relationship, and getting it right starts with understanding its four components.

### The Four Components of an Experiment (The Test Card)

**What it is.** The Test Card combines all components of an experiment in one tool so teams design experiments systematically:

1. **Step 1, Hypothesis: "We believe that..."** What would have to be true for your idea to work.
2. **Step 2, Test: "To verify that, we will..."** How you will test the hypothesis.
3. **Step 3, Metric: "And measure..."** What exactly you will measure.
4. **Step 4, Criteria: "We are right if..."** The target threshold that tells you whether the hypothesis is supported or refuted.

Card metadata: Test Name, Assigned to, Deadline, Duration, plus three rating scales: **Critical** (how critical the hypothesis is, from your prioritization), **Test Cost**, **Data Reliability** (strength of evidence) and **Time Required**.

**How to fill it in:**

1. Give the card a good, memorable name. You will likely run many of these, so naming matters, and assign it to a team member.
2. Add the hypothesis and circle how critical it is based on your prioritization exercise.
3. Describe the experiment, circle its cost, and circle how reliable its data will be. Interviews, for instance, produce relatively weak data because people share a lot of opinions, which is weaker than observing what customers actually do.
4. Add the metric and circle the time required to run the experiment.
5. Add the criteria: the threshold that tells you if the hypothesis is true.

**Example (LifeTree Bank).** Original hypothesis: "Generation Z prefers efficiency and mobility over traditional retail banking." Refined: Generation Z becomes "young adults between 18 and 24"; efficiency and mobility becomes "access to banking anywhere and anytime." Test: because this is an early-stage desirability experiment, conduct interviews with 50 potential Generation Z customers to better understand their jobs, pains and gains. Metric: how many people prefer mobile banking over traditional retail banking. Criteria: the team knows it can only be successful if at least 60% indicate a preference. Ratings: critical high; cost low; data reliability weak; time required medium. Result: 72% of young adults interviewed preferred mobile banking over traditional retail banking. That is clear evidence for a focused, testable and discrete hypothesis. Whether you can confidently validate or invalidate a hypothesis depends on how well you design these four components.

### Running Experiments: Stages and How Many

**Stages.** Beginners are often happy with one or two experiments, but that is not enough. Because you have several hypotheses, plan several experiments. Then:

1. **Plan:** take the most important hypotheses and experiments and plan the rollout.
2. **Design and build** your first experiments.
3. **Run and measure.** While these run, you can already design and build others.
4. **Analyze and learn** whether you can support or refute the hypothesis.

Several experiments can run at the same time.

**How many experiments, and how long?** It depends on speed and urgency. If you are not in crisis mode, you can test well and conduct many experiments, reducing uncertainty to a maximum. In crisis mode (running out of money, the company might go bankrupt) you accept that you can only run a few experiments and won't reduce uncertainty as much, but it's better than nothing.

**Characteristics.** Experiments are cheap or expensive, fast or slow, and produce weak or strong evidence. Should you only run cheap, fast ones? No. What matters most is running the right experiments at the right time:

- When uncertainty is at its maximum, run very fast experiments.
- The more you reduce uncertainty, the more time you can take, the stronger your evidence should become, and the more you should focus on action.
- Only build technological prototypes later on, because they require a lot of money and you can waste a lot of it.
- Rule of thumb: start with low-fidelity prototypes and move to high-fidelity prototypes later. Sometimes you don't even need a prototype: just start talking to customers and stakeholders.

### Choosing Experiments: The Three Constraints

**What it is.** There is no sure way to point to the one right experiment. Instead, three constraints help you identify the experiment that likely works best for your situation.

**Constraint 1: Risk category.** Certain experiments only work for certain risk categories. A/B tests illustrate this:

- Desirability: "We believe that young adults between 18 and 24 will indicate a preference for access to banking anywhere and anytime in comparison to going to traditional bank branches." An A/B test works: give users two sign-up options, one mobile, one in a branch.
- Feasibility: "We believe that we can build automations with our existing systems." An A/B test won't tell you whether you can build automations. Build a simple prototype instead.
- Viability: "Young adults between 18 and 24 are willing to pay $5 per month for an all-inclusive account." An A/B test works again, to learn about price sensitivity.
- Adaptability: "Our idea complies with banking regulations in all countries the bank operates in." A/B tests aren't ideal. Do research first or meet with expert lawyers.

Conclusion: A/B tests work for desirability and viability. Apply the same logic to other experiment types.

**Constraint 2: The journey.** Following Steve Blank's customer development process, the search phase has two parts:

- **Customer discovery.** Get out of the building to learn about customers' jobs, pains and gains. Mainly desirability, focused on the customer profile. Further evidence typically also indicates willingness to pay (viability) and whether market timing is right (adaptability).
- **Customer validation.** Evidence should show that customers are interested in your product or idea: desirability again, but focused on the value proposition. Collect viability evidence by learning how much customers will pay and what cost structures are required. Technical prototypes suggest you can manage feasibility.

**Constraint 3: Resources.** Each experiment requires a different mix of money, time and people. Adjust resources to where you are:

- **During discovery, be lean** with cheap, quick experiments. Interviews are a great example because you don't have to spend much. You can even test willingness to pay at the end of an interview by asking the person to sign up for an account. Surveys in a B2B environment may cost something to reach your target audience. If you include prototypes in discovery, use ones that need no technical skills: storyboards, videos and mock-ups.
- **Wait until validation** for technical prototypes and landing pages. By then you have evidence that the idea might work, so you can increase spending.
- **Once you have very strong evidence,** you may spend what it takes to build something closer to a real-life prototype.

### The Fourth Constraint: Strength of Evidence

**What it is.** Experiments produce different strengths of evidence, and the stronger the evidence, the more confidence you can have in the validity of your hypothesis. Four parameters influence strength, and you can dial them up and down like an equalizer.

**The four dials:**

1. **Say vs. do.** What customers say may be substantially different from what they actually do. Experiments where you observe what customers do produce stronger, more reliable evidence.
2. **Lab vs. real world.** In a lab context you are in direct contact with customers, and your presence may influence what they say and do. In the real world you are an observer and the customer doesn't know they are in a test, so evidence is more reliable because you don't influence people.
3. **Opinion vs. fact.** Some evidence is factual hard data (for example, from link tracking); some is opinion based and subjective (for example, from interviews). The closer to factual, the stronger.
4. **Low vs. high investment by the test subject.** Not only money, but also how much effort or time they put into testing your prototype. The higher the investment, the stronger the evidence.

**Worked examples:**

- **Interviews.** Fairly low strength: people say things, in a lab context (they know they are being tested), giving opinions. Investment is medium, since they take time to talk to you. Resources: fairly low.
- **Interaction prototype (lo-fi paper prototype).** People do something but also say a lot, so it sits in between. Clearly a lab context. Depending on design you get some facts and some opinions (for example, about which features users want). Investment is medium: they give time but don't commit. Resources: quite low, somewhere between a paper brochure and a napkin sketch.
- **Call to action (CTA) experiment.** Usually pretty strong. People do something: click a "notify me" button, sign up, or even make a purchase. It is a real-world experiment where people don't notice they are being tested. You collect facts: click-through rate, conversions, number of sign-ups. Investment is medium to high depending on the ask: a purchase is higher, a "notify me" or sign-up click is lower. Resources: a bit higher, depending on complexity.
- **Data analysis.** You collect information, so it's closer to say than do, since you don't ask people to interact with anything. It is real world and, depending on the research topic, likely more fact based (for example, government census data about your demography, or social media analytics). Subject investment is very low because you collect passively. Resources: fairly low to medium, depending on whether you pay for research.

**Why it matters.** Cheaper experiments tend to produce weaker evidence and vice versa. To get really strong evidence you need the dials up and will likely spend more. Rule of thumb: at the beginning of the journey, during customer discovery, you don't necessarily need the strongest evidence and shouldn't spend a lot. The farther along you are and the more you spend, the stronger you want your evidence to be.

**Why use all four constraints (risk category, journey, resources, strength of evidence):**

1. They help you find an appropriate experiment for where you are.
2. They help you justify decisions and get budgets from decision makers.
3. If an experiment is already running, you can use them to evaluate whether you are on the right path and whether you can have high or low confidence in the evidence you are collecting.

**Best practices for the final choice:**

- Go cheap and fast in the beginning.
- Always pick the experiment that produces the strongest evidence given your constraints.
- Reduce uncertainty as much as you can before you build anything.
- The less you know, the less time, energy and money you should spend. When you know little, your only goal is evidence that points you in the right direction, and quick, cheap experiments suit that goal despite generally weak evidence. The more you know, the stronger the evidence should become, usually through more costly and lengthier experiments.

### Designing Good Experiments: Participants and Artifact

**What it is.** Choosing an experiment is not enough. Some experiments produce weak evidence by nature, but you can design them to strengthen it. Others produce strong evidence by nature, but if designed or executed badly they produce noise and weak evidence. Two things matter: selecting the right participants and designing a well-crafted artifact.

#### Selecting Participants

**Common mistake 1: segments too broad.** Broad segments produce data that is too general to give a clear indicator about your hypothesis. Ask: who are you designing the idea for? That should be your test audience. LifeTree Bank's audience is age specific (Generation Z); they are not interested in parents' or older siblings' behaviors, so other age groups are excluded. Then segment further: all of Generation Z, or only specific genders, locations, professions, income brackets, occupations or education levels? Choose participants wisely by unbundling your customer profiles. Focus strengthens evidence.

**Common mistake 2: sample size too small.** In principle, the larger the sample, the better. Rule of thumb to start: at least 30 participants per experiment, because if you choose participants well you likely get a good distribution.

**Common mistake 3: sample not representative.** This is different from too small. A non-representative sample means participants don't represent your targeted segment. Once you have unbundled the customer profile, vet participants with qualifying screener questions during recruitment, such as "What is your age bracket?" or "What is your employment status?", depending on your ideal segment. Participants should represent the majority of who you want to target.

**Small markets.** Sometimes the market itself is small. In B2B you may have only 50 or 100 customers in total. If you manage to speak to half of those, you have done well.

#### Crafting the Artifact

**What it is.** An experiment and an artifact are not the same. In an A/B test, the artifact is the two landing pages. In a customer discovery interview, the artifact is the interview guide and its questions.

**Example: A/B test artifact (LifeTree Bank car loans).** Hypothesis: automations for car loan applications will increase conversion rates. Test: an A/B test with two pages, one with automation and one without. Metric: conversion rates between the two sites. Criteria: we are right with a 10% increase. Randomly, 50% of visitors get a page to make an appointment by phone; the other 50% get a page with a quiz where they learn whether they qualify. Changes between the sites are strictly limited to that one parameter, so you can compare exactly how current site A performs against automation B, creating a clear connection between evidence and hypothesis. If you also changed other parameters (for example, used the automation for a different product), you would get mixed data with no clear indication. In an A/B test, change only one parameter.

**Example: interview guide artifact.** A high-quality interview guide is a real art. A few of many best practices:

- Don't ask for opinions ("Would you...?", "Do you think...?"). Opinions are not strong evidence. Ask questions that get interviewees talking about facts, such as "Tell me about a time when..."
- Don't focus on solutions (your value proposition) too early. People might say yes, they're interested, but they may just want to be nice to you.
- Focus on the problem: identify jobs, pains and gains with questions like "What are the things you struggle with?" and "How do you measure success?"

Each experiment type comes with its own principles for crafting a strong artifact. Paying attention to artifact quality strengthens evidence and your confidence in insights.

### Choosing the Right Metrics

**What it is.** After a strong hypothesis and a strong experiment, decide what to measure. The data you collect is the basis for your decisions, so you must measure the right things, and decide this before running the experiment.

**Rule 1: Avoid vanity metrics; use actionable metrics.**

- A vanity metric makes you look good. It is exciting because it suggests improvement. It can be a great ego boost if millions of people follow your brand's Twitter account after a campaign, but if those follows don't translate into sales, it doesn't mean much.
- Vanity example: Hypothesis "We believe that increasing our Twitter followers will result in more sales." Test: run a Twitter campaign. Metric: number of Twitter followers. Criteria: more than 2 million followers. The metric is not connected to the hypothesis. It doesn't prove more sales, you can't decide anything, and you wasted time and money, no closer to validating the idea.
- An actionable metric has a clear connection to the hypothesis. Change the metric to the increase in conversion rate, with a threshold of a 1% increase. Because it ties back to the hypothesis, it drives action at the end of the experiment and informs next steps.

**Rule 2: Avoid absolute metrics; use comparative metrics.**

- Absolute metrics alone usually prove little. Followers going from 1.5 million to 2.3 million, 800,000 more, doesn't tell you whether the campaign was better than a previous one for sales, or better than doing nothing at all.
- Comparative metrics show whether something changes relative to something else. What's critical for this business model is conversion: did the campaign increase the conversion rate? Switching the lens from followers to conversion rate generates concrete business results (more sales) and will likely change how you design the campaign itself.
- Ideally use ratios. Typical ratio metrics are percentages showing that something increased over time, allowing comparison between old and new.

### Setting Success Criteria

**What it is.** Success criteria answer the question "What does success look like?" so you can make a decision at the end of the experiment.

**Why set them first.** A common mistake is setting success criteria during or after the experiment. First, it makes decisions harder because you don't know what you're looking for: you are flying blind. Second, and perhaps more impactful, it introduces personal bias. You become emotionally attached to the hypothesis, which creates pressure to perform and stress, and you unknowingly sabotage the testing process.

**How to do it:**

1. Set the criteria before running the experiment. This is the first and most important rule.
2. Have someone else review your criteria. An independent voice is best.
3. Don't cheat yourself by setting a low bar just to prove you're right. Set criteria based on your business needs, which depend on your idea and your industry.

**Three tips for getting the threshold right:**

1. **Back-of-the-napkin business model math.** How many users do you need for the idea to succeed? How much profit do you need to cover costs? What is your current performance? This high-level look is your first guidepost.
2. **Research industry patterns and trends.** Look at business models similar to your idea. For software, look at SaaS metrics and benchmarks. For a pop-up store concept, check retailers.
3. **Account for variations.** Testing with early adopters warrants a higher threshold than testing with the majority of users. Seasons of the year can also affect results, so adjust accordingly.

**Example (LifeTree Bank).** To decrease costs, an overarching goal is to move at least 20% of manually processed business from call centers to automation via the digital platform. One experiment is the car loan A/B test, a first indicator of whether some business can be automated. A quick Google search shows the national benchmark conversion rate hovers between 2.5% and 3%. LifeTree Bank starts at a conversion rate of 2.25%. A 10% increase lands at 2.475%. A more aggressive 20% increase lands at 2.7%, a good industry benchmark. Given the variation of Generation Z early adopters who are used to accessing things digitally, a 20% increase is an appropriate success criterion. Knowing your competitors and other industry players helps set the right criteria.

### Running a Mix of Experiments

**What it is.** Running several experiments, with increasing strength of evidence, for the same hypothesis instead of relying on one.

**Why it works.** A single data source is not enough evidence to reduce the risk of a hypothesis that could kill your business. Because you start with cheap, quick experiments, early evidence is usually not strong. Teams also tend to favor certain experiments. Some only do interviews, which tell you what people say but not what they do. Others dislike talking to customers and prefer A/B tests, which show what people do but give no insight into why. You need evidence for both. A mix gives stronger evidence over time, multiple data points, and more confidence.

**Example (LifeTree Bank pop-up stores).**

1. **50 customer discovery interviews** tested: "We believe that young adults between 18 and 24 will indicate a preference for access to banking anywhere and anytime in comparison to going to traditional bank branches." Evidence supported the hypothesis, but interview evidence isn't strong enough to justify building a pop-up store.
2. **A/B test** of how many users prefer a pop-up store over a retail concept, showing what people do rather than say. Evidence supported the hypothesis. Stronger than interviews, but still not enough to build a store.
3. **Real-life prototype.** The A/B test collected email addresses, so they invited sign-ups to a real-life prototype: a table with a bit of branding in a shopping mall. The question was whether people who signed up would also show up. Not a single person from the email list came. This type of experiment can produce strong evidence; here it was a strong signal that what people said wasn't what they did. The evidence did not support the hypothesis.

After three experiments of different strength, the team had to go back to the drawing board and figure out why sign-ups didn't show up. It doesn't mean dropping pop-up stores entirely: perhaps the location was wrong, or they selected the wrong audience. They need to identify the barriers before continuing. Had they stopped after the interviews and built a full-fledged pop-up store in a mall, they would have wasted huge amounts of money.

### Your Turn: Choose and Design an Experiment (45 minutes)

**What you need:** your business idea, your most important hypothesis, the Test Card, the Experiment Library (or a Strategyzer App project).

1. **Pick your most important hypothesis** from the prioritization exercise. This is what you test first.
2. **Assess your situation** on the four factors: risk category, journey (discovery or validation), available resources (early on, stick to cheap and quick experiments to pinpoint the right direction), and required strength of evidence.
3. **Choose an experiment** from the Experiment Library and double check it against the best practices: cheap and fast early, strongest evidence within your constraints, reduce uncertainty before building anything.
4. **Design it with the Test Card:**
   - Hypothesis: make sure it is testable, precise and discrete.
   - Experiment: selecting the experiment is just the start. A strong artifact and the right participants are key to strengthening evidence.
   - Metrics: avoid vanity and absolute metrics; use actionable metrics that allow a decision.
   - Success criteria: always set them before the experiment; do industry or competitor research to set them well.

## 5. The Experiment Library

Every experiment has strengths and weaknesses. Some are quick and cheap but produce less reliable evidence; some produce more reliable evidence but need more time and money. Consider your journey, risk category, resources and required strength of evidence when you choose. Rule of thumb: start cheap when uncertainty is high and increase spending as certainty grows. The five experiments below are a few of many; the book *Testing Business Ideas* contains a detailed library of 44 experiments.

### Library Overview

Resources and evidence strength are rated on a 5-point scale.

| Experiment | Risk categories | Journey | Resources | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unique Link Tracking | Desirability | Customer discovery | 1.5 / 5 | 3 / 5 |
| Interviews | Desirability, Viability, Adaptability | Discovery and validation | 2 / 5 | 1 / 5 |
| Mock sales | Desirability, Viability | Discovery and validation | 2.5 / 5 | 5 / 5 |
| A/B test | Desirability, Viability | Customer validation | 2.5 / 5 | 3 / 5 |
| Wizard of Oz | Desirability, Feasibility, Viability | Customer validation | 2.5 / 5 | 5 / 5 |

### Unique Link Tracking

**What it is.** Unique, trackable links that verify a potential customer's or partner's interest beyond what they tell you in a meeting, interview or call. An extremely simple way to measure genuine interest.

**Why it works.** People say they are interested to be polite. Whether they actually click a link you gave them is behavior, not opinion.

**How to do it:**

1. **"Fabricate" a unique link.** Make a unique, trackable link to more detailed information about your idea (for example, a download or landing page) with a link-shortening service such as bitly or goo.gl.
2. **Pitch and track.** Explain your idea to a potential customer or partner. During or after the meeting (via email), give them the unique link and mention that it points to more detailed information.
3. **Learn about genuine interest.** Track whether the customer used the link. If not, it may indicate lack of interest, or that they have more important jobs, pains and gains than the ones your idea addresses.

### Customer Discovery Interviews

**What it is.** Talking to customers to gain insights relevant to your context, using the Value Proposition Canvas to prepare interviews and to organize the chaotic mass of information coming at you during the interview process.

**How to do it:**

1. **Create a customer profile.** Sketch the jobs, pains and gains you believe characterize the targeted customer. Rank them in order of importance.
2. **Create an interview outline.** Ask yourself what you want to learn. Derive the questions from your customer profile, asking about the most important jobs, pains and gains (use the Interview Questions Cheat Sheet in Section 6).
3. **Conduct the interview** following the Ground Rules for Interviewing (Section 6).
4. **Capture.** Map the jobs, pains and gains you learned onto an empty customer profile. Also capture business model learnings. Write down your most important insights.
5. **Review the interview.** Assess whether the interview questions need revising based on what you learned. Repeat steps 2 to 5.
6. **Search for patterns.** Can you discover similar jobs, pains and gains? What stands out? What is similar or different among interviewees, and why? Can you detect specific (recurring) contexts that influence jobs, pains and gains?
7. **Synthesize.** Make a separate synthesized customer profile for every customer segment that emerges from all your interviews. Write your most important insights on sticky notes.

### Mock Sales

**What it is.** Setting up a mock sale before your value proposition even exists, to test sincere customer interest. The goal is to make customers believe they are completing a real purchase. Easy online, also possible physically.

**Online: three levels of customer commitment:**

1. Learn about customer interest by measuring how many people click a simple "buy now" button.
2. Learn how pricing influences interest. Combine with A/B testing to learn about demand elasticity and the optimal price point.
3. Get hard data by simulating a transaction with the customer's credit card information. This is the strongest evidence of customer demand.

**Physical world: what retailers do:**

- Introduce products that don't exist yet in a limited number of (mail order) catalogs.
- Sell a product in one retail location only, for a limited time.

**Managing customer perception.** Don't fear that mock sales will alienate customers and hurt your brand. Managed well, they become an advantage and turn test subjects into brand advocates:

- Explain that you were performing a test after the customer completes the mock purchase.
- Be transparent about which information you keep or erase.
- Always erase credit card information in a fake purchase.
- Offer a reward for participating (for example, goodies or discounts).

### A/B Test

**What it is.** A technique to compare the performance of two options. The most common form tests two variations of a web page. It was popularized by companies such as Google and LinkedIn, as well as the 2008 Obama campaign, and can also run in the physical world. The main learning instrument is comparing whether conversion rates for a specific call to action differ between competing alternatives.

**What to test:**

- Alternative features
- Pricing
- Discount
- Copy text
- Packaging
- Website variations
- Call to action

**Calls to action to measure (how many subjects perform the CTA):**

- Purchase
- Email sign-up
- Click on button
- Survey
- Completion of any task

**Tips:**

- Test a single variation if you want to clearly identify what leads to better performance.
- Use multivariate testing to test several combined elements and find which combination has the highest impact.
- Use Google AdWords or other options to attract test subjects.
- Reach a statistical significance greater than 95%.
- Use tools such as Google Website Optimizer, Optimizely or others to run A/B tests easily.

### Wizard of Oz

**What it is.** An MVP with a front stage that mimics a real, working value proposition, while on the back end you manually carry out what would normally be an automated delivery process.

**Why it works.** It tells you whether your solution actually exceeds a certain threshold before you automate and scale it more permanently, and it shows where automation is worth it.

**How to do it:**

- **Prepare.** Plan the steps of creating the product manually. Test the steps with someone internally first to make sure they work. Integrate and verify web analytics (if required).
- **Execute.** Find users. Conduct the experiment. Document how long each task took. Gather user feedback with interviews and surveys.
- **Analyze.** Review customer satisfaction feedback. Review metrics for: length of time for each task; where delays occurred in the process; how many purchased or interacted with the CTA.

Use the findings to improve your next Wizard of Oz experiment and to decide where to automate the process.

## 6. Customer Interviews: Ground Rules and Question Bank

### Ground Rules for Interviewing

**What it is.** Conducting good interviews that yield relevant insights for value proposition design is an art. Focus on unearthing what matters to (potential) customers rather than pitching solutions. Eight rules:

1. **Adopt a beginner's mind.** Listen with a "fresh pair of ears" and avoid interpretation. Explore unexpected jobs, pains and gains in particular.
2. **Listen more than you talk.** Your goal is to listen and learn, not to inform, impress or convince the customer of anything. Talking about your own beliefs wastes time at the expense of learning about your customer.
3. **Get facts, not opinions.** Don't ask "Would you...?" Ask "When is the last time you have...?"
4. **Ask "why" to get real motivations.** Ask "Why do you need to do...?", "Why is ___ important to you?", "Why is ___ such a pain?"
5. **The goal of customer insight interviews is not selling (even if a sale is involved); it's learning.** Don't ask "Would you buy our solution?" Ask "What are your decision criteria when you make a purchase of...?"
6. **Don't mention solutions (your prototype value proposition) too early.** Don't explain "Our solution does..." Ask "What are the most important things you are struggling with?"
7. **Follow up.** Get permission to keep the interviewee's contact information so you can come back with more questions or to test prototypes.
8. **Always open doors at the end.** Ask "Who else should I talk to?"

### Weak vs. Strong Statements Within an Interview

Interviews generally produce weaker evidence because what people say isn't what they do. But some pieces of evidence from an interview are stronger than others from the same interview, so watch for opinion statements versus fact statements:

- **Weak, opinions (beliefs):** "I would...", "I think... is important", "I believe..."
- **Strong, facts (events):** "Last week I...", "The last time that situation came up I...", "I spent $... on..."

### Interview Questions Cheat Sheet

**How to use it.** Your interview questions must always relate to one or several hypotheses underlying your Value Proposition Canvas. You will not cover all six topics in one interview and are not expected to. Start with the Customer Profile topics and expand to Value Map topics once you understand your customers fairly well.

#### Customer Profile Questions

**1. Customer segmentation.** Questions that help you understand the demographics characterizing your interviewee.

- Who are you?
- What do you do?
- Who handles the process of (job-to-be-done) at home/office?
- How involved are you in the process of (job-to-be-done)?

**2. Your customer's jobs, pains and gains.** Test hypotheses about jobs, pains and gains first. You'll learn what matters most, but only if you immerse yourself in their world and don't keep your solution in mind.

- What are your most significant problems? What happens when they occur?
- What are your personal/professional goals?
- What's the hardest part of your day?
- What is the most frustrating thing about (your task, your role, your day, etc.)?
- What's the hardest thing when doing (activity related to fulfilling a job)?
- What are your current challenges at work/home? At work/home, what tasks are creating serious headaches?
- When was the last time this happened? Can you tell me what happened in more detail? How often does this happen?
- What do these hurdles block you from achieving? Describe what a day would look like if these hurdles suddenly vanished.
- Can you give me a specific description of what success looks like for you? For your customers?
- How have you tried to achieve these goals so far? Can you give me an example?
- What are you trying to achieve by doing (activity related to fulfilling a job)?
- What could improve the process of (job-to-be-done)? Can you describe how this would work?
- What would make your life easier at work/home?

**3. The intensity of your customer's jobs, pains and gains.** Not all jobs, pains and gains matter. Identify which ones matter most and which are trivial.

- Can you rank the issues you mentioned with the most critical at the top?
- On a scale of 1 (not important) to 10 (very important), how much would this problem score?
- What's a 10/10 problem?
- How much time per week are you willing to spend to find a solution for (problem/process) or fix it?
- What's the maximum you would pay if someone or something could instantly dissolve this pain/problem?
- If you could solve only one problem at work/home, which would you focus on?

#### Value Map Questions

**4. How existing solutions create value and where they fall short.** Learning about existing solutions to the same jobs, pains and gains gives you an edge in designing a superior alternative. Learn what customers love or hate about them.

- Do you use outside services or products to solve (job-to-be-done)?
- Under what circumstances do you use them? Were they successful? Tell me about the last time you used one.
- On a scale of 0 (poor) to 10 (excellent), what grade would you give this product? Why?
- How do you obtain solutions to those needs?
- Tell me about the last time you searched for a solution: where/how did you search and what happened?
- What do you wish you could do with these products that you can't do today?
- What could be done to improve this product?
- What's most appealing to you about this product?
- What might improve your experience using the product?
- What motivates you to continue using this product?
- What's the hardest part about using this product?
- What features do you wish the product had? Why?

**5. The fit between your customer profile and your value proposition.** Interviews are opportunities to co-create with customers and check whether your value proposition addresses their jobs, pains and gains.

- What would an ideal solution do to solve your problem? What would it look like?
- Based on the ideal solution you described, could you help me rank the features by importance to you?
- Can you walk me through the process of acquiring, using and disposing of this solution?
- Why do you think this solution hasn't been created yet? If someone creates it, would there be hurdles to its adoption?
- What do you think could be done to help you with (pain/job)?
- What product do you wish you had that doesn't exist yet?
- How likely are you to tell your friends about this product?
- What would make you want to tell your friends about this product?
- Here is a set of 5 features: do they create value for you? How so? Can you rank them by importance?
- How would your (day, experience, task) be different if you could have (pain reliever/gain creator)?
- Would a product that focuses on (pain reliever/gain creator) solve your problem? Why?

**6. How much your pain relievers and gain creators matter to customers.** This one is critical. Your value proposition has to create substantial value and matter enough that customers make the effort to get it and pay for it (if it's paid).

- On a scale of 0 (useless) to 10 (extremely useful), how important is (your pain reliever/gain creator) to you?
- What obstacles might prevent you from using a product that does (pain reliever/gain creator): cost, task, office/home politics, other products?
- Can you rank the features I've presented by importance to you?
- How much time/money are you willing to spend on a product that does (your pain reliever/gain creator)?

## 7. Evidence: Collect and Synthesize

### What Evidence Is

**What it is.** Evidence is what allows you to support or refute a hypothesis. It is data generated from an experiment or collected directly in the field: the facts that support or refute a hypothesis. It comes in very different forms, such as quotes from interviews, behaviors you observed, or conversion rates from experiments.

**Why it matters.** Traditionally we don't focus on evidence at all. We write business plans and hold very strong opinions about our ideas. When high uncertainty exists, we are better off testing ideas to produce evidence that shows whether we are on the right path, because that is what ultimately reduces uncertainty. You support or refute hypotheses based on real-world facts, not opinions.

### Not All Evidence Is Equal

**Example: three ways to test the same hypothesis about product managers.**

1. **Interviews.** Measure what people tell you. The transcript contains statements that support and statements that refute the hypothesis. Very cheap and quick, a first indicator of whether you are on the right path.
2. **Field observation of product managers.** Takes more energy, but is stronger evidence because you measure whether they actually struggle with the task. Seeing people struggle (or not) is much stronger than just asking.
3. **Sales simulation.** Measure how many website visitors actually purchase. Very strong evidence because it gets closer to a commercial situation.

**Principle.** What people say is usually a poor indicator of what they will really do. Observing people is stronger than talking to them, and the best evidence gets closest to the real-world situation you want to test.

**Right evidence at the right time.** Shouldn't you only pursue strong evidence? No. When uncertainty is very high, relatively weak evidence is fine because it lets you move fast with fast experiments. As you reduce uncertainty, you want very strong evidence, and the closer you get to a real-world situation, the better it indicates whether you will succeed. Rule of thumb: it's okay to start with weak evidence, but the more you know, the stronger your evidence should get.

### The Evidence, Insight, Action Relationship and the Learning Card

**What it is.** Once experiments are designed with the Test Card, you run them and make observations. You collect evidence, draw conclusions about the hypothesis (insights), and take action. Four types of insight are possible: the evidence supports the hypothesis, doesn't support it, the insights are unclear, or you learn something new. Depending on the insight, you go back to the drawing board to redesign parts of your business model, or you continue testing the same or the next hypothesis. A good action is one where you can be highly confident you made the right decision, meaning the decision is clearly connected to the evidence collected, and every decision ties back to the original hypothesis.

**The Learning Card** captures learnings in four steps:

1. **Step 1, Hypothesis: "We believed that..."** The hypothesis you just tested.
2. **Step 2, Observation: "We observed..."** The evidence, grouped into themes, with a **Data Reliability** rating (strength of evidence).
3. **Step 3, Learnings and insights: "From that we learned that..."** Your conclusions, with an **Action Required** rating: if what you learned might endanger your business idea or put you out of business, identify something to do about it.
4. **Step 4, Decisions and actions: "Therefore, we will..."**

Metadata: Insight Name, Person Responsible, Date of Learning.

**Example (LifeTree Bank interviews).**

- We believed that young adults between 18 and 24 will indicate a preference for access to banking anywhere and anytime in comparison to going to traditional bank branches.
- We observed that 72% of young adults between 18 and 24 indicated a preference for access to banking anywhere and anytime (against a target of 60%). Data reliability: weak, because interviews produce weak evidence.
- From that we learned that the evidence supports the hypothesis: there is sufficient interest in banking anytime and anywhere.
- Therefore, we will conduct an A/B test to collect further evidence and to create an email list.

Because interviews show what customers say, and what people say is a poor predictor of what they do, following up with an A/B test that measures what people might do is a good decision. The team repeats this until it has high confidence the idea is ready for market. Four elements in the Test Card help design strong experiments; four elements in the Learning Card help structure learnings and decide next steps.

### Collecting Evidence: Qualitative Data

**What it is.** Qualitative evidence tells you about the why and how of human behavior. Interviews produce qualitative data as quotes, personal stories and challenges, and reveal a lot about jobs, pains and gains. The way you collect and code data affects evidence quality, and therefore insights and actions.

**How to do it:**

1. **Create a worksheet (Interview Notes template)** to take notes during the experiment. At the top: the hypothesis (there may be more than one) and the customer's name. Then sections for: quotes supporting the hypothesis; quotes not supporting the hypothesis; new insights; jobs, pains and gains for each customer.
2. **Interview in pairs.** One person interviews while the other takes notes on the worksheet. Writing helps process what's happening, and you debrief more effectively.
3. **Ask permission to record** so you have a transcript to work from.
4. **Build a customer profile per interviewee** from the transcript: highlight every place the customer mentions a job, pain or gain, then transfer them to a customer profile.

These techniques (worksheet, recordings, customer profiles) help you code qualitative data as efficiently as possible, and they apply to other qualitative experiments too, such as focus groups, usability tests and others where users verbalize their thoughts.

### Collecting Evidence: Quantitative Data

**What it is.** Quantitative evidence tells you how much, which choice is preferred, how long, and so on: things measured against a standard scale, such as time to complete a task, number of site visitors, or average purchase.

**Example.** In an A/B test, 50% of users go to site A and 50% to site B. Depending on your hypothesis you might measure: impressions on the starting page; time spent on each page once funneled into the test; conversion rates (which page performed better); social shares. Tools like Google Analytics help analyze the data and identify a leading option, and also give rich background data such as where users come from geographically, which search terms they used, and whether they came via desktop or mobile.

**How to do it well:**

1. **Choose a reliable instrument of measurement,** such as Google Analytics.
2. **Tag your data correctly from the beginning,** or you end up with a messy data set. This applies beyond websites: in preselling, tag people who purchase versus those who don't. The better you tag, the more insight you can derive.
3. **Collect as many data points as possible.** The more data points, the more reliable the evidence.

Quantitative data is easy to measure, so collection takes less time than coding qualitative data. Run experiments almost like a scientist so evidence is clean and not misleading. The better you collect and code data, the easier it is to synthesize evidence and identify patterns for the Learning Card.

### Synthesizing Evidence Into Observations

**What it is.** Turning many pieces of evidence into themes that become the observations (step 2) on the Learning Card. Evidence does not speak on its own. Two steps matter for both qualitative and quantitative evidence: **coding** (grouping similar pieces of evidence) and **finding themes** (headers for each group). Keep a copy of your detailed evidence.

#### Technique 1: Affinity Mapping (from recordings)

1. Gather all pieces of evidence and put them on a wall or online whiteboard, all visible at once.
2. Code: individually, take two pieces you think belong together and group them. Continue until every piece is grouped with something else.
3. Identify themes: once everything is grouped, use a different color sticky note to create a header for each group. Ask: what keywords summarize the central evidence of this group?
4. These headers are your major observations and go onto the Learning Card.

#### Technique 2: Worksheet Categories (from interview notes)

If you took notes on a worksheet that categorized quotes, you already coded your evidence to some degree.

1. Put all sheets in one place.
2. Create three categories: quotes that support the hypothesis, quotes that don't, and new insights.
3. Group every quote under a category.
4. Identify themes. LifeTree Bank examples: 39 of 50 interviewees use mobile banking; 33 of 50 don't enjoy going to a retail bank; 37 of 50 prefer mobile services over call center services.
5. Write the observation in relation to the Test Card metric (here: how many people prefer mobile banking over traditional retail banking). Although interviewees expressed their preference in different words, group them in relation to the hypothesis: "72% indicated a preference for access to banking anywhere and anytime" (72% being the average of all interviewees who indicated a preference for mobile banking in their own words).
6. Add new insights to the observations too. Example: although participants enjoy mobile banking, 21 of 50 interviewees also said they would like a physical bank to come to where they spend their time, for example at schools or in a coffee shop. It is weak evidence for a potential solution, but an interesting tidbit worth tracking.

#### Technique 3: Master Customer Profile

If you made a customer profile per interviewee, put them all on a wall or digital whiteboard. Ask: which jobs, pains and gains are mentioned several times? Are any new? Synthesize them into a single master profile and compare it with the original customer profile you started with.

#### Quantitative Synthesis

If you chose a good instrument and tagged data correctly from the start (meaning it's already coded), finding themes is much faster than for qualitative evidence. Example: an A/B test measuring conversion rates with Google Analytics yields two datasets that directly inform the observation, for instance "conversion rate for version B is more than 50% higher than version A."

**When the data looks odd.** Suppose both versions show unusually high conversion rates, much higher than the roughly 3% you had before, at 12.5% and 12.7%, performing about the same, and you tagged everything correctly. First, capture it objectively: "Conversion rates of 12.5% and 12.7% were unusually high and no clear preference came out of this experiment." Then dig deeper to understand what happened, which belongs in the learnings and insights step.

**Key distinction.** Observations (step 2) are the evidence grouped into themes, looked at as objectively as possible. Learnings and insights (step 3) interpret those observations against the hypothesis and draw conclusions. Evidence collected during an experiment goes in step 2, not step 3.

### Practice: Write an Observation

**Test Card (LifeTree Bank), name: Search Trend Analysis; assigned to: Monique.**

- We believe that young adults 18-24 want to learn about investment strategies.
- To verify that, we will perform search trend analysis on young adults 18-24 searching on YouTube for investment training videos.
- And measure domestic search volume (the amount of searches that occur for a particular keyword within a specific geographical region).

## 8. Insights: Learn From Evidence

### What an Insight Is

**What it is.** An insight is what you learn from studying the evidence: the patterns you detect. It is learning related to the validity of a hypothesis, but it can also be the discovery of a completely new direction. Insights are the foundation for informed business decisions and, ultimately, action.

**How insights differ from evidence.** Evidence relates to your original business idea, but evidence doesn't speak on its own. You need to analyze it. You take all the evidence, analyze, observe and study it, capture observations (with the Learning Card), and from those observations gain insights. Insights then lead your actions.

**Evidence or insight? Examples:**

- "75% of our users make investments of $1,000 or more per year." Evidence: clearly based on data generated from an experiment.
- "2 out of 10 interviewees claimed that they would never use Option A." Evidence: data from an experiment.
- "The current design of our online platform doesn't produce the behavior we intended from our users." Insight: a lesson learned from reviewing several pieces of evidence to validate or refute a hypothesis.
- "For our prototype design, we recommend working with option B as it is the one that creates the most value for product managers." Insight.

**More experiments, stronger insights.** Insights don't come from one experiment only. You can test the same hypothesis with a series of completely different experiments, producing more evidence. The more evidence, the more confident you can be about your insights and that you're on the right path.

**Insights are not the goal.** What you really want is to make informed decisions and take action from idea towards a validated business case.

### Generating Insights: The Success Criteria Question

**What it is.** You make sense of evidence by asking one question: **"Do our observations in the Learning Card meet the success criteria we defined in the Test Card?"** There are four possible answers:

1. Evidence supports the hypothesis.
2. Evidence doesn't support the hypothesis.
3. Insights are unclear and you need more testing.
4. The evidence produced a new insight.

**Example series (LifeTree Bank), same hypothesis:** "We believe that young adults between 18 and 24 will indicate a preference for access to banking anywhere and anytime in comparison to going to traditional bank branches."

**Experiment 1: interviews with 50 customers.** Metric: how many prefer mobile banking over traditional banking. Criteria: more than 60%. Observation: 72% indicated a preference. Do observations meet the criteria? A clear yes: **evidence supports the hypothesis.** A **new insight** also emerged: 42% of interviewees value a personal relationship with an adviser as very important. That's worth capturing as they design their mobile solution, because it tells them something they didn't know: people prefer mobile banking, but that doesn't mean they don't want personal relationships. One doesn't exclude the other.

**Experiment 2: A/B test.** Users choose between signing up for a physical branch or a pop-up store. Metric: how many sign up for the pop-up version. Criteria: the pop-up version meets a minimum conversion rate of 3% and is 20% higher than the physical branch version. Observation: version A (physical branch) converts at 12.5%, version B (pop-up) at 12.6%. Two lessons: the rates are unusually high (conversion rates usually hover around 2% to 6%), and no clear preference emerged, since the difference is marginal. **Insights are unclear.** It turned out the experiment was flawed: back-end data collection was set up incorrectly. LifeTree Bank improved the experiment, reran it, and the data supported the hypothesis.

**Experiment 3: real-life prototype in a shopping mall,** using the resulting email list. Metric: how many from the list show up. Criteria: 5% show up. Nobody came. **Evidence does not support the hypothesis.** Now they must ask: was a shopping mall the right location? Did they choose the right segment for the notification email? Or is the evidence strong enough to kill the entire idea? These questions directly shape the next action.

### When Insights Are Unclear

Insights are typically unclear when:

- The evidence failed the success criteria by a small margin, meaning there isn't a clear preference (for instance, between two options).
- The data doesn't add up or doesn't make sense, which may indicate a mistake in the experiment itself or in data collection.
- Qualitative data is all over the place.

Sometimes this signals a poorly designed experiment, but it can also just be the way the situation is. With mixed qualitative data, it can help to focus on just one segment or one specific pain or gain.

## 9. Taking Action

### What an Action Is

**What it is.** An action is your next step towards derisking your business idea: an informed decision based on the evidence you collected in the field and the insights you generated from it. It is the decision to abandon, change and/or continue to test your original idea. Learning and insights without action are useless.

**Why it matters.** Testing is not about running experiments; it's about making progress towards building a successful business. To make progress you take action at the end of each testing cycle based on what you learned.

**Mindset.** When evidence refutes a hypothesis, it's bad news, but if you've been doing this right, you didn't waste a lot of time and energy. Be prepared: the more innovative you are, the more likely you are to be wrong. That's okay, because it's designed into the process.

### The Four Types of Action

1. **New hypothesis:** stay in testing and move on to the next critical hypothesis.
2. **Same hypothesis:** continue testing the same hypothesis to collect further evidence.
3. **Update business model and value proposition:** move to design and update (or start over).
4. **Kill the idea:** the evidence shows the idea won't work in reality.

**Mapping insights to actions:**

| Insight | Possible actions |
|---|---|
| Evidence supports hypothesis | Move to the next hypothesis, or continue testing the same hypothesis for stronger evidence. Depends on strength of evidence and on previous experiments for the same hypothesis |
| Evidence doesn't support hypothesis | Move to design (start over or update the business model), which creates new hypotheses; or continue testing the same hypothesis with a different experiment; or kill the idea |
| Unclear insights | Continue testing the same hypothesis (continue the last experiment or run a series of new ones) before making any decision |
| New insight | Treat it as a newly discovered assumption: continue testing it as a new hypothesis for stronger evidence, or update the design. Depends on the type of insight. It could be new jobs, pains and gains, a completely different customer segment, or that competitors already have a much better solution. Everything is open, so keep your eyes open |

### The Two Questions Before Every Decision

1. **How strong is the evidence you collected?** This indicates whether the evidence is of high quality. Different experiment types produce different strengths: a quote from an interview is a relatively weak indicator of future behavior, while a purchase in a simulated sale is a very strong one. But choosing the experiment is not enough; strength also depends on design quality. Use the Test Card as the reminder: a strong hypothesis (testable, precise, discrete); the right participants and a well-crafted artifact (for example, interview guide questions); a metric that measures the right things; and appropriate success criteria that allow a decision.
2. **Did you run any other experiments for this same hypothesis previously?** The more experiments for the same hypothesis, the more confident you can be about its validity: more data points and, ideally, experiments with increasingly stronger evidence. The first question is automatically implied in the second.

### Confidence Levels

**What it is.** Your confidence level indicates how much you believe your evidence is strong enough to support or refute a specific hypothesis. There are four levels: **very confident, somewhat confident, not really confident, not confident at all.** They depend on how many experiments you ran, how many data points you collected, and whether experiments produced increasingly stronger evidence (moving from what people say to what people do). The higher you climb the decision ladder, the more confident you can be.

**Worked examples (Owlet):**

| Hypothesis | Tests, strength, data points | Confidence to move forward | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nurses find wireless pulse oximetry more convenient than traditional pulse oximetry | Interview, weak, 58 nurses | Not confident at all | Only one experiment, and it produced weak evidence. More experimenting needed |
| A smart bootie is convenient and easy to use for monitoring | MVP, strong, 17,000 views, 5,500 Facebook shares, 500 positive comments | Somewhat confident | One experiment, but strong evidence and many data points |
| Parents want to rent the product versus buy at a $200 sales price | Survey (weak, 105 mothers); interview (weak, 33 baby stores); survey (weak, 139 and 40 mothers); A/B test (strong, 1,130 people) | Very confident | Several experiments for the same hypothesis with increasingly stronger evidence; the last one produced very strong evidence with many data points |
| We can build wireless transmission from the baby alarm to the smartphone | Prototype, medium, 1 data point | Not really confident | Needs more and stronger experiments, such as a single-feature interaction MVP. Building a prototype in a lab is different from a real-life prototype used by customers |

### Action When Evidence Supports the Hypothesis

**How to decide (decision ladder):**

1. How strong is the evidence? If weak, continue testing the same hypothesis, either with a new experiment that produces stronger evidence or with the same experiment better designed.
2. If strong: did you run other experiments for this hypothesis? If not, stay with the same hypothesis and run a new experiment that creates even stronger evidence.
3. If yes (several experiments with increasingly stronger evidence), only then are you confident enough to move on to the next important hypothesis.

**Example (LifeTree Bank).** The first experiment for the highest-risk hypothesis, customer discovery interviews, supported it. Interviews generally produce weak evidence, though good design strengthens it a bit. Because evidence was weak, they should test the same hypothesis more; because the experiment was well designed, they can pick a new experiment with stronger evidence rather than rerun it. They chose an A/B test, which also built an email list for future purposes. At this point they are not confident at all about the hypothesis, because interviews only reveal what people say, which may differ substantially from what they do. The A/B test shows what people do, and running more experiments with stronger evidence lets them move up the confidence levels.

### Action When Evidence Doesn't Support the Hypothesis

**How to decide:**

1. How strong is the evidence? If the experiment type produces weak evidence, or the experiment wasn't well designed, test the same hypothesis again: a new experiment with stronger evidence, or the same experiment improved.
2. If strong: did you run other experiments for this hypothesis? If not, stay with the hypothesis and run a new experiment with even stronger evidence. Don't give up on the hypothesis yet.
3. If you ran other experiments and they **supported** the hypothesis, and this is the first refuting result, follow the same path: test again with a new experiment producing stronger evidence.
4. If you ran several experiments and previous ones **also** didn't support it, revisit the design of your business idea: start from scratch or update it to reflect that the hypothesis isn't supported. This creates new hypotheses you will eventually have to test.

**When to kill an idea.** You prioritized hypotheses by how much risk they represent, testing first what is most likely to kill the idea. So if you are very confident (tested with increasingly stronger evidence) that one of your highest-risk hypotheses shows the idea doesn't work in reality, you may have to kill it, unless you can pivot it into, say, a different customer segment, value proposition or pricing model. The more high-risk hypotheses you've invalidated with high confidence, the clearer it is that the idea is dead. That isn't necessarily bad: you may have saved your company thousands of dollars before investing in an idea that doesn't work.

**Example (LifeTree Bank).** Interviews supported the hypothesis; the A/B test also supported it with stronger evidence, but they couldn't be very confident yet. The third experiment, the mall prototype, drew nobody: strong evidence that did not support the hypothesis. Because earlier experiments had supported it, it's worth running another experiment for the same hypothesis. They ran a slightly altered experiment: invites only to domestic students, and the prototype pop-up store on a campus instead of a mall. This should produce very strong evidence because it is much more focused.

### Action When Insights Are Unclear

**What it means.** Observations that failed the success criteria by a small margin, data you can't make sense of, or mixed qualitative data. Something isn't adding up the way you imagined, so you continue testing the same hypothesis before making any decision.

**How to do it:**

1. Check the quality of your experiment design to rule out inconsistencies: Did you set up the experiment correctly? Did you read the data the right way? Is the artifact well crafted? Did you test with the right audience? Is the data set large enough?
2. If you found a design weakness, run the same, improved experiment again for clearer data.
3. If the experiment was well designed, choose a new experiment for the same hypothesis to collect further evidence. Or simply rerun the same experiment to make sure the data is representative.

**Options for inconclusive interview data (common with qualitative data):**

- Run the exact same interview with more people to increase sample size and confidence.
- Run it again with a more specific audience (a slight improvement). Sharpen your questions or home in on the contradictory points you heard. In customer discovery interviews especially, dial into your segment and focus on no more than three specific jobs, pains or gains.
- Try a new experiment to corroborate findings: for example, search discussion forums to identify unmet customer needs, or do a customer support analysis to understand why customers behave a certain way.

**Example (LifeTree Bank).** The A/B test after the interviews was confusing: unusually high conversion rates and no clear preference. Examining the artifact (the two A/B sites), they found analytics set up incorrectly in the back end. They improved and reran the experiment; with data collection set up properly, the data supported the hypothesis, and the resulting email list enabled the third experiment (the mall prototype).

### Action When You Have a New Insight

**What it means.** Treat a new insight like a newly discovered assumption. You didn't know it before, and it might change your idea substantially.

**How to decide:**

1. How strong is the evidence behind the insight? Rule of thumb: formulate a new hypothesis from the insight and run an experiment to see whether you can reproduce it. If the evidence supports the new hypothesis, switch to design and update your Business Model Canvas and Value Proposition Canvas.
2. If the insight already rests on strong evidence (for example, an experiment that demonstrated people's behavior), jump directly to design and update your business model and value proposition.
3. Updates may be substantial: a new pain to address in the value proposition, or something from the business model environment that changes the design. Updated designs create new hypotheses that you must prioritize against existing ones and test.

**Example (LifeTree Bank).** The first interviews revealed that 42% of interviewees value a personal relationship in advisory situations as very important. Knowing this was weak evidence, they formulated it as a new hypothesis and ran a second series of interviews to learn about this potential customer gain, which mattered because it affects their automation plans. Metric: how many people prefer a personal relationship over an automated one in advisory situations (for example, mortgages or financial planning). Criteria: at least 60% indicate a preference, a threshold they just met, so evidence supported the hypothesis. They needed stronger evidence before changing the design, so they ran a "day in the life" experiment, observing a number of Generation Z students and young professionals as they went about their everyday banking needs. Only once this produced stronger evidence did LifeTree Bank add the new gain to the Generation Z customer profile. Now they must rethink how this gain changes the value proposition, because it's unclear whether the current idea sufficiently addresses it.

### Practice: When a Roadblock Appears (Owlet and the FDA)

**Situation.** Owlet had evidence its idea might become a promising business, but learned (later in the game than they would have liked) that the baby alarm needed U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) clearance. The first person they talked to said it would cost over $1 million. They talked to 16 professionals in the space, including the labs they'd need to work through, and learned the range: minimum $120K and 9 months, maximum $200K and 13 months total time with clearance. That validation cost $200 and took 3 weeks.

**Which action?** Move to design and update the business model and value proposition (pivot). It makes sense to pivot to get a less risky product into the market in a shorter time. The validation was bittersweet: was $200,000, 13 months and all that liability really "running lean" before a product reached customers? What was holding them up was the alarm, because alarm plus pulse oximetry requires FDA clearance.

**Practice directions (20 minutes).** How could Owlet pivot its business model without the alarm? Which new customer segment could it target, and which new value proposition would address it? Update Owlet's Business Model Canvas and Value Proposition Canvas and write the new hypothesis to test.

**What Owlet did.** They scratched the alarm and kept the same product, with all its data possibilities, as an "infant health tracker." This was completely unvalidated, so they tested: "We believe that parents are ready to adopt and buy a wireless baby health tracker without alarm." They took clipboards to retail locations and interviewed 81 people in person, handing them four pieces of paper and asking "Which one would you buy if you had to?": the Owlet tracker versus three other product types at the same price points. Metric: percentage choosing the no-alarm product. Result: 20% chose Owlet's product. Hypothesis validated.

**New insight: two segments.** Customers were not one size fits all. On one side, a "hippie type mom" who is much less worried; on the other, the worried mom. Less worried parents said, over and over, "I wouldn't want an alarm on this. This would drive me crazy." The alarm version wouldn't even capture that part of the market. The Value Proposition Canvas for worried parents and the baby alarm stays the same. The new profile for less worried parents drops SIDS as a pain, replacing it with a light worry about baby sleep, so the baby alarm is removed from the pain relievers while still addressing their jobs, pains and gains. The decision: two value propositions, the infant health tracker for less worried parents and the baby alarm for worried parents. Two products that don't cannibalize each other because they serve different segments. Starting with the tracker means a completely separate market and a much less risky start; the alarm launches later, after learning from and avoiding some risk with the first product. Less worried parents remain a new insight worth further testing to strengthen the evidence. Cost: nothing; time: about three weeks.

## 10. Managing Testing and Measuring Progress

### Measure Progress by Risk Reduced, Not Work Done

**What it is.** On the journey from unproven idea to validated business model, you start with high uncertainty, and it's the innovator's responsibility to reduce that risk. Progress means risk reduced, not activity.

**Why velocity misleads.** Reducing risk is not counting experiments run, tasks completed, Trello cards moved or interviews conducted. Testing is not about velocity, a measure of how much work a team handles in a period. Teams often mistake velocity for progress (how much risk was reduced), but there is no direct correlation between the number of experiments and risk reduced. What matters is the amount of evidence gathered for each hypothesis, how strong it is, and what it tells you.

**How to measure it.** Each building block of your Business Model Canvas and Value Proposition Canvas is an area of risk with hypotheses to validate or invalidate. Your job is to derisk each block. Group them into the four risk categories. Each represents roughly 25% of total risk, and you make progress by reducing risk in each. The more hypotheses you validate per category, the more progress towards a validated business case.

**Caveat: 25% is a maximum, not an absolute.** Ideas have different starting points. If your idea targets an existing customer segment, you already know something, so desirability risk is lower and you've already made some progress. If your idea is a new product with new key resources, feasibility risk is at its maximum of 25%, meaning no feasibility progress yet.

### The Progress Board

**What it is.** A board to structure, manage and track experiments and measure progress. You won't run one or two experiments but many, often with multiple team members at once, and teams quickly lose track of who's doing what and how much risk has been reduced.

**Why it works.** It is built on Kanban, a popular framework used to implement agile software development, which helps manage flow and keep an overview of workload now and into the near future. Kanban usually has backlog, to do, doing and done columns. The Progress Board iterates "to do" into experiment design, "doing" into in progress, and "done" into learn. Because all experiments are visible, team members can see the state of every piece of work at any time. Overlaid with the testing process, it aligns perfectly: hypotheses in the first column, experiments in backlog and design, evidence collected while in progress, insights in learn, and the four actions in decide.

**Columns and how to use them:**

1. **Business Hypotheses.** List the hypotheses that need to be true for your idea to work. From your prioritized list, add the highest-risk hypotheses that could most likely kill your business idea, one sticky note each. These are tested first. All experiments are organized around hypotheses, because that's what you ultimately test.
2. **Experiment: Backlog.** The experiments you intend to run, created for your most critical hypotheses and designed with the Test Card, listed in order of priority.
3. **Experiment: Design.** Experiments being planned, designed or built: those you decided to do now. Assign a person, start designing the artifact, recruit users, book rooms, buy tickets, and organize everything to make it happen. Depending on team size, run several simultaneously.
4. **Experiment: In Progress.** Experiments currently running and collecting data. Record the evidence as you go.
5. **Learn.** Experiments executed, with data collected, ready to be analyzed. Look at all the evidence to generate insights and document them with the Learning Card.
6. **Decide.** Take action:
   - **Evidence supports hypothesis:** continue testing to collect stronger evidence for the same hypothesis, or test the next critical hypothesis (take the next experiment from the backlog, assign a name, start testing).
   - **Evidence doesn't support hypothesis:** kill your idea, pivot your design, or continue testing to collect stronger evidence before deciding.
   - **New insights:** continue testing to collect stronger evidence, formulate a new hypothesis from the insight, or update (pivot) the design of your idea.
   - **Unclear insights:** design another experiment and learn more before deciding.
7. **Progress.** A bar from 0% (unproven idea) to 100% (validated business model), in steps of 20%, 40%, 60% and 80%. When evidence supports a hypothesis and you're ready to move on, record the progress here, then move to the next critical hypothesis.

### The Innovation Project Scorecard

**What it is.** A tool to assess whether you are making progress from initial idea to profitable business. To demonstrate progress, a team must show hard evidence from all four risk categories. The scorecard is fully aligned with the nine building blocks of the Business Model Canvas.

**Risk Reduction scoring (0 to 10 per statement):** 0 = there is no evidence at all (no experiments yet); 5 = there is evidence from one experiment; 10 = there is evidence from several experiments (with increasingly stronger evidence). Scale labels: unclear, somewhat clear, some evidence, good evidence, strong evidence.

- **Desirability**
  - Customer segment: Our critical customer segments have the jobs, pains and gains relevant for selling our value proposition.
  - Value proposition: Our value proposition resonates with our critical customer segments.
  - Channels: We have found the best channel(s) to reach and acquire our critical customer segments.
  - Customer relationship: We have developed the right relationships to retain customers and repeatedly earn from them.
- **Feasibility**
  - Key resources: We have the right technologies and resources to create our value proposition.
  - Key activities: We have the right capabilities to handle the most critical activities for creating our value proposition.
  - Key partners: We have found the right key partners who are willing to work with us to create and deliver our value proposition.
- **Viability**
  - Revenues: We know how much our customers are willing to pay us and how they will pay.
  - Costs: We know our costs for creating and delivering the value proposition.
- **Adaptability**
  - Industry forces: We understand the competitors and emerging players in our markets.
  - Market forces: We understand the critical issues and shifts in our addressable market and its economic value.
  - Key trends: We understand the key technology, regulatory, cultural and societal trends that affect our business model.
  - Macroeconomic forces: We understand the macroeconomic and infrastructure factors that affect our business model.

**Strategic Fit (0 to 10):** 0 = no alignment; 5 = some alignment; 10 = strong alignment (scale: no, little, potential for, good, strong alignment). For established companies:

- Vision/culture/image: Our value proposition and business model are aligned to our company's vision, culture and image.
- Innovation guidance: Our value proposition and business model are aligned to our company's innovation guidance.
- Leadership support: Our value proposition and business model have support from key sponsor(s).

Startups check instead whether the idea corresponds with their vision and values.

**Opportunity (0 to 10): "We understand the financial potential of our idea."** 0 = value created under $100K; 5 = value created $10M+; 10 = value created $100M+. Scale: little or unknown value; moderate (potential) value (e.g. to a local team); some (potential) value (e.g. to a country); good (potential) value (e.g. to a region); strong (potential) value (e.g. across the globe). The more leaders and teams know about an idea's value, the better their decisions about whether to scale or stop it.

**How scores evolve.** At the start you have little evidence and will score yourself low. As you run more experiments with increasingly stronger evidence, scores should rise in all four risk categories.

**Who uses it and when:**

- **Teams** evaluating their own progress during a sprint or weekly stand-up: are they moving the needle?
- **Coaches and consultants** benchmarking a project at any time. Each project has a unique pattern of evidence per risk category, so the scorecard helps decide what to do next.
- **Innovation teams pitching to leaders for investment,** so a fair decision is reached that isn't based on whether leaders like or dislike the idea.
- **Investors** deciding whether an idea is worth investing in, based on evidence rather than opinion or flashy pitches.

### Progress in Practice: Scoring LifeTree Bank

**Starting point.** After three months of experiments, LifeTree Bank paused to assess progress, focusing not on the idea but on the evidence collected to derisk it. They had mapped the business model, extracted and prioritized hypotheses, and done a first pass on the scorecard. As a new business model and value proposition for a new audience, they operated from many unknowns and scored low, but not zero: reusing existing banking products gave slightly higher scores in key resources and revenues.

**Hypotheses put on the Progress Board:** "Generation Z prefers efficiency and mobility over the traditional banking experience"; "We can save $500 million over three years by switching from retail branches to pop-up stores"; "Generation Z wants to discuss important investments face to face." Each got a Test Card, an owner, evidence and a Learning Card.

**What they learned:**

- **Customer segment hypothesis.** Interviews with 50 people: 72% preferred banking anywhere and anytime (supported). A/B test between signing up for a physical branch or a pop-up store: 6.2% preferred the pop-up version (supported). Mall prototype from the email list: nobody showed up. Altered and rerun with invites only to domestic students and the pop-up on a campus: 6.5% of invited students visited the prototype (validated).
- **Customer relationship hypothesis** (face-to-face investment discussions). Only one experiment so far: web traffic analysis of existing Generation Z customers' behavior. Only certain investment conversations were preferred in person. Insights partially unclear, partially supporting; more experiments needed, perhaps starting by breaking the hypothesis down into the various investment conversations.
- **Cost structure hypothesis** ($500 million over three years). One experiment: interviews with 25 expert stakeholders about cost structures and the impact of the changes. Savings of $450 million were more realistic. Not exactly invalidated, since that's still a large sum, but more testing is needed for a clear number. (The walkthrough transcript says "$550 million" once and "$450 million" immediately after; the presentation of the same case says $450 million.)

**Scores if they stopped here:**

- Customer segment: 10 (several experiments, increasingly stronger evidence).
- Value proposition: 5 (some evidence from the pop-up store).
- Channels and customer relationships: between 0 and 5 (experiments run, but evidence not yet telling or fully supporting).
- Revenues: 5 (mostly existing banking products, so existing evidence). Note: existing evidence from previous businesses can earn a 5 without running an experiment.

Overall, some progress backed by evidence, but a long way to go before a validated business idea.

## 11. Presenting Your Tested Idea

### Presentation Structure: Three Chunks

**What it is.** When you pitch a tested innovation project (for example, to senior management asking for funding to keep going), decision makers act as gatekeepers and use the Innovation Project Scorecard to judge whether the idea is worth investing in. So don't focus on the idea itself: focus on the evidence your team collected to derisk it, and cover every point of the scorecard.

**Why it works.** We no longer just have conversations about ideas. We have conversations about the experiments and evidence that support an idea and the journey to get there. Evidence is proof the idea might actually work, and it shows you did the work.

**Chunk 1: Hook and big-picture idea (the first 1 to 2 minutes, the elevator pitch).** These minutes give you the legitimacy to keep speaking and capture attention.

1. **Opportunity or problem statement.** Show that you identified a customer job, pain or gain that isn't addressed today. This is your opening.
2. **Your solution,** the idea in a nutshell. If you have a picture, mock-up or MVP, show it now.
3. **Testing overview.** How much time you spent testing, what kinds of experiments, what data, all at a high level. Show a list of everything you did to get where you are today.
4. **Your most compelling evidence.** The single most interesting piece that shows you're on a great track.

Scorecard coverage: the opportunity/problem statement and the idea address strategic fit (vision, culture and image fit; innovation guidance). The solution can address the scorecard's opportunity (financial potential). If you can put an estimate on the value you create, share it early to capture the audience.

**Chunk 2: Experiments, evidence and insights (the heart of the presentation).** This addresses the scorecard's middle section: evidence reducing desirability, feasibility, viability and adaptability risk. It's fine if time or resources only let you test some desirability and viability; highlight later what still needs testing. That shows you were focused, realistic and aware of what remains.

1. Make explicit what you tested: the riskiest hypotheses you went out to test.
2. Rule of thumb: for each hypothesis, show the one experiment you learned the most from. Don't overburden the audience with every experiment.
3. Describe that experiment, the evidence gained, and whether it supported the hypothesis.
4. Show photos of experiments, mock-ups and MVPs you created.
5. Wrap up: which hypotheses you validated, what progress you've made, and where more work is needed.

**Chunk 3: Conclusions and next steps.**

1. Remind the audience of the big idea: why the organization should pursue it and why it's worthwhile.
2. Repeat your most compelling evidence. Good things need repeating.
3. Make explicit what you haven't tested yet.
4. The ask: zoom back to the big picture and explain what resources (further funding or time) are needed to finish the remaining work, and how you plan to spend that money and time.

### Presentation Best Practices

**Focus on evidence, not on why the idea is great.** Beginners in particular make this mistake because they don't understand the power of their evidence. Show how much progress you've made towards a validated business model, backed by solid data. Nothing is more convincing.

**Explain your decision logic, don't just list experiments.** A list of experiments and their evidence loses the audience in a maze and hides your decision making. Explain step by step how you used experiments, evidence and insights to develop the idea.

**No blah blah blah; tell a story.** Nervous or unrehearsed presenters drift into talking about things that don't matter to the audience in too much detail. (Strategyzer uses a "blah blah card" in workshops that the audience holds up when things get boring.) Instead, captivate with the story of where you started, where you are today, and what you still need to do. Include an interesting personal story or ask a question that gets the light bulbs going. Was there a moment your idea got killed by an invalidated hypothesis? How did you solve it? What was the climax of your research?

**Don't commit cognitive murder.** Revealing too much at once is one of the biggest mistakes. Passionate Business Model Canvas users often show an entire canvas at once and then explain it, but brains can't handle that density and shut off. Instead:

- Use short, descriptive words in a big font (like a 30 point font) and repeat them throughout. They become hooks for the audience's brains, so pick a few purposeful, catchy words.
- Make it visual and engaging: videos, quotes, pictures of your MVPs, pictures of conversations with customers. People learn best when what they hear is supported by visuals.
- Build slides up over time, revealing one piece at a time, so what you say is always supported by what they see.

**Practice.** Not practicing is one of the worst mistakes: you can't anticipate questions and look less confident reading from the screen. Prepared presenters exude confidence, which strongly affects how a judging panel sees the idea. Videotape yourself until you can deliver the pitch by heart, without hesitation and full of confidence.

**Above all:** the purpose of the presentation is to present evidence that your business idea can work.

### Adapting to Circumstances and What Else to Prepare

Who you pitch to determines length and structure. The Strategyzer template (available in Keynote and PowerPoint) is designed for a 10 to 15 minute presentation at a structured networking event or competition, but sometimes you only get a couple of minutes with a major angel investor you meet at the airport. Depending on the setting, you may be asked for:

- **Roadmap:** how you will use the money, major milestones in the next 3 to 12 months, time to market, etc.
- **Financial documents:** revenue projections, operational expenses, cash flow, etc.
- **Research data:** competitors, market forces, etc.
- **Technology stack** (if you have one).

### Example Script: LifeTree Bank's Pitch

What follows is the full sample presentation, mapped to the three chunks.

**Hook.** "Hi. We are the innovation team of LifeTree Bank. In our team, we have Pete, Stephanie, Jordan, Carol and Elliot. Today, we want to show you how our new lifestyle bank concept will help us better serve our Generation Z customers. With Generation Z, we have a new audience that is used to being able to access everything on their phone 24/7. This is a generation that grew up with the Internet and smartphone, and as a bank, we need to be able to respond to this adequately. And this is our dilemma. We know that our current banking experience is kind of dated and does not serve the needs of our newest emerging customer base. So we asked ourselves, how can we balance these needs and serve them better with a new banking concept? So instead of having this, why can't we have more of this? Because we believe that we need to go where our customers are with our mobile pop-up stores. For Generation Z, that means coworking spaces, school campuses, coffee shops and entertainment venues. And we married that with our growing digital platform for a more holistic experience. Not only will we be able to learn more about our customers, it will also enable us to provide a more personal experience. In a nutshell, our lifestyle bank concept helps Generation Z customers who want to manage their money and plan for the future by delivering a more flexible, convenient and more personal banking experience."

**Most compelling evidence up front.** "We learned that we can save our parent company up to $450 million over three years, and that Generation Z customers are twice more likely to sign up for an account or investment product with our new lifestyle bank concept. So let us show you how we got there."

**Business model and what we already knew.** For the digital bank: an automated relationship relying heavily on data collection for personalized recommendations; a strong lever is creating as much traffic as possible for the digital platform, a key resource and activity delivered with technology vendor partners. Banking products (account types, investment products) stay similar, so the revenue model barely changes. Pop-up stores replace branches as channels for the face-to-face relationship, requiring a new in-store experience and resources, plus partners such as coworking spaces or college campuses for temporary pop-ups. Costs piggyback on the parent company's operations. Already known: revenue model, and experience with stores and the digital platform. Big unknowns concern the customer-facing experience: are people interested in pop-up stores, is that the right channel for face-to-face relationships, and above all, do we truly understand the segment's jobs, pains and gains? Also: who will partner with us for pop-up stores, and how will the changes affect costs?

**Evidence, hypothesis 1: Generation Z prefers efficiency and mobility over the traditional banking experience.** "We spoke to 50 people who fall into the Generation Z customer segment. We ran an A/B test with 5,000 customers, and we had a real-life prototype of a pop-up store, even though it only consisted of a table with some cloth, on a university campus with 65 Generation Z students. With increasingly stronger evidence, we learned that indeed Generation Z prefers efficiency and mobility, but also that we need to be very selective about where we place our pop-up stores. When we initially ran that experiment in a shopping mall, nobody showed up. But once we segmented our customers, narrowed them down to students and organized the pop-up store on a campus, that truly changed our story. Generation Z students who visited our real-life prototype on campus were twice more likely to sign up for an account or investment product compared to the average numbers from traditional bank branches. They said things like 'Waiting in line is such a waste of time in today's age' or 'I wish banks were more like food trucks.' That last comment we found really interesting, so one of our next experiments will be a pop-up store, probably at a music festival."

**Evidence, hypothesis 2: not everything can be automated; some conversations must happen in person.** "We did a web traffic analysis of 4,000 customers to analyze the behavior of existing Generation Z customers on our website. We learned that only certain investment conversations were preferred in person: the higher the loan, the more important it is to conduct conversations in person. But some loans can easily be automated, which will save us money and provide valuable customer data. We still need more testing to identify which conversations to have face to face and which to automate."

**Evidence, hypothesis 3: we can save up to $500 million by switching from retail branches to pop-up stores and running more automated services.** "We interviewed 25 expert stakeholders about our cost structures and the impact of our suggested changes. Our learning was that $450 million in savings over the next three years was more realistic. Not exactly invalidated, since that's still a large sum, but here too we need more testing for stronger evidence."

**What's still untested.** Whether schools, coworking spaces and events like music festivals will partner for pop-up stores; whether the idea complies with banking regulations around the world; and, once those are validated, more testing of the value proposition and customer relationships across digital and face-to-face channels.

**The ask.** "Overall, we had a promising start and would like to continue testing our idea. For that, we need a total of $35,000 so we can run more interviews, conduct more research and run another prototype of our pop-up store. We also need two more months full time for two key team members, with the other three members in a supporting role at 20%. Thank you for your support in our lifestyle banking concept that could help us build better relationships with our Generation Z customers and save our bank up to $450 million in the next three years."

### Example: Owlet's Pitch as a Story

Owlet's founders pitched their tested idea as a chronological story of hypotheses, experiments, evidence, cost and time per step, ending with what remains and a launch plan (see Section 13 for the full arc). Its strengths as a model pitch: a personal origin story (a pain experienced firsthand in the hospital, and a founder's family loss of a child to respiratory failure); each step framed as a question (will someone buy it? how will we reach customers? could we build it? do we need FDA clearance?); evidence with cost and time for every experiment; honest admission of being wrong ("we were completely wrong", "surveys can also lie if you don't word it correctly"); a clear remaining-risk list; and a closing personal note ("I'm a dad, and I can tell you firsthand, having kids is the most life changing amazing experience you can have. And at Owlet, we really hope to keep it that way").

## 12. Case Study: LifeTree Bank (Full Arc)

### The Idea

LifeTree Bank is a brick-and-mortar retail bank that has been around for many decades. Its corporate innovation team's mandate from senior management: redefine the banking experience for Generation Z to attract and keep customers of this generation. The idea: a new lifestyle bank that marries digital and physical into a holistic experience.

**If the idea were an ad:** "LifeTree Bank is now mobile, just like you. Not only can you perform your most important banking needs from your mobile phone, but we also come to where you are with our mobile pop-up stores. So next time you see us on campus, at your favorite coffee shop or a coworking space, come say hi and open a free all-inclusive account now with LifeTree Bank, mobile just like you."

### Value Proposition Canvas

- **Customer jobs:** managing everyday money needs; planning for the future.
- **Pain:** the traditional retail experience in branches is poor and needs a refresh.
- **Gains:** efficiency and mobility; they are used to accessing everything on their phone 24/7.
- **Gain creator:** a digital bank that moves traditional banking activities from branches and call centers online, creating hyperconvenience for customers and reducing the bank's operational costs.
- **Pain reliever:** a new pop-up store concept that creates a different personal experience when people need advice.
- **Products and services:** banking products stay similar (checking and savings accounts, investment vehicles like retirement and mortgages), since people still rely on them; the bank plans to reuse much of its existing offering.

### Business Model

An automated customer relationship relying heavily on data collection for personalized recommendations; a strong lever is maximizing traffic to the digital platform, a key resource and activity delivered with technology vendor partners. Revenue model barely changes because the products are the same. Pop-up stores replace branches as channels for face-to-face relationships, requiring a new in-store experience and resources, and partners such as coworking spaces or college campuses. Costs piggyback on much of the parent company's existing operations.

### Hypotheses Extracted and Prioritized

- Desirability: Generation Z prefers efficiency and mobility over traditional banking experiences; automations will increase conversion rates; Generation Z will spend more time in temporary pop-up stores than retail branches; Generation Z wants to discuss important investments face to face.
- Feasibility: schools and coworking spaces will partner with us; we can build automations with our existing systems; we can partner with our existing software vendors.
- Viability: we can save $500 million over three years by switching from retail branches to pop-up stores; our digital platform will save $200 million in call centers over three years; young adults 18-24 will pay $5 per month for an all-inclusive account.
- Adaptability: our idea complies with banking regulations in all countries the bank operates in (placed right at the top of the riskiest quadrant).

Desirability is high risk (new model, new segment); feasibility is lower risk (existing infrastructure). Desirability and adaptability can be tested in parallel.

### Experiment Sequence for the Riskiest Hypothesis

Hypothesis: "We believe that young adults between 18 and 24 will indicate a preference for access to banking anywhere and anytime in comparison to going to traditional bank branches."

| # | Experiment | Criteria | Result | Insight and action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Customer discovery interviews, 50 people | At least (more than) 60% prefer | 72% prefer; new insight: 42% value a personal adviser relationship; 21 of 50 want a physical bank where they spend time | Supports (weak). Run an A/B test and build an email list. New insight becomes a new hypothesis |
| 2 | A/B test, sign-up for physical branch vs. pop-up store | Pop-up version at least 3% conversion and 20% higher than branch | 12.5% vs. 12.6%, unusually high, no preference | Unclear. Back-end analytics set up wrong; improve and rerun |
| 2b | A/B test rerun (5,000 customers per the pitch) | Same | 6.2% preferred the pop-up version | Supports. Email list enables prototype |
| 3 | Real-life prototype in a shopping mall (table with a bit of branding) | 5% of email list show up | Nobody showed up | Doesn't support. Earlier experiments supported, so test again with a more focused design |
| 4 | Pop-up prototype on a university campus, invites to domestic students only | Not stated | 6.5% of invited students visited (65 students); visitors twice as likely to sign up for an account or investment product vs. branch averages | Validated |

### Other Hypotheses Tested

- **Personal relationship (new insight from 42%).** Second interview series: at least 60% prefer a personal relationship in advisory situations (mortgages, financial planning), just met. Then a day-in-the-life observation of Generation Z students and young professionals. Only after that stronger evidence was the gain added to the customer profile.
- **Face-to-face investment conversations.** Web traffic analysis (4,000 customers per the pitch): only certain conversations preferred in person; the higher the loan, the more important in person; some loans can be automated. Partially unclear, partially supporting.
- **Cost savings.** 25 expert stakeholder interviews: $450 million over three years more realistic than $500 million.
- **Car loan automation (A/B).** Two pages, 50/50 split: phone appointment vs. qualification quiz; benchmark 2.5% to 3%; baseline 2.25%; +10% target equals 2.475%; +20% (chosen, given early adopters) equals 2.7%. Part of an overarching goal of moving at least 20% of manually processed business from call centers to automation.
- **Investment learning (search trend analysis).** YouTube searches by 18-24 year olds; criteria over 20,000 domestic searches; "investment for beginners" led at 24k.

### Scorecard After Three Months and the Ask

Customer segment 10; value proposition 5; channels and relationships 0 to 5; revenues 5; costs 5; feasibility largely untouched. Ask: $35,000, two more months full time for two team members, three others at 20%. Still to test: partnerships (schools, coworking spaces, music festivals), regulatory compliance worldwide, digital and face-to-face channels.

## 13. Case Study: Owlet (Full Arc)

### Background

Owlet is a bootie (smart sock) for babies that informs parents of vital health signs. It made headlines in 2015 when it raised a $7 million A round, after winning the 2013 International Business Model Competition (the first and largest Lean Startup competition in the world). The product pitch: "Is the baby sleeping peacefully or is something wrong? It's a question every new parent can relate to. That's why we created the Owlet Smart Sock." Parents enter basic information in the baby's profile; the sock silently checks on the baby during sleep; parents see everything on their phone wherever they are; Owlet alerts only if there seems to be a problem with the baby's heart rate or oxygen levels. It uses pulse oximetry, a proven safe technology doctors and nurses have used for decades, and its base station can alert parents even if Wi-Fi and cell service go down. "It's all about giving you a little more rest and a little less to worry about."

### Iteration 1: Wireless Pulse Oximetry for Hospitals

**Origin.** Tanner, part of the Owlet team, worked in a hospital with pulse oximetry (the little red light put on your finger to monitor vital signs). Day in, day out, he found a pain: the cord. Patients didn't like it, nurses didn't like it; it got tangled and got in the way. He assumed other nurses would feel the same and thought: what if we had a wireless pulse oximeter, used by nurses and sold through hospitals?

**Experiments.** Interviews with 58 nurses: 93% wanted it over the existing technology (criteria: more than 60% positive feedback). Hospital administrators told a very different story: the cord was not a pain for them. It was about money, the device wasn't cost effective, and they said no (0% ready to pay more for wireless). Lesson: your user is not always your customer. Nurses loved it but had no purchasing power, so the hypothesis went from "yellow" back to "red," unvalidated. Cost: nothing; time: about one week.

### Iteration 2: Baby Alarm for Parents (Desirability)

**Pivot.** Kurt Workman had experienced firsthand in his family the pain of a child lost to respiratory failure. Idea: use the same technology in an in-home setting for infants, monitoring vital signs (blood oxygen levels, heart rate) and alerting parents in an emergency during the night, giving peace of mind. SIDS (sudden infant death syndrome) is the number one cause of infant death in the United States and is often respiratory related, which greatly increases parents' anxiety. With 4 million babies born in the US every year, they assumed it was a big pain.

**Assumed solution.** Technology in a smart anklet worn by the baby, wirelessly sending data to a relay station able to alert and alarm parents in an emergency, and pushing the information to a smartphone for convenience. New value proposition canvas: products (baby alarm, pulse oximetry monitor), gain creators (wireless monitoring, smart bootie, mobile app), pain reliever (baby alarm); customer profile for parents: job (taking care of babies), pain (SIDS), gains (peace of mind, convenience).

**Two risks.** Market risk (will someone buy it?) and technology risk (can we build it?). They tackled market risk first because it was easier to get to and more important.

**Experiments:**

- **Survey/interviews with 105 mothers.** Average score of 8.5 out of 10 on wanting the product; 96% said they would use the technology (criteria: more than 75% would adopt and buy a wireless baby alarm). Responses like "This is awesome, I want to buy one now, take my money now and start selling it." Very early validation, but pointed in the right direction.
- **Lo-fi MVP: website and video** (built as a class project). The video was "smoke and mirrors" showing what the product could be. It leaked and was picked up by 40 news agencies in 13 countries. Results: 17,000 views, 5,500 Facebook shares, 500 positive comments (criteria: more than 100 positive comments), and over 500 emails, mostly parents begging for the product ("We want the peace of mind you provide. We're not sleeping well at night. We're willing to buy a prototype if that's all you have"). Fifteen distributors worldwide reached out. One, from a major competitor, was willing to drop that account, worth $2 million in revenue per year, to pick up Owlet's. The fastest-moving one wanted $50,000 in presales to get into its market quickly. All this came from building virtually nothing. Cost: $220; time: about two weeks.

### Iteration 2: Channels and Pricing (Viability)

**Channel.** They first thought of hospitals, but after talking with pediatricians and mothers realized retail locations (such as Babies R Us) were the best place to sell.

**Cost and price.** Manufacturing would cost $40 to $60; to keep good margins, customers would have to pay around $200. Were they really creating that much value? They assumed they would have to rent the product. Hypothesis: "We believe that parents want to rent the product versus buy at a $200 sales price."

**Experiments (increasingly stronger evidence):**

1. **Survey of 105 mothers: "What would you expect to pay?"** Average $92. Supported the rent hypothesis, but weak evidence.
2. **Babies R Us and 33 other baby stores.** Babies R Us said there's a huge span of prices and the best-selling ones are in the $200 range. They didn't believe it, so they called 33 other baby stores, which said exactly the same: 71% of baby monitors purchased today are in the $200 to $250 price range. Slightly stronger evidence. "We were completely wrong."
3. **Two more surveys: "What's the maximum you would pay?"** Averages of $182 (139 mothers) and $214 (40 mothers). Very different results from the first survey. Lesson: surveys can also lie if you don't word them correctly.
4. **A/B price test on the website.** A mock sales page with a reserve button at different price points; people thought they were purchasing. This revealed demand elasticity and the demand curve: the profit-maximizing price was $299 (1,130 people, 15 days). Stronger evidence, assuming a well-crafted artifact with only the price changed between A and B.

**Result.** Proved wrong, luckily: they can sell (not rent) the product at $299. Cost: $30; time: three weeks. A textbook example of multiple experiments for the same hypothesis with increasingly stronger evidence.

### Iteration 2: Feasibility

**Wireless transmission** (tested first because it was easier): a proof of concept that did everything they wanted, even though it looked "kind of ugly." Cost: $300; time: 7 weeks. (Confidence quiz verdict: a single lab prototype with medium evidence and one data point means "not really confident"; a single-feature interaction MVP used by real customers would be stronger.)

**Pulse oximetry specific to infants.** They went back to the hospital, interviewed nurses and watched how they used current pulse oximeters, and realized their design wouldn't work. For technical reasons they switched to a more sock-like design, tested with over 120 hours of testing in 26 different homes and 7 overnight tests with parents. With the new design, they could build it. Cost: $500; time: 12 weeks.

### Iteration 3: The FDA Roadblock and the Pivot

**Regulation.** Learned later than they wished: the product needed FDA clearance. The first person said over $1 million. After talking to 16 professionals and the labs: minimum $120K and 9 months, maximum $200K and 13 months. Cost of validating: $200; time: 3 weeks. Bittersweet: $200,000, 13 months and all that liability wasn't running lean. The alarm was the blocker (alarm plus pulse oximetry requires FDA clearance).

**Pivot.** Scratch the alarm, keep the product and its data, call it an infant health tracker. Hypothesis: "We believe that parents are ready to adopt and buy a wireless baby health tracker without alarm." In-person interviews with 81 people at retail locations, choosing among four pieces of paper (Owlet plus three competing product types at the same price points): 20% chose Owlet. New insight: two segments, worried parents (baby alarm) and less worried parents (health tracker; "I wouldn't want an alarm on this. This would drive me crazy"). No cannibalization, lower-risk start. Cost: nothing; time: about three weeks.

### Where Owlet Ended Up

In total, just under or just over $1,000 and 24 weeks, with hundreds of one-on-one interviews and fake sales, not just surveys. Plan: launch that summer with the non-alarm health tracker (no FDA needed, lower risk), then launch the flagship alarm product about seven months later. Still to validate: other subscription-like revenue streams (and maybe an app), and key partners in operations with retail experience. Scorecard opportunity estimate: 4 million US births per year at 1% adoption is roughly $12 million per year for the US alone.

## 14. Tool Templates (Field by Field)

### Test Card

- Header: Test Name; Assigned to; Deadline; Duration.
- Step 1, Hypothesis: "We believe that..." Rating: Critical.
- Step 2, Test: "To verify that, we will..." Ratings: Test Cost; Data Reliability.
- Step 3, Metric: "And measure..." Rating: Time Required.
- Step 4, Criteria: "We are right if..."

### Learning Card

- Header: Insight Name; Person Responsible; Date of Learning.
- Step 1, Hypothesis: "We believed that..."
- Step 2, Observation: "We observed..." Rating: Data Reliability.
- Step 3, Learnings and insights: "From that we learned that..." Rating: Action Required.
- Step 4, Decisions and actions: "Therefore, we will..."

### Interview Notes Template

- Customer name
- Hypothesis
- Quotes supporting hypothesis
- Quotes not supporting hypothesis
- New insights
- Jobs, pains and gains

### Progress Board

Business Hypotheses | Experiment: Backlog | Experiment: Design | Experiment: In Progress | Learn | Decide (supports / doesn't support / new insights / unclear) | Progress (unproven idea 0% to validated business model 100%).

### Innovation Project Scorecard

Header: Pitch; Completed by. Sections: Strategic Fit (vision/culture/image, innovation guidance, leadership support); Opportunity (financial potential); Risk Reduction (desirability: customer segment, value proposition, channels, customer relationship; feasibility: key resources, key activities, key partners; viability: revenues, costs; adaptability: industry forces, market forces, key trends, macroeconomic forces). Full statements and scales in Section 10.

### Business Model Canvas and Value Proposition Canvas (for sketching hypotheses)

- Business Model Canvas blocks: Key Partners, Key Activities, Key Resources, Value Propositions, Customer Relationships, Channels, Customer Segments, Cost Structure, Revenue Streams. Header fields: Designed for, Designed by, Date, Version.
- Value Proposition Canvas: Value Proposition side (Products and Services, Gain Creators, Pain Relievers) and Customer Segment side (Customer Jobs, Gains, Pains).
