## 1. Where Testing Fits: The Wedge

### The Ideation Sequence So Far

**What it is.** Testing is one step in a longer ideation process. Before it come three phases:

1. **Ideation:** decide which market you want to enter and what your goals are. Goals matter a lot here, because depending on your goals you will run different test processes and evaluate the results differently.
2. **Exploration:** research the market. Understand who the competitors are and how the market works.
3. **Definition:** create your wedge in the market.

The wedge has two parts. The first is the **vision**: what will happen in the future, and what your hypotheses are for how the market will change over time. The second is the **narrow spear tip**: your atomic unit of value, the specific wedge you use to get into the market. That spear tip is your go-to-market strategy, and it has two parts of its own: **testing** (making sure you have the right product for the market) and **traction** (getting it to spread, covered separately).

### Understanding Your Market Type

**What it is.** Steve Blank's classification of start-ups by the kind of market they enter (the four types of start-ups, based on market type):

1. **Existing Market:** a market with well-defined competitors and customers.
2. **Resegmented Market:** a market dominated by one or a few key players.
3. **New Market:** a market where competitors do not exist and it's very hard to define the customer.
4. **Clone Market:** a market where, due to geographical or cultural barriers, a business model can be cloned and transposed.

**Why it matters.** The market type changes the economics of almost everything downstream, so the same product needs a different plan depending on which of the four markets it enters. What the market type influences:

1. **Market.** Depending on the market type, market conditions change: market size, cost of entry, launch type, competitive barriers, positioning.
2. **Sales.** The market type affects sales processes: sales model, margins, sales cycle.
3. **Finance.** The market type influences ongoing capital and time to profitability.

**How to use it.** Classify your idea into one of the four types first, then plan your market sizing, positioning, sales model and funding needs for that type rather than borrowing assumptions from a different one.

### The Wedge Venn Diagram

**What it is.** The wedge, or atomic unit of value you deliver to a customer, sits at the intersection of three circles:

- **Market opportunity:** what your research tells you.
- **Business model:** how you are going to make money.
- **Customer insight:** what you know about your customer or your market that no one else knows, or that no one is properly executing on today.

**Why it works.** Once you have a market, you need to start creating a business around it. Depending on your skills and goals, plus market needs, plus customer needs, you can create a very different business. At this point in the process you must have a profit motive: it is not enough to see an opportunity for a product, you have to see the financial opportunity and map it to one of the business models, because you have to make money somehow.

**How to do it.**

1. Take the market opportunity from your research.
2. Map it against the list of startup business models (Section 2) and pick the one that fits the opportunity and your skills.
3. Add the customer insight that no one else has or executes on.
4. Find the point where all three overlap. That overlap is the wedge you will test.
5. From the wedge, move to testing (Section 3): "Once you have the Venn Diagram, you can now create your atomic unit of value (Testing)."

**Why the overlap matters.** Inference: a market opportunity without customer insight produces products nobody wants; customer insight without a business model produces something loved but unprofitable; a business model without a real opportunity has nothing to monetize. Only the overlap gives you an entry point sharp enough to break in. The customer insight side can be gathered as the jobs, pains and gains of the customer (see the Value Proposition Canvas in Section 11).

### The Bud Caddell Diagram

**What it is.** A Venn diagram that helps you cut through the noise of understanding whether a business idea has potential for you. It lets you assess whether you want to deal with a problem, whether you can do it well, and whether people are willing to pay you for it. Based on a few questions, you can understand whether you're ready to launch a successful business in the long run.

**How to read it.** Three circles:

- **What we do well** ∩ **What we want to do** (but not paid) → **Learn to monetize**.
- **What we do well** ∩ **What we can be paid to do** (but don't want to) → **Learn to say no**.
- **What we want to do** ∩ **What we can be paid to do** (but don't do well) → **Learn to do this better**.
- All three → **Hooray!**

### Niche Marketing Strategy to Scale

**What it is.** A niche marketing strategy looks at a subset of a market to build a business that reaches an audience where there is lower competition, strong demand and higher margins. A niche also lets you build an intimate relationship with that segment where larger brands can't.

**How to do it.** Look for the profitable niche at the intersection of **lower competition**, **strong demand** and **higher margins**. Win it, then expand outward from it (scaling up) into the larger market. Amazon's start in books (see the Amazon case in Section 12) is the model: begin from one relatively smaller market, dominate it, then move on.

## 2. Startup Business Models and Their Riskiest Hypotheses

### The Ten Startup Business Models

**What it is.** Ten business models probably encompass 99% of startups and technology companies (with "technology company" used liberally: a media newsletter business counts). Very few people are starting a genuinely new business model. The inventor of cryptocurrency created probably the newest business model in existence, and a thousand startups then tried to use it to succeed or fail. The goal here is not moonshots but making money and building a business, so you will almost always pick from this list.

| Model | Pricing model | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Enterprise | Single license per company | Docker, Cloudera, FireEye |
| Usage-Based | Cost per individual action on service | Google Maps API, Twilio, Sendgrid |
| SaaS | Subscription sold per user | DocuSign, Zapier, Salesforce |
| Services | Time of employees | TaskUs, Accenture, VaynerMedia |
| Transactional | % of transactions enabled by service | Stripe, PayPal, Eventbrite |
| Product (Hardware) | Sell your own product | GoPro, FitBit, Allbirds |
| Subscription | Subscription for service or software | Tinder, Birchbox, BlueApron |
| Marketplace | Connect buyers and sellers | Etsy, Kickstarter, AirBnB |
| (E-)Commerce | Offer a selection of products | Zappos, Honest Company |
| Ads | Advertisers buy special access | TikTok, Reddit, YouTube |

The slide's tagline for this table: "A rising tide lifts all ships."

**B2B versus B2C.** A second version of the same slide splits the list into segments, in descending order of value per customer: Enterprise, Usage-Based, SaaS and Services are **B2B**; Transactional sits on the **bridge between B2B and B2C**; Product (Hardware), Subscription, Marketplace, (E-)Commerce and Ads are **B2C**. Its caption: "As you get lower down this list, you are less willing to pay for customers and more interested in seeing them naturally jump and get addicted to the product."

### Ordering Models by Value Per Customer

**What it is.** The list is roughly ordered by how much each customer is worth, top to bottom. There is a lot of movement (Stripe makes far more from a single large contract such as Lyft than from its average customer, so the transactional model can climb higher), but the rough gradient holds.

**Why it matters.** Value per customer dictates how you can afford to acquire customers:

- **Top (Enterprise):** you sell into big corporations that pay $20,000 or $50,000. You can afford a lot of salespeople, sometimes two salespeople per client per year, or one salesperson closing five or ten deals a year. The value you give the customer must be so high it is worth tens of thousands of dollars, because selling to the enterprise is expensive.
- **Middle (SaaS through subscription, including products and marketplaces):** marketing-based businesses. Cost per customer is moderate. You usually have to be willing to afford marketing, or have a product so unique that it goes viral on the uniqueness of its value.
- **Bottom (e-commerce and ads):** in an ads business your average customer might be worth $1 a year or a few dollars a year. You cannot afford to do any marketing at all, so you may want a business that goes viral on its own.

### The Riskiest Hypotheses for Each Model

**What it is.** Almost every company within a given business model shares a common question. Your goal is to de-risk these as much as possible before you start.

| Model | Riskiest hypothesis #1 | Riskiest hypothesis #2 |
|---|---|---|
| Enterprise | Will they buy? | Will they pay enough for profit? |
| Usage-Based | Will they use? | At what volume? |
| SaaS | Does this meet a need? | Will they keep using? |
| Services | Can I sell? | Can I scale? |
| Transactional | Will they use? | At what volume? |
| Product (Hardware) | Will they buy? | At what volume? |
| Subscription | Will they buy? | Will they churn? |
| Marketplace | Will buyers want sellers' products? | Will buyers repeat purchase? |
| (E-)Commerce | Will they buy? | Will they repeat purchase? |
| Ads | Will they use? | Will they get addicted? |

**How to use it.** Find your model in the table, take hypothesis #1 as the first thing your MVP must prove, and hypothesis #2 as the second. Do not spend effort on anything else until these two are de-risked.

**Why "will they get addicted" for ads.** An ads business needs very high frequency: people must come back at least weekly. The most successful ad businesses (Facebook, TikTok) have people coming for hours per day. There is a balance: if you want $1 million rather than $100 billion, an ads business fed by search engine traffic can work, but you still ideally want people to come regularly so you are not dependent on Google traffic.

**The running theme.** Across models the riskiest assumptions come down to two factors: **will people buy** (does this meet a need, can I sell it) and **how many people will buy**. Inside both sits a third: **at what price, and is that price profitable**. Together these are the value equation of the business. During hypothesis testing figure out your potential value, how big the market might be, what price you can charge, whether people want it, and above all **who** wants it, because once you know who wants it you can work out how many of those people exist.

### Three Archetypes by Actors and Interactions

**What it is.** A second way to classify business models is by the number of actors and interactions. The more interactions, the more complex the model.

1. **Direct model (simplest, one actor):** the user is also the customer, or can become the customer. Example: Starbucks. You go into the coffee shop, you smell the coffee, if you like it you buy it. The user becomes the customer.
2. **Multi-sided model:** users and customers are different people. On Facebook the user side is "free" only in the sense that users do not pay directly. There is no such thing as a free user in a business model: users pay with a currency, which is attention. The data Facebook collects and packages for advertisers turns into money. The pattern applies anywhere a user side creates an asset that then gets monetized: data, user-generated content, and even nonprofits, which monetize impact. At the Red Cross, donors are the customers and the people served are the users.
3. **Marketplace model:** buyers and sellers come together to transact (Airbnb). The business makes money by taking a percentage of transactions as a commission or some other transactional fee.

### Business Models Are Always Evolving

A business model is never finished. Google's model shifts constantly, and publishers and the SEO world worry that Google may automate content generation or extract more and more content from websites, so the value proposition of such companies changes all the time. To read where a model will move, look at the key player around which it was built: if users get value from the information Google offers, Google will optimize for what users want rather than for publishers. Publishers are another key player, but users come first.

### Is the Business Model Itself What You Test?

Most of the time, no. Your business model is rarely the unique thing you need to test or the thing you are reinventing. Sometimes it is, but not most of the time. If the profit equation is a big risk in your business, focus a test on it; otherwise the MVP is about other risks.

(One source adds a strong counterweight: monetization is one of the riskiest building blocks and should be tested early rather than deferred. See "Test Monetization Early" in Section 3. The two positions fit together: do not test the model's novelty, but do test whether someone will pay.)

### Match the Model to Yourself, Not the Zeitgeist

A valuation "nirvana" of being valued on top line rather than EBITDA tempts founders toward recurring revenue, or bundles of recurring revenue streams (sometimes called "runnels"). The better rule: your business model should match what you are good at. If you are good at selling subscription software and like subscription businesses, great. But it is easy to get excited by the zeitgeist: when a big consumer business becomes hugely successful, tech investors pile into consumer; when consumer lulls, they pile into SaaS. Those are temporary enthusiasms. There are successful businesses in every model, so build one that is good for you.

## 3. Hypotheses: Find, Rank, Learn, Test

### Rank, Learn, Test

**What it is.** The most important step in the whole testing process. Once you have an idea and a model for how it makes money, write down the hypotheses that must be true for the business to succeed, then create an atomic unit of value that tests the riskiest of them.

**Why it works.** Business plans, canvases and pitches are full of hidden bets. If they stay unwritten they never get tested, and the thing most likely to blow up the business goes unexamined while you work on easy things.

**How to do it.**

1. **Rank:** write your highest-impact assumptions in rank order. Ask: what are the riskiest assumptions? What is most likely to blow up this business? What do I need to believe for this business to work?
2. **Learn:** learn what you can about each highest-impact assumption and come up with a "test" that lets you evaluate it and learn more.
3. **Test:** focus the test on an "atomic unit of value": the most specific spear point that provides all of the force necessary toward the value you think users really care about.

**Example.** A hot sauce e-commerce site. The thesis: hot sauce is a market where people want to buy different types, so the site lets people observe different options and choose the ones they like. Next step: list what would kill this business, rank those risks by impact, and design a wedge that tests the top one.

**Second example.** Inference (applying the method to Section 2): for a SaaS idea, the ranked list starts with "Does this meet a need?" then "Will they keep using?". The atomic unit of value is the single piece of the product that delivers the core benefit; the test delivers only that piece, by whatever means is cheapest, to see if users care. Inference on why the method works: you have limited time and money; ranking forces you to spend them on the assumption that would kill the business if wrong, and focusing the test on one atomic unit of value keeps the test small enough to run fast and clear enough to read.

### Three Mistakes in Hypothesis Testing

1. **Assuming it will work.** If you tend to be assumptive about what will work, lean hard on the process in this document so you do not assume success before it happens.
2. **Treating testing as yes or no.** Testing is not a zero or a one. It is a gradient: you keep learning more so that when you finally reach a working product it has a higher likelihood of meeting your goals.
3. **Ignoring your goals.** Whether you would be happy with a $100k a year business, $1 million, $10 million or $100 million changes which hypotheses matter and how you test them. Tailor the tests to your goals.

### What Kills Startups: The Failure Data

**What it is.** Twenty or thirty years of startup life cycles have been studied (CB Insights ranked the top 20 reasons startups fail). Only a few general themes decide success or failure. The ranking shows that most failures are failures of untested assumptions about the market and the model, not of technology.

- No market need: 42%
- Ran out of cash: 29%
- Not the right team: 23%
- Get outcompeted: 19%
- Pricing / cost issues: 18%
- User un-friendly product: 17%
- Product without a business model: 17%
- Poor marketing: 14%
- Ignore customers: 14%
- Product mistimed: 13%
- Lose focus: 13%
- Disharmony among team / investors: 13%
- Pivot gone bad: 10%
- Lack passion: 9%
- Failed geographical expansion: 9%
- No financing / investor interest: 8%
- Legal challenges: 8%
- Didn't use network: 8%
- Burn out: 8%
- Failure to pivot: 7%

(The spoken lecture says roughly 60 or 70% of failures come from not building a product people want; the chart shown with it puts "no market need" at 42%. Unresolved which framing the speaker intended.)

A separate estimate from innovation research: at least 80% of new products and new businesses fail, and in some industries 90% or more, consistently across markets, across time, and inside giants like Google and Microsoft, almost like a Pareto rule.

**What testing can fix.** Most of the top reasons are testable before you commit:

- **No market need:** the core of all testing.
- **Get outcompeted:** you built a product that worked and someone built a better one. Sprig's product worked, then Uber Eats came in and killed it.
- **Pricing / cost issues:** you can test whether people will pay a given price and learn your cost structure.
- **User un-friendly product:** same.
- **Ran out of cash / not the right team:** testing is also a test of you. Ask: do I like this idea? Am I the right fit? Was this natural or difficult for me? And check that your model and wedge can get you to the next milestone with the money you actually have.

### "Will They Buy" Versus "Can I Sell"

**What it is.** Two different risks that founders conflate. Often it is crystal clear people want the product (commodities, simple things with obvious demand). The hidden risk is whether **you can sell it**.

**Why it matters.** Selling to the government, public schools or huge companies can be extremely hard even when every user says "I absolutely need this." In those markets, the ability to sell is often the number one or number two risk and must be proven.

**Example.** Many education companies tested whether they could get teachers to adopt a product and use it regularly, assuming that enough regular teacher usage would eventually convert some into paying. That required assuming 70 or 80% of teachers might want the product, so niche products died in the testing phase. You build tests that give you increasing confidence that you will eventually be able to sell.

### Will They Buy Now? Timing

Founders forget to test for timing and new needs. "Will they buy" really means "will they buy **now**." Is there something they need and want at this moment? Timing gets tested further in the traction phase.

### Phases Are Fluid

Like every ideation phase, these are fluid. You may not finalize every assumption before going to market, and some assumptions only get proven once you actually get traction. But if you skip testing you are far more likely to fail at the traction phase (as happened at Udemy). That is why testing is worth doing for every business idea.

### Make Assumptions Explicit With a Canvas

**What it is.** A business model canvas (or any diagram or document) has two critical uses: first, a surface check that you have not missed something obvious; second, and more important, to **identify the underlying assumptions** you hold about the opportunity and make them explicit.

**How to do it.**

1. Put everything onto a canvas.
2. Rewrite each element as a belief: "We think customers will pay for this." "We believe this is the channel we will use." "We believe this is the type of resource that makes this business model work."
3. Once you recognize what those assumptions are, you have set the stage for experiments that tell you whether the business model is viable.
4. Run fast and inexpensive experiments against them. That is your best indicator of whether you have a viable business model, and it leads straight into lean startup methods such as finding your minimum viable product.

**Pitfall: unstated assumptions.** The most common mistake: founders build a business model but keep assumptions in the back of their heads that drive how they think about it and never write them down. If you don't write them down, you will never test them. That is extremely dangerous, because studies of entrepreneurs show their assumptions about how the business will work often do not match market reality: very often we are not our company's primary customers.

### Model, Prioritize, Test (Quality Over Volume)

**What it is.** Like a scientist, build models; for an entrepreneur, the model is the business model. Use it to make predictions, prioritize what is riskiest, and test.

**Why it matters.** Many founders latch onto experimentation and then run lots of experiments, picking the fun ones: landing page tests, tweaking words in the copy. Those are optimization experiments; nice, but often not the most critical things in the business. An entrepreneur must, within a limited timeframe, find a business model that works before running out of resources. So prioritize what might be riskiest in the model, not what is easiest, and do the hard stuff. It is not about the volume of experiments but the quality.

**Entrepreneur versus scientist.** Flying by gut is a recipe for driving on faith, so thinking like a scientist (treat your startup as an experiment, use facts to make course corrections) helps. But the dark side is running a business like a scientist: you would take forever to launch because you would want perfect experiments, double-blind tests and no selection bias. Business differs from science: you are not uncovering perpetual truths but **temporal truths** that may not even be absolute (a quirk of millennial behavior may suddenly make a product possible). Entrepreneurs are opportunistic and must act on that. "Being able to see signals in the noise and build something that can make the business model work is the goal of the entrepreneur." All business models get disrupted, so do not go overboard looking for truths that hold forever.

A second source makes the same point from the other side: an entrepreneur has different goals than a scientist. The scientist tries to uncover more universal truths. The entrepreneur needs business model experimentation to test assumptions, uncover market opportunities, reduce time to market and eventually build a valuable business. An entrepreneur is a market-driven animal: rather than starting from theories and running experiments to see if they are true, an entrepreneur starts from a problem and goes back to theory to understand the underlying assumptions preventing the business from succeeding. Once those assumptions have been streamlined, they can be tested, so the entrepreneur can move on and make the product or service fit the market. "Where scientists use labs to test their hypotheses through experimentation, entrepreneurs build business model experiments to test their business ideas in the real world."

### Test Monetization Early

**What it is.** The difference between a hobby and a business is that one is monetizable. If the goal is a business model that works, one key aspect is that it captures value, meaning it makes money. The sooner you test that, the better.

**Why founders defer it.** Since the dot-com days many people push "who is the customer and what will they pay" to later. Many entrepreneurs behave like artists: they create art but are uncomfortable putting a price on it because in their minds it devalues the art, so they underprice. Outgrow those biases.

**How to do it.** Ask two questions early:

1. **Who is the customer?** In some models some people are users and others are customers. Knowing that distinction is very important.
2. **How do you collect money?** What is the pricing model, how do you collect it, and how much is it?

(Contrast with the MVP guidance in Section 5 that a first MVP need not charge at all. Both hold: charging can come in the second, third or fourth MVP, but the monetization question must be on the risk list from the start and tested as soon as it becomes the riskiest item.)

### Cast a Wider Net: Multiple Model Variants

**What it is.** Early on, before you have a viable model, take one idea and create multiple variants on multiple canvases: the same idea sold to businesses (B2B), to consumers (B2C), or as a marketplace. Each canvas stays narrow and specific.

**How to do it.**

1. Draft several canvases for the same idea.
2. Run experiments, in parallel where possible.
3. Watch which canvases, customers and markets react the way you predicted and which create more traction.
4. Double down on the one that also aligns with your mission, vision and values.
5. Drop ideas that look promising on paper but fail light validation: there are already existing alternatives, or customers do not actually have the problem.

**Pitfall: a model that works but does not fit you.** You can find yourself in a model that works but does not align with your values. Example: a founder building products for entrepreneurs found marketers were more excited about the product. Going after marketers would have gone against the brand, the channels and other assets, possibly requiring a wholly separate company or identity. The founder declined. "It's a hard thing to say no to a good customer problem and potential solution that you have, but sometimes those are things that one has to do."

## 4. Why Ideas Fail: The Wrong It, Thoughtland and Your Own Data

### The Right It Versus The Wrong It

**What it is.** The number one reason new businesses and products fail has very little to do with execution. Execution still matters, but the main cause is building "The Wrong It": an idea that will fail in the market even if competently executed. No amount of design brilliance, engineering excellence or marketing fireworks will save it. "The Right It" is a product that, if competently executed, will succeed in the market.

**Why it matters.** The natural first instinct when studying failure is to blame incompetent execution: poor design, poor engineering, poor delivery. The data says otherwise. One founder who had joined two big successes early (Sun Microsystems, and Google as its first engineering director) believed he was immune to failure, then raised $25 million for a company that failed 5 years later. That is what led to studying why people who know what they are doing still fail.

### Market Research Done in Thoughtland

**What it is.** Nobody wakes up thinking "I have a bad idea and I'm going to waste two years building it." Everyone thinks their idea is great, so they present it to other people and collect their thoughts and opinions. That is "market research done in Thoughtland": ask people what they think, hear good or bad, proceed accordingly.

**Why it fails.** People are really bad at predicting whether they would actually buy something, how often they would use it (if at all), and how they would use it. Opinions do not tell you whether an idea has market potential.

### Get Out of Thoughtland: Collect Your Own Data (YODA)

**What it is.** The good data is "Your Own Data" (YODA): data you collected about your own idea that lets you make an informed decision on whether it has market potential.

**Why it works.** Other people's data describes other people's projects. If a founder of an electric car company had looked at the data of every person and company that tried to build electric cars and failed, the conclusion would have been "there's no way to succeed in this market." Instead Tesla created its own data: it got people to pay ahead of time, before building the car, collecting cheques for $5000 from people who really, really wanted the car. What better evidence is there?

**Caveat.** Follow the inspiration of successful people in your market, but do not follow exactly in their footsteps, because what worked for them is unlikely to work for you. Collect your own data about your own project so you can do things your own way.

### Skin-in-the-Game Data

**What it is.** Valid data is not people telling you "if you build it, I will buy it." They need to give you something.

**How to do it.**

1. Decide the smallest meaningful commitment. The smallest amount of skin in the game someone can give is a valid email address, given with a clear understanding that you will use it to tell them when the product is ready.
2. Larger commitments (pre-orders, deposits, payments) are stronger evidence.
3. Count commitments, not compliments.

**Example.** Show a pretotype to 1000 people and get 3 email addresses: there is probably not great demand. At least you have hard data, and if not enough people want it, you do not bother building it.

### Data Beats Opinion

**What it is.** A mantra inside Google: "data beats opinion." You do not convince people by waving your hands and being passionate. You make the case with data.

**How to use it inside an organization.** In large companies most people prefer to work on proven products (Gmail is safe) rather than bet their careers on new, unproven ones, even though a new project that succeeds is far more exciting. The way to overcome the reluctance to take risks is to make the point with data: "We're going to build this project, but we're not going to ask you to spend two years building it just to watch it fail. Before we build it, let's do a series of experiments that give us very high confidence that if we invest two years, it will succeed." The mantra to spread: "make sure you are building The Right It before you build it."

Failure is not limited to startups or to a stage of growth: large companies keep failing too, especially when they keep innovating (the "Google Graveyard" is a long list of products Google killed after they failed). That is exactly why a data-first culture matters inside big organizations.

### Flip the Research Question

Stop asking "If I build it, will you buy it?" Flip it 180 degrees and approach the market with: **"If you buy it, we will build it."** Pretotyping is the method for asking that question and collecting that market data. The first thing to unlearn is doing market research in Thoughtland; you cannot take on new principles if you are not willing to unlearn old ones.

### The Innovator's Bias

**What it is.** The tendency of entrepreneurs to fall in love with their solutions. We see an idea and jump to the first solution that enters our mind, but the first solution is often not optimal and often wrong.

**Why it is dangerous.** We come to believe that solution is what we have to build, then spend our energy chasing the wrong risks. We chase building a product, and if the market does not need or want it, by the time it is built and resources are exhausted we realize we must start over. That is a big setback, especially for first-time entrepreneurs.

**How to overcome it: problem first.**

1. Like first principles in science, deconstruct the idea. Break the business down into **customers and problems**.
2. If you do not have the right customers trying to get a problem solved, no solution matters. Unless you solve a problem, customers will not use it or pay, even if you can reach them.
3. Even a patent or an unfair advantage does not matter if your customers don't care.
4. Test continuously: do not build the entire product. Build something smaller (an MVP), and before even that, start with a demo, or first go find customers, see if you can reach them and talk to them. "If you can talk to customers, we can sell them anything."
5. Start with the end in mind, then deconstruct your way back to the beginning and validate bottom-up.

### The Ego Trap

Getting excited about your own idea and the vision of yourself as a successful founder, then going gung-ho toward it, is actually egotistical. You think you are protecting yourself, but you are so concerned about being disappointed that instead of evaluating the idea and being willing to change it, you assume you are right, either because you do not want to deal with feedback or because you are blind to it. The concierge model (Section 6) is the best way out: do the product for people and you will really understand what they want and whether they will keep engaging.

### The Risk-Taking Misconception

**What it is.** The popular image is the heroic entrepreneur who jumps off a cliff and builds a parachute on the way down. In reality many seasoned entrepreneurs are very risk-averse: they pack a parachute and a backup parachute before jumping, because they want to protect their downside.

**How to apply it.** Prioritize removing the riskiest pieces in order. Start with the riskiest things, and gradually what you are left with is a business that can work. Entrepreneurs are practical people who try to launch businesses and minimize risk, not generate additional financial risk; they are not bankers or financiers.

### Speed: The Old Planning Playbook No Longer Works

**What it is.** The execution mindset comes from traditional business planning: take an idea, spend a long time on analysis without knowing all the facts, raise money, build a team, execute the plan.

**Why it stopped working.** That playbook worked when there was little competition: you could build a product, get it to market, and even if it was wrong you still had customers' attention and could build again. That is how big companies survived. Today one statistic puts the number of ideas started each year at 100 million, which is 300 a second. If you spend nine months building a perfect plan, others with similar ideas are moving, and only one of them needs to break through. You have to go much faster, which means constantly learning: a search mindset versus an execution mindset, with rough plans that are corrected through validation. The business plan is such a big artifact that nobody keeps it up to date, so use a lighter tool that allows dynamic evolution. "We are constantly correcting course, which is how business planning should have been in the first place!"

One founder's personal trigger for this: across many projects that all started out looking amazing, the problem was not having to shut some down but the time it took: about two years from having an idea to knowing whether it would work. That is far too long when you have more ideas than time.

### From Lean Manufacturing to Continuous Innovation

**What it is.** For roughly the last hundred years (the manufacturing era), the unfair advantage was mass production: whoever produced the most for the lowest cost won; it was all about efficiency. After the war, squeezed companies looked for new ways of thinking: Taiichi Ohno at Toyota invented the Toyota Production System, using Just in Time techniques that became Lean Manufacturing, with core principles of being less wasteful and continuous improvement. That is the origin of much of today's startup thinking.

**How it evolved.** As products became digital, speed mattered ever more. PC computing made requirements change faster, so methods moved from traditional manufacturing to waterfall, then from waterfall to agile, with more iterations and feedback. Lean Startup arrived with the shift from PCs to the internet. Online, the old distance between companies and customers almost vanished: we are more connected to customers than ever, so we can learn faster, but customers also demand more than ever. The need for speed reached a point best called continuous innovation: "It's no longer stop and go, we can't take six months to do requirements analysis and then another six months to build a product." Amazon, Facebook and Netflix continuously build and launch; requirements gathering, building and testing have blurred together and happen continuously.

### Remove "Failure" From Your Vocabulary

**What it is.** Even with all the talk of "fail fast," humans do not like failure. No one says "I failed 10 times today and I'm going to keep doing it."

**Why it matters.** When an experiment fails, the first reaction is to hide it or justify it. That is where bias sets in: we produce a biased and often completely wrong explanation to feel good, then sell that story to everyone else. That moves away from scientific thinking into covering up and using faith to explain away things not baked in reality.

**How to reframe it.** Buckminster Fuller: "There is no such thing as a failed experiment, only unexpected outcomes." You start with a business model and predictions about how customers will behave. If they do not behave that way, that is an unexpected outcome, and it tells you your thinking was off by some assumption. Find and fix that assumption and you might find a breakthrough. By definition there is no breakthrough without some unexpected outcome: if everything you expected happens, where is the breakthrough?

**How to do it.**

1. When a prediction does not come true, do not let the team sugarcoat it with a justification.
2. Go deeper: call the customer and find out why they did not buy, or run another experiment to understand the root cause.
3. The answers are the gems. That is where breakthrough insights and the inflection points of the hockey stick curve hide.

## 5. The MVP: Principles, Budget and Cadence

### What an MVP Is

**What it is.** The minimum viable product is another word for the atomic unit of value. It is the least work you have to do to prove your hypothesis: build whatever you are building with the least effort possible, just enough to prove the hypothesis you have. Your MVP needs to focus on one thing: the goal.

### Pretotype Before Prototype

**What it is.** A pretotype is something you build way before the product, and also way before a prototype. You do not actually build the thing to see if the market is interested.

**Why it works.** A prototype answers "can we build it?": will it work, how will it work, how long will the battery last. Research on new products shows that 99% of the time you can build it. Most apps in the app store do not fail because people could not build them; they fail because people are not interested (around 95% of apps make no money). A pretotype asks a different question: "should we build it?", or "if we build it, will people buy it?" All the uncertainty and risk lies not in building but in the market wanting it.

**Example.** An app that recommends interesting books based on personal preferences. Any engineer can build it, so prototyping is pointless. The question is whether the market wants it, which a pretotype answers (see the YouTube Pretotype in Section 6).

The name came from a story (see the IBM speech-to-text test in Section 6) where a team pretended to have a working prototype: it was first called a "pretendotype," then shortened to "pretotype."

### Demo, Sell, Build (Not Build, Demo, Sell)

**What it is.** The traditional sequence is build the product, demo it to a customer, then sell it. Reverse it: build a demo first, sell that demo, and if you cannot sell the demo, do not even build the product.

**Why it works.** You go much faster. The demo validates market risk clearly, and it also helps you test what you will build, because the demo shows what the product will do: if the customer does not like it, you can still change it. You are systematically testing risks, and customer/market risk is recognized as the riskiest.

**Caveat: feasibility risk remains.** If you sell something you have not built, you now carry feasibility risk: can you build what you promised? That is usually less risky, but it is not a given; many companies cannot build good products and fail anyway. At least you have proven that if you build what you showed, you stand to get that customer. At the extreme, Richard Branson and Elon Musk are already selling tickets to the moon: they know it is technically impossible today but will happen one day, and early adopters who want to be first to vacation on the moon are already buying.

### CHESS: The Five Rules of an MVP

**What it is.** A framework for remembering what an MVP must be. Follow it so you do not waste time on everything else.

1. **Cheap.** Do not spend money on an MVP. If you are talented at advertising you might spend some on ads; if not, do not even advertise. Certainly do not pay a developer to build it, and do not spend a lot of time or effort on it.
2. **Haphazard.** It is not supposed to look good. "Your MVP needs to look like shit. If it looks good, you screwed up and put too much effort into it." Two reasons: first, you want users to buy because they want the product, not because it looks great (later, when selling to many people, you will want it to look great, but at the start you want pure value); second, effort.
3. **Easy.** Build most MVPs, at least the first version, in two to four weeks. An experienced builder often does it in one to two weeks; if you are new, two to four weeks is understandable. Longer than twelve weeks is an insane timeline and you are doing something wrong. This holds for 99% of businesses (hard exceptions exist, such as SpaceX or Tesla). (The slide says "Take 1-2 weeks to build," while the spoken guidance gives 2 to 4 weeks for beginners; treat 1 to 2 weeks as the experienced target and 12 weeks as the hard ceiling.)
4. **Specific.** Test only one or two hypotheses, not five, not twenty. That keeps the test targeted at the goal, and it is what makes cheap, haphazard and easy achievable.
5. **Simple.** Very few features and a clear value proposition, so the customer understands what is going on.

**Why it works.** Inference: each letter removes a way founders waste effort: money (cheap), polish (haphazard), build time (easy), scope creep in what is being tested (specific) and scope creep in the product (simple). Whatever does not serve the one or two hypotheses being tested is waste.

**Pitfall.** Inference: if your MVP costs real money, looks finished, takes months, tests many things at once or has many features, it is no longer an MVP; it is a product built on untested assumptions.

### Build, Measure, Learn

**What it is.** Within each section of the ideation and exploration process you are constantly testing, building and iterating: you try something, put it out there, then change it and improve it.

**How to do it.**

1. **Build:** write down a thesis and the riskiest assumption you are testing.
2. Create goals that evaluate the thesis, and write them down.
3. **Measure:** was the thesis right? Why or why not? Write down what you measured.
4. **Learn:** what about the thesis needs to change? Write down what you learned.
5. Repeat.

Each turn of the loop narrows the gap between your thesis and reality.

**What it really looks like.** People picture a clean path; in reality you go back and forth many times until you finally get a product that starts getting traction. Once it has traction you keep building on it, and eventually you reach a mature and stable company.

### Iteration Cadence: 1a, 1b, 1c, Then 2a

**What it is.** You iterate on different tests to get more and more confidence, which justifies doing more and more work.

**How it plays out.** If each test succeeds, your first MVP might take a week or five days, your second three weeks, your next ten weeks. More likely: you spend a week on the first, try it, realize you screwed it up, spend another week, and run 1a, 1b, 1c, tweaking as you go; maybe on 1d you finally get it right. Then you move to 2a, which might take three or five weeks to build. It takes time. At each stage, each hypothesis must be either thoroughly exhausted or eliminated based on what you learned.

### How Much to Spend

**What it is.** Ideally $0, or just the cost of no-code software or transaction fees (Eventbrite fees, for example): a few hundred dollars.

**Rule of thumb.** Spend money you would be willing to lose in a poker game: the buy-in you would accept for a game that is high stakes for you. If that number is zero, come up with a hypothesis that can be tested for zero dollars, for example just by emailing people.

### Should You Advertise?

(The two instructors disagree here; keep both views in mind.)

- **Generally, no.** You should not advertise unless you are genuinely good at it. Advertising can cost a lot of money for nothing, and you need to know what you are doing first.
- **Yes, if you know how, but spend enough.** Advertising is an amazing way to test for people who know how to do it. The common mistake is spending far too little: founders think $50 will show a response, and that is typically not the case. If you advertise, know what you are doing and be prepared to lose all of it, likely thousands of dollars. That is exactly why most people should not, since thousands of dollars is a lot of money to most people.

On customer acquisition cost (CAC): unless you know you will have a lot of money to invest, do not rely solely on paid acquisition, and especially not in your MVP. Look for a business where you make so much money per customer that you can afford a three to five times LTV to CAC ratio (you make three to five times your money back on advertising); otherwise do not rely on advertising as your business model.

### How Much Traffic an MVP Needs

A good MVP still needs a fair bit of traffic and eyeballs to learn anything. A working target: ten to twenty customers who actually buy, or show some strong indicator, whom you then talk to in order to understand them. Getting ten, twenty or thirty such customers might require five hundred to a thousand people seeing the page. If you cannot find ten people to try your product, or get a hundred people to your landing page, the issue is bigger than "people don't want it": you have to figure out who wants it and where they live.

### Does the MVP Need to Charge or Make a Profit?

No. An MVP only tests the hypothesis you have. If users do not pay, that does not mean the product is bad; you may not even charge. Charging is something you might test in your second, third or fourth MVP. The MVP is a proof of concept to answer one specific question. If the profit equation is a major risk in your business, focus a test on it; otherwise profit does not matter at all at this stage.

## 6. The MVP Playbook: Test Types and Full Cases

**The slide list of MVP types.** The course slide names five types: 1. Marketing Test; 2. Build a user base first; 3. Wizard of Oz; 4. Concierge Model; 5. No-code product. After any of them comes **MVP Feedback**: collecting and acting on what the MVP taught you (Section 7). The staged rollout and the 10x model below are additional types from the spoken sessions.

### Type 1: The Marketing Test

**What it is.** The classic MVP. You create a marketing asset (a page, a video, a tweet, an email) that gets people excited about the business, and you gauge excitement by clicks, by emotional reaction, or any other way.

**When to use it.** When the question is "do people want another one of these products?" If you have never started a business or launched a page, start here: running the test is itself a lesson for you.

**Why it works.** You learn whether the customers you think exist actually respond, and whether you know where to find them, before you build anything.

**How to do it.**

1. Write the one narrow hypothesis you are testing (usually: do people in this place want my product?).
2. Pick a format: video, landing page, a tweet, or an email to fifty friends who are good fits if you have no following.
3. Put it where your hypothesized customers are. Finding and reaching the customer is part of the test: if you build for athletes, you must get in front of athletes.
4. Capture emails at some point.
5. Measure the response, then talk to the people who responded.

**Pitfall: know what you did not test.** A marketing test answers a narrow question. It does not tell you whether people will pay, whether they will use it, or how many want it. You have to build further tests for those.

**Pitfall and bonus: unexpected segments.** You may post for athletes and find athletes do not care, but two chefs on the same forum love it. Expect a lot of variance in customer segmentation during MVPs.

#### Case: Dropbox (Video Test)

The riskiest hypothesis: do people want another file management product? The vision was that file management and storage was hard, but dozens of file management products already existed, and the founder was not sure people wanted another one, so he tested before spending months building. He posted a simple YouTube video explaining what Dropbox did. The product in the video did not really work: it did not run on everyone's computer and many features were not built. He posted it on Digg, a very famous tech site similar to Reddit at the time, and it reached the top page of its channel, maybe even the Digg homepage. He learned he had an early set of customers and early evangelists in that place. He did not learn whether people would pay, or whether they would actually use it; only that they were excited by the promise. The hypothesis was narrow: "Do people want my product in the place where I'm posting my MVP?" He then had to build other tests to go to the next level.

#### Case: The YouTube Pretotype (Book Recommendation App)

Instead of coding an app, use PowerPoint, Keynote or any similar program to simulate what the app does and record it as a little movie. For the book recommendation app: one screen where you enter your name and the books you like, a button, and then the app showing a recommendation. No code at all; anyone who can use PowerPoint can do it.

1. Create the video.
2. Build a one or two page website that describes what the app does and shows the video.
3. Show it to people, via some ads or by posting on forums: "Here's an app I am trying to build. If you are interested, give me your email address and once I launch it I will let you know."
4. Count the skin-in-the-game data. Shown to 1000 people with 3 email addresses back, demand is probably not great, and you do not build it.

#### Case: Buffer (Landing Page, Fake Door and Pricing Test)

Buffer was a simple standalone Twitter scheduler, a function that existed inside many other, more complicated social media SaaS products. The founder was already building his brand on Twitter, had the problem himself, and wanted to know if others had it. First question: do people want a standalone Twitter scheduler?

- **Page 1:** a dead-simple landing page describing the product, probably built in a couple of hours, shared on Twitter.
- **Page 2 (the fake door):** clicking through revealed, in effect, "Oops, I don't actually have this product yet. Sign up with your email address." This is the fake door hack.

Second question: will people pay? For many products that is not a risk because existing spend is obvious (people already buy toothbrushes; if yours costs the same as in store, the risk is differentiation, not willingness to pay). But for Buffer it mattered: scheduling was bundled into bigger products, and a simple tool sits low on the totem pole, with many potential customers who probably will not pay much. He needed to know whether a paying customer base existed and, more importantly, who they were.

- **Page 3 (inserted between the two):** a pricing page with $0, $5 and $20 options. Nothing sat behind it; the click was tracked, then everyone landed on the same email sign-up page.

#### Taking Real Money in a Test: Ethics and Refunds

Buffer did not take money, but you can. One practice used many times: take the payment, refund it right away, and say "you caught us before we're ready" or simply "this is just a test." Then offer, for example, a 50% discount with delivery in six months; if they are happy, fine; if not, full refund (or refund anyway). This is not stealing, since everyone gets their money back, and early payers are rewarded as early evangelists, sometimes getting the service free when it goes live. Unverified: whether this is legal in every jurisdiction. The speakers' understanding is that false advertising requires advertising inaccurately and actually completing a sale and taking money with intent to follow through; they state clearly they are not lawyers and this is not legal advice, so check with a lawyer.

### Type 2: Build the Audience First

**What it is.** Build an audience before you have a product. Post on Twitter, a blog, Pinterest, TikTok, whatever fits, in an area you are genuinely interested in and have interesting thoughts on. Build people who like what you are cooking up, then slowly sell something to them. You do not even try to sell a specific product at first; you just try to get users.

**When to use it.** When your riskiest hypothesis is "can I actually get the users?": you are not confident in your marketing ability, unsure how to acquire people, or do not know what it will cost. It suits natural marketers who like talking, posting and engaging with communities. You can apply the principles of this whole process to building an audience instead of a startup.

**Why it works.** Every piece of content and every pitch is a test of message and market. By the time you build a business, it is already running and you know far more than the average founder.

#### Case: The Hustle

The founder first ran a conference, HustleCon, made it popular through an email list, and learned he was good at emailing and writing. The conference made half a million dollars, which funded The Hustle. The first move was a blog post that went viral: about a million monthly uniques in the first month. But traffic was not the business model; advertisers had to pay. He grew the email list to thirty thousand people in a handful of months, then needed to hit thirty thousand dollars in monthly revenue to pay the bills. He cold-emailed advertisers (Phrasee was one) with no idea what he was doing (he did not know what CPM meant) and ran the same process as testing: call people, ask questions, push the limit and see where the market falls. The pitch went roughly: "You need more users? Great. I think I can get you more users. What's a user worth to you?" If they said fifty dollars: "I think I can get you a thousand of them," then price it, then discount.

The lesson: many businesses start by doing one thing that leads to another and another until you realize you have a business. What holds people back is a big vision (a media empire) with no idea of steps two, three or four. You do not need to know those steps. You need to know the first step, the general direction, and have faith you will figure out the rest.

#### Case: A Writing Course Built on an Audience

A college student in his early twenties, lonely in a college town in North Carolina, discovered Twitter, engaged, followed people and messaged them, making plenty of mistakes along the way; it took about five years to become well known. He wrote about what he read, largely summarizing tech companies, famous tech bloggers and well-known internet thinkers, on Twitter and his blog, and slowly built an audience (85,000 followers at the time of the screenshot shown). At around twenty-five he built his first course, Write of Passage, on how to write online, teaching how he built his own audience. He had no money: a grant of about twenty-five thousand dollars and a video camera, enough to pay a video crew. With his business partner he ran it live from Mexico City, much like a live cohort: they filmed content, sent it daily to a list of people, who watched, reviewed and gave feedback, and he updated the videos constantly over the first week or two. After iterating he launched publicly. Today it is a multi-hundred-thousand-dollar business, likely heading to a few million a year within about five years, and he owns 100% of it.

Why the branding is so crisp: he had done the pitch a thousand times. Probably five hundred times he emailed people directly, writing fresh emails each time and updating the copy a little each time; another fifty to a hundred times he sent form emails and improved them; then he promoted on Twitter and started calling people. He was testing his pitch and marketing continuously and knew exactly who he was marketing to.

### Type 3: The Wizard of Oz

**What it is.** You fake the solution. The customer thinks there is technology; behind the curtain, humans do the work. The user has no idea you are doing everything manually on the back end.

**When to use it.** When the problem is validated (by your own test, or because it is well established) but your solution is new, and you need to know whether the solution actually solves the problem. Especially when the technology is hard and expensive to build. It works well for recommendation systems, engagement products and community businesses.

**How to do it.**

1. Present the product interface to the customer as if it were automated (a chat, an upload, a "processing time" message).
2. Do the work manually or with hired humans on the back end.
3. Deliver the result as if the system produced it.
4. Over time, build more technology: gradually reduce reliance on humans and increase reliance on technology instead of building it all at once.

**Example (hypothetical).** A recommendation system to help people make better money choices: tell people "I'm going to be your personal coach," run it over chat, and manually produce every recommendation behind the scenes.

#### Case: CardMunch

Business cards are a pain, and most business card management attempts failed (LinkedIn, maybe Plaxo and ZoomInfo did well). The founder wanted machine scanning of business cards from an iPhone, which meant hard machine learning to read any card. Before building the tech he needed to know if people would use the service. He hired humans on sites such as Mechanical Turk, Craigslist or Fiverr. Users sent photos of their business cards, the app said "processing time 24 hours" or "48 hours," humans typed out the cards, and the results were sent back to the iPhone.

#### Case: IBM Speech-to-Text (the Original "Pretendotype")

Around 1980 IBM wanted everyone to have a personal computer, but most people would never learn to use a keyboard (only secretaries, programmers and writers used one). Idea: run the computer by speaking into a microphone. The technology could not be built for years; computers were not fast enough. Before investing, IBM tested whether people would want to use a microphone exclusively. They brought people into a room with a microphone and a screen: "This is a new way of running a computer. No keyboard, you just speak to it." Someone dictated "Dear Mr. Jones..." and saw the letter and commands appear and the letter sent. In the next room a fast typist heard the input through headphones and typed it. To users it looked like a working prototype. The data was valuable and fast: people get a sore throat talking to a computer for several hours a day; an office where everyone talks is very loud; you cannot use it for confidential material because everyone overhears. IBM concluded it had better accept that people would learn keyboards, and 40 years later we all use keyboards, even though they are arguably not an efficient way to use a computer.

### Type 4: The Concierge

**What it is.** You do it for them, openly. Unlike the Wizard of Oz (they don't know you are doing it, because you are testing whether a solution works), in the concierge model you know the problem exists but do not know the right solution, so you walk the customer through it personally, even physically, in person.

**Why it works.** Doing it by hand teaches you the details of why people like or dislike the service, so what you later build is efficient. It is also the best antidote to the ego trap (Section 4): you see what customers really want and whether they keep engaging. Even founders of companies making fifty to a hundred million dollars a year stay this hands-on; one founder who sold his company for five hundred million dollars still personally fixed typos and broken features when users messaged him.

**A second definition.** Inference (the slide source defines it only through the Stripe and Sprig examples): a concierge MVP delivers the value proposition to the customer by hand, doing the work for them, instead of building the technology that will eventually automate it. The customer gets the real outcome; the founder gets to learn exactly what needs building before building it.

**The riskiest hypothesis it de-risks.** "What do I build?" (Stripe slide). Inference for the rest of this paragraph: when the product is technically complex, the biggest danger is spending months building the wrong technology. Doing the job manually for real customers shows you which parts of the product actually matter and which are irrelevant, at almost no engineering cost. It also tests the riskiest business hypotheses (will they use it, can we deliver at a profitable price) directly with paying customers.

#### Case: Stripe

Dozens of ways to take payments existed; eBay had been around for over fifteen years when Stripe started (around 2008). Online payments were hard and clunky and the founders thought they could make it easy. Their early practice: they showed up at your office (or home office) and integrated the API into your software for you, sitting next to you and coding it. An incredible level of effort and ingenuity, and in the process they learned why people liked or did not like the service. Investors had a nickname for this force-it-to-happen approach, a pun on the founders' surname, Collison; in the session it is called the "collision effect."

**The slide version.** Stripe was a payments processing startup taking on an extremely complex B2B problem. It had to build a lot of technology to make it work. Its biggest concern was building the wrong tech. So it had a simple challenge: how do we de-risk our time and make sure we build the right thing? **Riskiest hypothesis: What do I build?** "To solve their problem, they did the most ghetto MVP I've heard of. They literally went to startups' offices and actually implemented Stripe for them, instead of building the implementation themselves." At the time, the product was presented as **/dev/payments**, with a simple landing page: "Payment Processing Done Right.", three feature blocks ("Sane, simple, open-source APIs.", "Immediate sign-up", "Powerful analytics") and a "Try it now" button with "No sign-up required."

**Caveat.** The slide adds: "Obviously this can prevent you from learning what people" (the sentence is cut off in the source). Inference: doing the integration for customers can hide how hard it would be for them to do it themselves, so what you learn about self-serve usage is limited.

#### Case: Sprig (Concierge Test of a Food Delivery Idea)

**Thesis:** deliver healthy meals for fifteen dollars in fifteen minutes. Earlier competitive analysis showed one competitor (SpoonRocket) was good at speed and another at healthy, quality meals; the goal was to combine the two. The test: can we deliver quality food fast?

**Features that mattered (and nothing else):**

1. Delivery: could the food get there?
2. Underlying cost structure: no profit needed on the MVP, but the costs had to be understood, because the founder had never run a food business.
3. Quality: the food had to arrive intact and look good.

The founder knew people would love $15, 15-minute healthy food; the uncertainty was whether it could be done at that time and price point. So the value proposition itself was the hypothesis.

**The slide version of the thesis and question.** Product thesis: "Using GPS-managed drivers, Sprig will provide healthy meals to customers for $15 in 15 minutes." The question the MVP must answer: "Will this work? Can we deliver food at a profitable price point?" Features that matter (test these): delivery model (can good food be delivered in 15 minutes); costs (need to find out what the underlying cost structure is); quality (how will the food look when it arrives?). Features that don't matter (skip these in the MVP): app for ordering; delivery algorithm; full kitchen setup; inventory and order management software; driver dispatch / GPS (didn't even use "Find My iPhone"); text message / order confirmation system.

**Lesson.** Inference: every feature that makes the eventual product scale (apps, algorithms, dispatch, confirmations) was removed. Only the three things that decide whether the business works (speed, cost, quality) were tested, and they were tested by humans doing the work manually.

**What the final product had, and what the test skipped.** The eventual product had ordering apps on web, Android and iPhone; a delivery algorithm routing the closest driver by time and distance; a full kitchen; inventory and order management from kitchen to driver to customer with feedback; GPS and a driver app feeding the routing; and a confirmation system. The test had none of these, or very crude versions.

**Timeline.** Heard of the idea in early April 2013; ran the test in the first week of May 2013: about a month, while working part-time at Lyft. Two weeks developing the idea, two weeks executing the MVP.

**The atomic unit of value.** One night of delivering dinner to customers in a very small area of a small city (San Francisco, only about a million people).

**How it ran.**

1. **Ordering:** Eventbrite instead of an app. It is free software on the internet (not what people usually call no-code, but it works). Tickets were the orders; Eventbrite's form collected order details. The menu had no pictures, just text: roughly "meatballs or chicken, what do you want?"
2. **Customers:** only friends and family of the co-founders, because demand was not being tested. They got an email and ordered in real time on the night.
3. **Dispatch:** the founder's living room. A printed map of San Francisco without street names (he knew the city by heart), Google Maps on the laptop, and Settlers of Catan pieces in different colors, one per driver, six drivers, moved around the map to track where each was.
4. **Operations:** orders in a spreadsheet, text messages to customers and drivers.
5. **Cost:** about $500 up front: roughly $400 to a chef to make the food and $100 on TaskRabbit for delivery help, plus team members driving.
6. **Brand:** no real name; a throwaway name ("Fresh").

**CHESS check.** Cheap ($500). Haphazard (no name, no pictures). Easy (two weeks). Specific (only whether it could be delivered at the target cost and speed). Simple.

The proof of concept succeeded; the atomic unit of value worked. Sprig later ran for about four years; see Section 7 for what the survey from this single night revealed.

#### How to Run a Concierge MVP

Inference: these steps generalize the structure of the Sprig slide.

1. Write your product thesis in one sentence (who, what, price, speed).
2. Rewrite it as the question that decides whether the business works (usually "can we deliver this at a profitable price?").
3. Split your feature list into "features that matter" (they answer that question) and "features that don't matter" (they only make it scale).
4. Deliver the value manually for real customers, covering only the features that matter.
5. Measure the delivery, cost and quality results, then decide what to build (Build, Measure, Learn).

**Pitfalls.**

- Inference: the manual delivery does not scale; it is a learning tool, not the business.
- Because you do the work for the customer, you may not learn what they would do on their own (the Stripe caveat above).

### Type 5: No-Code MVPs

**What it is.** Hundreds of no-code products now let non-engineers cobble together a working business: blogs and sites (WordPress), email tools, chatbots, full-stack builders like Bubble, Crowd app and Yola, and other website builders, scrapers, and connectors. Even software engineers should use them for early MVPs. Do not learn all of them, only the ones you need. Directories (such as "create without code") list the options.

**Zapier "zaps".** Connections between products: when an action occurs in one product, it triggers another. Example: a Typeform sign-up form asks whether someone wants email. Yes goes one way, no goes another; or five branches: "willing to pay now" gets an email tomorrow, "willing to pay later" gets one in a week. You can build an entire subscription business this way. One eight-figure business still runs on Zapier: scrappy, but scalable and it works well. Inference from the speaker: whole billion-dollar companies may be built on no-code within about five years without ever hiring an engineer.

#### Case: A Community Business Built on 179 Zaps

OnDeck, a community for people planning their next startup (founders meeting co-founders), running for about a year, had 179 zaps. Uses included pipeline automations; auto-generated Google Docs and Slides from templates (a customized deck for each applicant interview, synced with Google Calendar so every meeting had its own deck); intro requests between members; event RSVPs straight into Google Calendar; and a referral leaderboard showing who referred the most members. The operator used a set of about twenty core tools.

### Type 6: The Staged Rollout ("Permission to Scale")

**What it is.** Do not launch to everyone at once. Start with a small group, prove the model there, then roll out systematically. Rather than rushing to scale and being overwhelmed by risk and mistakes, give yourself permission to scale: products go through a life cycle, and a smaller number of customers makes the process manageable and lets you tackle the riskiest assumptions first. It applies to startups and corporates alike.

#### Case: Facebook's Accidental Staged Rollout

The founder was a college student without the resources or credibility to raise money or launch publicly; Friendster and Myspace already had millions of users and revenue. So he launched on one campus. It immediately took off. He benchmarked traction against the closest competitors and saw it outperforming on usage and engagement, which in that world translates to advertising revenue. He tested monetization cheaply by adding Google ads and showed about twice the revenue, from a very small number of very highly engaged users. To prove it to himself, the team and future investors, he picked the next campuses carefully: schools that already had social networks (early adopters, where the job was displacing the incumbent) and Ivy League and well-known schools (which created exclusivity). By the time the next four schools were done, word spread that it was the coolest college network and only elite schools had it, so every other school wanted in. A constraint ("we can't afford to launch publicly") became a pipeline of schools waiting their turn. Picture telling an investor: "You pick the next school, and we'll demonstrate amazing traction there within 30 days." That repeatable system is a "customer factory": users arrive, get highly engaged, get monetized, and the model works. Each school was a petri dish; repeatability made a compelling scalability story and sent the valuation through the roof.

#### Case: Tesla's Deliberate Staged Rollout

Stage one was an expensive sports car, the Roadster, built to test the riskiest assumption: can we build a new electric battery that goes 200 miles on one charge? Stage two was a car they built entirely themselves, to get good at building cars. Stage three was the affordable electric car, the Model 3. The founder could have put the battery in a cheap car from the start, but that would have taken on too many risks at once.

### Type 7: The 10x Model (Doublings From One Customer)

**What it is.** A systematic version of the staged rollout. Instead of "I'll launch to everyone," start with one customer.

**Why it works.** One sounds wrong, but getting one person to part with hard-earned money is the "singularity moment" of a product and worth celebrating. Every company, Amazon, Facebook or yours, starts with one customer, then doubles, and doubles again. Three doublings is 8x; keep doubling and you reach 10x. Most people will reach 10 customers; some will reach 100; some 1,000. Facebook simply doubles more times in rapid succession before slowing down. Your business may not need to get that big because your model may work at a much smaller scale. The 10x frame gives you a mathematical way to think about permission to scale, and nonlinear thinking automatically prioritizes the right risks.

**How to do it.**

1. Ask: how do I convert my first customer?
2. Then: how do I get to 10? Most products suffer customer and market risk, not technical risk. At 10 customers you can cover a lot technically: one web server may be enough, and you can do high-touch onboarding to make up for the product's shortcomings.
3. Then: how do I get to 100? The next level is not another 10 but 100, which forces incremental investment in the things that need to scale.
4. Then 1,000, and so on. Each jump is hard, but a manageable harder step rather than one impossible leap.

## 7. After the MVP: Surveys, Feedback and Unexpected Outcomes

### The Post-MVP Survey

**What it is.** After an MVP, ask for structured feedback. The survey from Sprig's single test night (Section 6) is the model.

**How to do it.**

1. **Rate your experience** on a 1 to 7 scale. You can use 1 to 5 or 1 to 10, but 1 to 7 forces people to think for a moment instead of giving an automatic 9 or 6 out of habit, pulling them out of their survey-response bias.
2. **How likely are you to order again?** Always let people explain why or why not in a comment: the reasons are where the feedback lives.
3. **Open-ended comments** at the end.

**Reading the score.** Sprig's average was 6 out of 7 on most questions, which is good for an MVP. But take it with a grain of salt: respondents were friends, and first customers are always more excited than second and third customers. Expect early enthusiasm to be inflated.

### What One Night of Feedback Predicted

The open-ended comments from the very first test articulated almost every challenge the business would face over the following years: "In four weeks, we knew everything we needed to know about the business." The company went ahead anyway, knowing the problems up front and working on them.

1. **Packaging.** Customers said to be careful with packaging and that moving food from plastic to a plate was awkward. So delivery food had to look beautiful in its own container. Some of the company's largest spending went to design firms to improve packaging. In hindsight the team was too dogmatic about making packaging compostable (a strongly environmental San Francisco culture); being forced to compost produced bad packaging and almost killed the company. Visuals were decent from the start but never reached the next level.
2. **Deliver two nights at once.** Some customers suggested delivering two meals at once and were happy with that. The company eventually realized it could deliver dinner one night and lunch the next day. The economics mattered: unless an order was worth about twenty-eight or thirty dollars, delivery cost too much. That was learned a little too late; multi-meal delivery (two or three nights at once) is now common in the category, and other companies (for example Trifecta) do exactly what that early customer suggested. Someone will make a business work in any market; the goal is to give yourself the highest chance of being that person.
3. **The chef did not sell them.** This changed the business. Customers care who the chef is at first (that is why they buy the first time), but for a product bought fifteen or twenty times, by the second or third order they only care whether the service delivered the right thing. (Contrast: in a product bought once or twice, such as some education, the teacher may matter a lot.)
4. **Healthy positioning attracted a specific segment.** Paleo eaters and people who like salads, live in affluent neighborhoods and want lean food that does not taste like diet frozen meals. The survey told the team who their customers were.

### What Surveys Can and Cannot Do

- **They can** show how people received the MVP (not the final product, which will be different).
- **They can** identify who your customers are. The people who give you 7s are the ones to interview: "Who are you? What are you about? How do I find more people like you?"
- **They cannot** gauge absolute enthusiasm. You do not know if people will pay in future unless you charge; and even if you charge, you do not know how many people will like it.

### Unexpected Outcomes Are the Gold

When predictions miss, dig: call the customer, find out why they did not buy, or run another experiment to find the root cause (see "Remove 'Failure' From Your Vocabulary," Section 4). That is where breakthroughs are.

## 8. Finding First Customers and Getting Unstuck

### Where Do First Customers Come From?

**What it is.** It depends on what the MVP tests.

- If you are **not** testing demand (for example, testing whether you can deliver), friends and family are fine. Sprig used them.
- If you **are** testing whether people want the product, do not ask friends. State your hypothesis as "this is the type of customer I think wants this," go find those customers, and **find them organically**.

**Why organic.** Invariably, if nobody on the internet wants your product organically, nobody wants your product, period. Finding customers is part of the test: a test of the product and of you, since you learn how to find customers.

**If you have no list and no audience.** Focus on finding customers where they are, online or in person. If you cannot find ten people to try it or get a hundred people to a landing page, the problem is bigger than demand: you do not yet know who wants it or where they live. Go find where they live, in person or on the internet. Practical channels named across the sessions: forums, Facebook groups, LinkedIn, subreddits, Craigslist, Thumbtack (find people requesting a service in the real world and message them), Twitter, and an email to fifty well-chosen friends.

### Response Rates When You Ask Strangers for Time

- The response rate should be much higher than you think.
- There is no numeric benchmark; with experience across fields you develop a gut feeling for it.
- Benchmark to aim for: if you cannot get five people from the target group on the phone within a week (for whom the problem is interesting), something is wrong with your pitch or your idea.
- Post types differ. "Can I get feedback on this?" posts tend to get lower response. Alternatives: "I'm starting this company and looking for first employees; does anyone want to talk to me, give feedback and see if they'd want to join?"; or a quick landing page asking people to submit an email to be first to test it.
- Target finesse: pick the easiest group with the highest likelihood of success (mid-career people who are not inundated, rather than senior hotshots), then expand. Posting to the wrong group (for example senior architects in their fifties in Kigali, Rwanda, for a US-based founder) will get few calls.
- Early adopters often do reply. One investor-operator reports answering roughly 20% of the time when asked a hundred times by earnest founders, by email or phone. Others answer email but never phone, and reply mainly to people who seem intelligent and send basic emails that make sense, or via Messenger or Twitter DMs.

### Three Reasons People Stall, and What to Do

1. **"I could never build an MVP in two weeks."** Just try. Pick the easiest hypothesis you possibly can and build something in two weeks that helps validate it: get twenty people to a landing page, or find customers on Thumbtack or Craigslist who need a service and message them.
2. **"I won't learn anything, or not enough."** Just try, and write your hypothesis down beforehand: what you think you will learn, what you assume about the business. If you are bad at self-reflection, ask a really smart friend: "What's the highest-likelihood thing that will kill this business? Be brutally honest; I don't care if it hurts." If their answer is weak, find someone who will give better feedback, because you need an honest riskiest assumption. Write it down, test it, and you are guaranteed to learn something.
3. **"I'm scared of the feedback, or of putting out something bad."** Most people are here, and it is normal. The first launch of Udemy took a year and a half because the co-founders were scared; after launch they learned so much more that the year and a half was wasted. Respect yourself and try something as soon as possible.

### The Construction Company Story

A friend at twenty-one wanted to start a construction company without knowing how. He saw a Craigslist ad for a painter and an electrician, bought an electrician's outfit and a tradesman's uniform, and showed up at a commercial job. After being shown everything that needed doing he admitted: "I don't know what I'm doing. I thought I could figure it out, but I can't." The person hiring said: "I respect your honesty. What you're trying to become is a general contractor. Go find some electricians for me and I'll see if you can figure it out." That became a general contracting business making a couple of million a year. It is an extreme (and in construction, dangerous) version, but it shows how far "just start" can go.

### Testing Versus Traction: Where to Spend Time

In any business, the hardest phase is not obvious in advance. For some, getting the product right is the challenge; for others, traction is. Do not assume it is traction; run the tests. If the tests start producing lots of traction (as Sprig's early prototypes did), focus on traction. Work every phase, finish the easy ones quickly, and spend your time on the hard ones. You might spend months or years on traction, or years iterating design and prototypes, depending on the business, but ideally move far faster than your initial assumptions so you do not waste time.

### Treat Any Offering as an MVP

A live course can itself be an MVP with explicit hypotheses. One instructor's hypothesis: how much work is an online course business, do I like it, and does it fit my company? The other's: across models of online education, is live valuable at all, and why (community, which was deliberately excluded and might come later in a tier priced around $1,500 to $2,000; answering questions; or celebrity)? That was around the fourth or fifth version of that MVP. Feedback forms matter more than revenue in that setting.

## 9. Hot Seat Workshops: Three Ideas Turned Into Tests

Each workshop follows the same steps: state the idea in one line, list three or four riskiest assumptions, cut the weak ones, then design the simplest test.

### Workshop 1: Weekly Weight-Loss Accountability for Men 25 to 35

**The idea.** Once-a-week accountability sessions about losing weight for 25 to 35 year old men.

**Riskiest assumptions offered.**

1. People will show up for a once-a-week meeting. (Good.)
2. An accountability meeting will actually help people lose weight. (Good.)
3. People aged 25 to 35 care about losing weight. (Weak: a "gimme." Weight loss is a multi-hundred-billion-dollar market; we know people care.)
4. Replacement for number 3: can you find those people with this product? Discovery of the right user.

**Coaching point.** Assumptions everyone already knows are true are filler; replace them with the ones you genuinely do not know.

**Simplest version.** A weekly Zoom with five people where they weigh in and talk.

**Test design.** A simple Shopify page using AIDA (attention, interest, desire, action):

- **Attention (big H1):** "Lose weight through accountability in the next thirty days."
- **Interest:** "My name is Ben. Over the next thirty days you're going to weigh in with me every single week, and I'm going to help you lose weight."
- **Desire:** holding yourself accountable. Call it shaming or anything else; it is effective.
- **Action:** "Sign up below and try it." Charge money.

With about 500 Twitter followers and about 1,500 Facebook friends mostly in the target market, this could plausibly make five hundred dollars between early afternoon and midnight the same day.

**A stronger variant.** Buy Nokia smart scales (about $150), charge $500 for one month, ship each person a scale, require a daily weigh-in; if they do not lose at least one pound a week, they lose their money, or their lack of progress becomes visible to the group.

**Related precedent.** Someone tweeted an offer to put a blood glucose monitor in your arm: you paid him $1,000, and every time your glucose went above a threshold he kept a bit of it. About 300 people signed up, roughly $30,000 from one tweet. The test was good even if the idea was not (monitors are not mass market), which proves the shape: commitment contracts around health sell.

**Closing lesson.** A category like weight loss has thousands of possible products. It does not matter whether the coach likes the idea; you need more people like the ones who would buy. Your goal is twofold: does the product work (will people show up, will they lose weight), and can you find the target customers and get them to pay?

### Workshop 2: "Figma for Architecture"

**The idea.** Figma turned design software from saved files on one device into live, multiplayer design, like Google Docs did for Word. Apply that to architecture and home design: everyone sees the latest version, updates in real time from any device, clients comment and can even make changes. Current architecture software is like pre-Google-Docs Word. The founder is not from the industry. (One unverified guess named AutoCAD as the incumbent; researching the incumbent is part of the prior research phase.)

**Riskiest assumptions offered.**

1. Do architects have the same pain points Figma solved: would these files benefit massively from real-time design instead of static saved states?
2. Can I sell to them? Is it hard? Are there regulations I do not know? Is 3D far more complex than designing for a 2D screen?
3. How big is the step up from current software: is it worth it (ten times better)?

**Test for 1 and 2.** Copy the incumbent's homepage pattern: make a simple video or GIF showing teams designing together, for architecture. Post it in architecture forums, Facebook groups for architects (by city or discipline) and LinkedIn: "I'm building this. I'd love to talk to architects who have this challenge." Get them on the phone and learn everything about architecture. Aim for five interested architects on calls within a week; if you cannot get five, either the pitch or the idea is wrong. You could post the same day.

**Test for 3 (feasibility, when you are not an engineer).** Find software engineers who have built similar software: mid-career engineers (around five years of experience, clearly strong) at the incumbent, people at Figma, or hotshot engineers in your network. Message them on LinkedIn and ask whether it can be built. You learn two things: which engineers could become your first co-founders (critical for success), and whether they agree it can be built, with a rough estimate of how long the first version would take and what it would look like. This is slower because of cold outreach, and MVPs are harder when the idea is not squarely in your own interest space.

**What to offer in return.** Nothing special is required; people respond more than you expect, especially mid-career professionals who are not inundated with email.

### Workshop 3: Live Video With Celebrities and Experts

**The idea.** Connect regular people with celebrities and experts over live video, whether for opinions on news, expertise in a field, or pure celebrity. The pitch: a culture of "expert lust" and instant gratification; open the app and talk live with a famous person if they happen to be live. Undecided whether it is live-on-demand or scheduled. No target market chosen yet; the comparison offered was Cameo, which skews toward reality TV people and Instagram influencers. Existing competitors (such as Superpeer) exist, which does not by itself kill a test.

**Riskiest assumptions offered.**

1. Scheduling, or getting talent to be available and show up.
2. Will people pay as much as celebrities want to charge?
3. The sessions will be a mess and will not provide value.
4. Building a network matters: an individual with an audience can set this up alone, and MasterClass could open Gordon Ramsay office hours tomorrow.

**The reframe.** The question is not whether this business can work, but whether **you** can start it. If you cannot, find a partner or pick an easier business that fits you. Example: the Udemy founders could never have started MasterClass because they could not get famous people, so they started a marketplace of ordinary instructors; the MasterClass founder could not have started Udemy, because he thought like a marketer, not like someone balancing a two-sided marketplace.

**Unfair advantage check.** The founder had co-founded an influencer marketing platform acquired by Twitter, had influencer connections (a few years old), and an audience of five to ten thousand across channels. That changes the plan.

**Test design.**

1. Get any influencer to do one session, promoted via their Twitter or Instagram, through a link, to learn whether live works or recorded is preferred.
2. Pitch: "I want you to be the first customer. I just want to make you money right now." Give them 100% of the money; it costs you nothing. If it becomes a business, offer equity if needed. An alternative is to cold email or cold DM the celebrity you want directly and offer a flat fee (for example "I will give you five hundred dollars") for a set number of sessions.
3. Build it with no code: a payment page (Gumroad) plus a Zoom link, possibly with a zap between. Each person who pays lands in a Zoom waiting room; refund anyone you cannot get to. (There is also a service built for paid Zoom events, a paywall for virtual coaching or concerts: stream.club.)
4. Define an atomic unit: for example, ten-minute sessions, six of them, because you can get an hour of an influencer's time.
5. Pick three or four "celebrities" in different verticals, post on different channels, see which gets the highest engagement.
6. Deadline: one influencer agreed by Friday; revenue within about another week. (One instructor pushed for at least $100 by Friday.)

**A hypothesis discovered live.** Asked to be the test celebrity, one instructor raised a real objection: people with big audiences can be embarrassed to publicly promote that they are taking money to talk. That is a hypothesis the founder must prove. Conditions that would make it acceptable: make clear it is a test for a course, and donate all the money to charity (for example to underrepresented people), so it looks positive rather than like cashing in.

## 10. Traction, Growth, Scale and Funding

### Traction: The Metric That Matters

**What it is.** The one metric entrepreneurs, innovators, investors and stakeholders all want. Money or profit is not enough, because you can make money this quarter and less next quarter. It has to be a rate, and growth-driven. Traction is not the rate at which you make money but the leading indicators toward making money: "the rate at which a company captures monetizable value."

**Why it works.** The right traction metric is a leading indicator of future revenue and profit; revenue and profit are trailing indicators. If you get the traction metric right, revenue and profit almost take care of themselves. Early on, when you have no revenue or profit to show stakeholders who want growth potential, traction is a good stand-in. A leading indicator is input-oriented: it drives the lagging indicator, but is often hard to measure and easy to influence; lagging indicators are simple to measure and hard to influence.

**Example: reading the "why" behind growth.** Starbucks' balance sheet shows it is growing every quarter, but not how or why. To learn why, study customer behavior (what people do in the store that contributes most to revenue), then design campaigns, policies or experiments that double down on those drivers, and watch the next quarter go up. That loop is traction.

**Example: engagement before ads.** Early Facebook had lots of users hitting the site and engaging heavily. The question: if we put ads in, will engagement drop? Will people click through? They ran experiments with Google ads and found it stuck. Even today their investor story is about user growth (daily active users) rather than advertising growth, because one controls the other: more engaged users and the advertising takes care of itself.

### Throughput Accounting: Chase Dollars, Not Pennies

**What it is.** A concept from the Theory of Constraints (The Goal), originally for factories. On a factory floor people go into cost-cutting mode, but cost-cutting has a floor below which you cannot go. Revenue potential is almost infinite, at least in theory. So yes, care about cost and efficiency, but the bigger potential is upside.

**How to apply it to a business model.** On the value-delivery side (the solution you build), making it as efficient as possible early is "chasing pennies and letting dollars slip through the cracks." Focus first on the revenue stream: maximize things like pricing and identify the right customers. Optimize the cost structure further down the road.

### Growth Versus Profit

**What it is.** Many tech IPOs show negative bottom lines, losses in the millions or billions, and decacorns with crazy valuations still do not turn a profit.

**Why it is not viable forever.** When companies hit product-market fit they face a choice: become very profitable, or become fast-growing. Many investors choose growth over profit, following the idea of getting so big you gain an unfair advantage. Amazon ran very tight margins on the online store for years, but it was diversifying its business model: the store captured market value and drove other units that today contribute most of its success and margins, building a whole stronger than the sum of its parts. Growing for the sake of growth with no end in which it turns into profit is a speculative game, like derivatives and debt markets. Skype grew to a huge valuation, was sold at a big loss and now sits inside another company: a hot potato game. At some point all companies need to turn profitable. Strategies such as capturing "liquidity network effects" (growing a network to full control to capture higher margins later, as in a ride-hailing prospectus) or blitzscaling (prioritizing growth over all else) are risky; at worst they are "narrative currency," clever-sounding terms that people assumed were true, as during the US housing crisis. If wrong, the people who made the bets pay.

A business model always has to create demonstrable and ultimately monetizable value; there are no exceptions (see Section 11). Freemium adds its own risk: with millions or billions of users you still only have an assumption about whether 1% converts to paying, and results are completely different at 3% or 5%. You can model many scenarios and it remains very uncertain; there is no guarantee.

### From Problem/Solution Fit to Scale

**What it is.** A staged path:

1. **Problem/solution fit:** prove a big enough market by finding early adopters. For Tesla's Roadster these were tech entrepreneurs and tech workers interested in an electric car, largely in Silicon Valley. For Facebook, students at Harvard and Ivy League schools.
2. **Product/market fit:** roll out from the small group until the product reaches more and more mainstream customers.
3. **Scale:** grow well beyond that.

### Early Adopters and the Chasm

**What it is.** "Crossing the chasm" is a high-tech startup's move from early adopters to the early majority, and it is difficult.

**Why it matters.** Most companies never even saturate their early adopters, because building something valuable even for early adopters is hard: "you should be so lucky even to have the chasm to jump over." Prepare by making the early adopter group not too narrow: not five or ten people, but big enough that you can build at least a small, self-sustaining version of the business model, giving you room to grow to product-market fit and beyond. Serving early adopters gives you learning and, crucially, social proof, which is what the early majority needs to de-risk the decision: "They don't want to be first but they can happily be second as long as there are enough first people in front of them." Aim for the smallest market that is big enough to make the business sustainable.

### When to Raise Money (and When to Bootstrap)

**What it is.** In an ideal world, raise your big round as you cross product-market fit.

**Why.** Once you have product-market fit, some success is guaranteed; the question is how big it can get. Your goals and the investors' goals are then aligned around growth. You have figured out the product and customer; now it is about engines of growth: how to pump money into paid or viral engines and maximize the potential.

**The realistic picture.** Everyone has a J curve: before profitability and product-market fit you must invest time, money and effort.

- **Bootstrap** to product-market fit if you can: you keep the most control and a lot of say over where the company goes.
- **Venture capital** makes sense when the product requires capital investment up front.
- **Super angels and angel investors** form an increasingly mature market for the pre-product-market-fit stage. They understand you will learn, pivot and course-correct, tend to be more patient, give smaller amounts, and ask for a bigger proportional return than a VC because they take bigger risks.
- **Before launch,** even angels are hard to find. That is where bootstrapping and "the three Fs" (friends, families and fools) come in: seed capital, usually small amounts, to get early validation and early traction.

VCs investing one, two or three million want a high return and an exit or IPO, which is why they always ask how scalable your business model is (Section 12).

### Ramen Profitability

**What it is.** Serial entrepreneur and venture capitalist Paul Graham popularized the term. "Ramen profitable means a startup makes just enough to pay the founders' living expenses." It is a different form of profitability from what startups have traditionally aimed for: traditional profitability means a big bet is finally paying off, whereas the main importance of ramen profitability is that it buys you time.

**Why it works.** Founders can finally pay their living expenses through the startup's revenue generation. This lets founders buy time to keep scaling their startup, and it also lets them leverage their found profitability to scout a better deal from investors, allowing the startup to move to the next stage of growth.

### The Bootstrapping Cycle

**What it is.** The general concept of bootstrapping is "a self-starting process that is supposed to proceed without external input." In business, bootstrapping means financing the growth of the company from the available cash flows produced by a viable business model.

**How the cycle works (numbered as in the source):**

1. Customers
2. Organic growth
3. Cash flows
4. Reinvested in the business
5. Viable business model
6. Better customers
7. Bootstrapping (the loop repeats, now with better customers)

### Pirate Metrics: The AARRR Funnel

**What it is.** Venture capitalist Dave McClure coined the acronym AARRR, a simplified model that shows which metrics and channels to look at at each stage of the users' path toward becoming customers and referrers of a brand.

| Stage | Question | Channels / tools |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition | How do potential customers get to know us? | SEO, SEM, social, blog, email, PR, affiliates, BizDev, apps and widgets, TV and more |
| Activation | How do potential customers get to try our product? | Homepage, landing page, product features |
| Retention | How many potential customers will stick around? | Emails and alerts, system events and time-based features, blogs, content |
| Revenue | How many will actually become customers? | Ads, lead gen, BizDev, subscriptions and more |
| Referral | Will those customers invite other potential customers? | Campaigns, contests, emails and widgets |

Referral loops back to acquisition.

### Eric Ries' Three Engines of Growth

**What it is.** In The Lean Startup, Eric Ries defined the engine of growth as "the mechanism that startups use to achieve sustainable growth." He described sustainable growth with a simple rule: "new customers come from the actions of past customers." Each engine is measured and tracked by a few key metrics, and together they drive Lean Startup growth:

- **Sticky engine:** customer acquisition rate, churn rate, customer retention rate.
- **Viral engine:** viral coefficient.
- **Paid engine:** customer lifetime value versus cost per acquisition.

These are the paid and viral engines referred to above under "When to Raise Money."

### Blitzscaling Business Model Innovation Canvas

**What it is.** A canvas that checks whether a business model can scale extremely fast, organized around four key growth factors and two key growth limiters.

**The 4 key growth factors:**

- **Market size:** How large is the market you're targeting when Blitzscaling? Is it really reachable?
- **Distribution:** Are there existing networks you can leverage on? What viral loops can you create to spread your product/service quickly and at scale?
- **High gross margins:** When you grow your revenues do you generate larger amounts of cash available to finance growth?
- **Network effects:** Is each additional user joining in bringing a positive effect to the whole platform?

**The 2 key growth limiters:** the first is **lack of product/market fit**: Is the market satisfied with your product/service? If not, what's missing? (The second limiter is not legible in the source.)

Compare the warning above under "Growth Versus Profit" that blitzscaling is a risky strategy.

## 11. What a Business Model Is: Definitions and Design Tools

### What a Business Model Is Not

1. **Not a strategy.** Strategy (business or corporate) is about relative performance against the competition: can you outperform competitors? That is not what a business model is.
2. **Not a marketing plan.** Many people see a business model as how you present information to customers and interact with them. That is important, but it is not what a business model fundamentally is.
3. **Not a revenue model.** The most common and pervasive misconception, reinforced by terms like "razor-blade model" and "freemium model." Razor and blade (sell the razor cheap, then sell supplies) and freemium apps (free, then upgrades or in-app purchases) are revenue models: how you generate revenue and set pricing. Useful to keep in mind, but they do not encompass a business model. Startup pitch decks still routinely show value proposition, customer, problem, and then a slide titled "business model" that only talks about how they will earn money.

### More Myths, With Examples

**Myth: a business model is a business plan.** For most people who have not studied the topic, "business model" resonates with "business plan". Google shows this: typing "how to ... business model" suggests "how to create a business model", "how to make a business model", "how to write a business model", "how to build a business model", "how to develop a business model", "how to make a business model for a startup", "how to describe a business model", "how to make business model canvas", "how to design a winning business model" and "how to use business model canvas". Clicking the suggested "how to write a business model" returns (about 523,000,000 results) a featured snippet titled "3 rules for writing a business plan" (keep it short, know your audience, don't be intimidated, executive summary, opportunity, execution, company and management summary, financial plan) and a top result "How to Write a Business Plan [Updated for 2019] | Bplans".

Why Google does this: Google is a commercial search engine that adapts to the search behavior of billions of people. If collectively people think a business model is a business plan, Google may make that true in its results. The consequence for an entrepreneur searching for help: either you are lucky and find the right resource, or you end up writing a hundred-page business plan that won't help much with your business. If anything, it will prevent you from starting it, because you start making things more complicated than they should be.

**Myth: a marketing plan is a business model.** In a marketing plan you structure how you make your clients or potential customers perceive you. That is not a business model.

**Myth: a business model is how you make money (the revenue model, with examples).** How you make money is part of your revenue generation strategy, which is one building block of a business model, but it is not the business model. Airbnb's early pitch deck "Business Model" slide said only "We take a 10% commission on each transaction", then showed 10.6M trips with AB&B (share of market) → $20 average fee ($70/night @ 3 nights) → $200M revenue (2008-2011). WeWork's pre-IPO filing, under "Our Business Model", describes only revenue generation: "We monetize our global platform through a variety of solutions, mainly by selling memberships but also by providing ancillary value-added products and services to our members and extending our platform beyond offering workspace. Currently, we derive the majority of our revenue from recurring monthly membership fees, which accounted for approximately 83% of total revenue during the six months ended June 30, 2019... Our membership and service revenue also includes revenue from business services purchased by our members, such as conference rooms and printing credits, and from commissions on the sale to our members of additional products and services offered by us or in conjunction with partners, which, together, accounted for approximately 5% of total revenue during the six months ended June 30, 2019. The remaining 12% of total revenue during the six months ended June 30, 2019 represented revenue we generated from additional products and services that we deliver through our suite of We Company offerings. These services, which are dedicated to improving our members' experience, allow us to further connect with our members, driving higher retention, which attracts valuable third-party partners who can utilize our platform to reach our large member base."

This is not wrong in context. Startup pitches and financial forms are often also marketing tools meant to communicate and simplify a concept, so if investors ask about your business model and mean "how do you make money", it is fine to simplify. **The pitfall:** if you as the entrepreneur believe a business model is only how you make money, you limit your options, because you will spend all day thinking about monetization strategies instead of taking a more holistic and strategic approach. Business modeling is far more than monetization. It is how you make a great product or service so that customers keep coming back; how you make that product or service scalable; how you keep making financial sense of the business over time; and the value proposition you deliver to key partners, which are a crucial ingredient of success. Business model innovation can be about changing how you charge customers, but it can equally be about other critical aspects of the business that let you scale.

**Myth: technological innovation is business model innovation.** Technology might help build an innovative business model, but a business model starts from problem-solution fit (see also "Disruption Is Business Model Innovation, Not Technology" in Section 13).

**Myth: the best product wins.** When you go online looking for something you are not sure of, you probably land on a white page with a small search box: Google. You get there because Google is an incredible product that finds anything on the web at super-fast speed. But is it the best product, and how do we define best? Google gives relevant results to any question, but it also benefits from network effects: one reason it is good at intercepting search intent is that billions of people use it every day. It also has drawbacks. In one experiment, an SEO expert tried to rank a site written in Latin (a language used by ancient Romans and no longer in use), and it ranked successfully. Google is the most widely used search engine on earth partly because of its distribution strategy: since its scale-up phase, Google aggressively acquired deals that made it the tech giant it is. Few people realize this, and it is easy, especially in tech, to think the best product wins. **A great product with a lack of distribution strategy won't go far.**

**Myth: technology is what gives a competitive advantage (first-mover advantage).** Conventional thinking says "be the first and you'll probably win over time", which is first-mover advantage. Peter Thiel (former CEO of PayPal) pointed out the opposite paradigm, especially in tech: the **last-mover advantage**. Companies that come later can win over existing organizations even when those were the first movers. Google and Facebook were not the first movers in search and social media, yet they dominated. What happened? Business model innovation.

**Myth: business model innovation is just about how you make money.** When Google came out of the Stanford dormitory where two Ph.D. students had invented it, it was a great search engine that many argue was 10x better than competitors. Yet it was not financially successful until, through a couple of years of trial and error, it designed an innovative business model. Google introduced an auction system for advertising aimed at removing the inefficiencies of how advertising had worked for decades. The auction itself was not the primary innovation: another search engine, Overture, was already doing it successfully. Google's innovation was adding a few critical parameters that decided which ads appeared at the top of its text-based ad results. It was not enough to offer a higher bid on a keyword; Google crossed the bid with other parameters so that the ads shown on top were those most relevant and with a higher click-through rate. It sounds trivial now, since the whole web after Google has been built on click-through rate, but it was not back then, and that innovation was critical to Google's hypergrowth, scale and domination (full case in Section 12).

### A Short History of the Term

Economic researchers talked about "models of business" 10 or 20 years before the internet, trying to create process models of how organizations function. The phrase "business model" itself emerged in the late 1980s and early 1990s, almost randomly, as shorthand for value creation and the way an organization works. It picked up steam during the dot-com boom, where it was often used to argue that dot-com companies (many of which turned out to be disasters) did not have to generate value and could operate at a loss indefinitely because they had a "different business model." That was fundamentally wrong. There are no exceptions. Every organization, for-profit or nonprofit, internet company or biotechnology company, whether it sells products or raises money and gets acquired before it ever brings a product to market, has to create demonstrable and ultimately monetizable value. That is the core concept: every organization must have a viable business model to survive. The same pattern returned with later tech IPOs that generate large revenues but no profit, such as Uber and Lyft, where people bank on long-term value.

The phrase itself barely existed before the second half of the twentieth century. On the Google Books Ngram Viewer, "business model" is flat from 1800 onward, first appears around the 1950s-60s, and then rises almost vertically from the 1990s into the 2000s. (The spoken source dates the phrase to the late 1980s and early 1990s; the Ngram chart shows the first traces earlier and the steep rise from the 1990s.) Google Trends for the topics "Lean startup" and "Business Model Canvas" (United States, 2004 to present, web search) shows both near zero until around 2009, then a sharp rise, with "Business Model Canvas" overtaking "Lean startup" in later years.

### Definitions Over Time

The concept evolved through a series of landmark statements. Keep them verbatim, since each marks a shift in thinking:

- **Michael Porter, 1979 (strategy era, competitor focus):** "The state of competition in an industry depends on five basic forces... The collective strength of these forces determines the ultimate profit potential of an industry. It ranges from intense in industries like tires, metal cans, and steel, where no company earns spectacular returns on investment, to mild in industries like oil field services and equipment, soft drinks, and toiletries, where there is room for quite high returns." (Harvard Business Review, "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy", March 1979.)
- **HBR, 1993:** business modeling as competitive advantage ("Strategy and the Art of Reinventing Value").
- **Amit and Zott, 2001:** "A business model depicts the content, structure, and governance of transactions designed so as to create value through the exploitation of business opportunities."
- **Marc Andreessen (a16z):** "The only thing that matters is product/market fit." His description of what product/market fit feels like: "The customers are buying the product just as fast as you can make it, or usage is growing just as fast as you can add more servers. Money from customers is piling up in your company checking account. You're hiring sales and customer support staff as fast as you can. Reporters are calling because they've heard about your hot new thing and they want to talk to you about it. You start getting entrepreneur of the year awards from Harvard Business School. Investment bankers are staking out your house. You could eat free for a year at Buck's."
- **Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur, 2010:** "A business model describes the rationale of how an organization creates, delivers, and captures value."
- **Steve Blank, 2013:** "A Lean Startup favors experimentation over elaborate planning, customer feedback over intuition, and iterative design over traditional 'big design up front' development."
- **Fred Wilson, AVC, July 2019:** "I believe business model innovation is more disruptive than technical innovation."
- **Business Model Navigator (BMI Lab):** "A business model is an holistic, repeatable and scalable way to create and capture value."

### A Multi-Faceted Concept

**What it is.** "Business model" has no single correct definition. The concept adapts to the context it sits in and to the objective of the person using it, and it is fine to have multiple definitions based on each practitioner's goal. A concept carried across several fields will take on different meanings.

**Why it matters.** If you know which meaning someone is using, you avoid talking past them (an investor asking about your "business model" usually means something narrower than a strategist does), and you avoid adopting a narrow meaning that limits your own options.

The main meanings in circulation:

- **Analysts use business models to produce financial analyses.** Business modeling is a technique to dissect any organization and business, used by analysts and business people trying to understand those businesses. Business and financial analysts use it to better understand tech companies, in order to give investment recommendations, financial reviews and investment advice.
- **Academics study business models to classify things.** For academics a business model might be just a holistic way to describe a business. Their purpose can seem more rigorous than an entrepreneur's: the academic has to prove the business has certain features that make it different from or similar to other businesses, and from those features derive classifications. As those classifications become more and more complex, they only live in "theory land". The research does not necessarily have a practical purpose; its goal is uncovering universal classification systems for things in the real world, and as such it can lose practical application.
- **Most people confuse business models with business plans** (see the myths above).
- **Startups confuse business models with monetization strategies** (see the myths above).
- **Entrepreneurs use business modeling as an experimentation mindset** to test assumptions quickly and cheaply (see "Business Modeling Is an Experimentation Mindset" in Section 12).

### Product, Business Model, Organization: Three Nested Layers

A company can be pictured as three concentric circles: **Product** at the center, wrapped by the **Business Model**, wrapped by **Organizational Design/Culture**. The product alone is not the business. The business model is the layer that makes the product commercially viable, and the organization and culture are what let the business model be executed and changed.

### The Fundamental Myth

The most concerning myth: that you can craft a business model on paper (a napkin, a canvas), evaluate it, and know whether a company will succeed. Because a business model is shorthand for value creation and how the organization functions, it is tempting to think writing it down lets you predict long-term viability. It does not. The canvas's real value is surfacing assumptions for experiments (Section 3). A related myth: that you can have a business model that never has to generate profit.

### Definitions That Help

There has been no agreement on a precise definition in about 25 years of academic research, and academics did not think about business models the way managers and entrepreneurs do. Useful definitions:

- **Academic (Zott and Amit, 2001):** the first major study defined a business model as "the content, structure and governance of transactions" designed "to create value." (Full quote under "Definitions Over Time" above.) Non-obvious and very academic, but by limiting it carefully to how the organization manages transactions internally and externally, it becomes much simpler to assess what a business model is or is not. That definition did not survive; ten years later its authors moved to other, activity-based definitions.
- **Entrepreneurial:** "the design of the organization to exploit an opportunity." It captures the configuration of the business, how things tie together, all focused on generating value where there was none before.
- **Canvas-era working definition:** over the last five to six years, largely due to the Business Model Canvas, a business model has come to mean, more vaguely, a set of activities, resources and processes that create value. Helpful and aligned with the canvas, but vague enough that it gets stretched to include business strategy, which blurs things.
- **Value creation and capture:** a business model has two purposes: create value for a customer, and capture some of that value for itself in revenues and profits, or it does not survive. Who you capture value from is very important, and it need not be the same party you create value for. Supermarkets create value for shoppers (products at reasonable prices), but many make more money from manufacturers via slotting fees (rent for shelf placement) than from margins on the products sold.
- **The four Ws (who, what, how, why):**
  1. **Who** is the customer? You must be able to describe the customer; business model innovation can mean a new customer or a new customer problem.
  2. **What** do you offer? Products and services, plus the benefits you create and problems you solve. Who and what must fit: you solve a problem the customer has.
  3. **How** do you deliver? Key partners, key activities, key resources.
  4. **Why** does it make money? Not only revenue but also purpose, and the profit formula: why do you earn more than your costs? The revenue model is just one element of the why.

**Three things to keep in mind whatever definition you use:** it is a set of elements (activities, resources, processes); all of them link to value creation; and it is about design, how the organization functions. Keep those in mind and you can work with the available canvases without a perfect definition.

### Choosing a Design Tool by Stage

1. **Business Model Canvas:** a phenomenal tool, but for an early-stage venture, or when you only have an opportunity and no company, it asks for information you do not have yet.
2. **Lean Canvas:** built on top of the Business Model Canvas, very focused on the customer problem and the solution, intuitive, handy for very early stage teams at the initial stage of launching.
3. **RTVN (Resources, Transactions, Value, Narrative):** for even earlier, when you have just come upon an idea and even the Lean Canvas (channels, customer relationships, cost structures) asks for more depth than you have. An extremely simple way to start:
   - **Resources:** what essential resources will you need?
   - **Transactions:** what transactions take place? Who do you interact with: suppliers, customers, partners?
   - **Value:** what creates value, and how is value created and then captured? These are not necessarily the same thing.
   - **Narrative:** the story that ties it all together.

The workshop's map of the discipline puts **Mindset** first, then **Tools** (Business Model Canvas, Lean Canvas), then **Business Model Innovation** with its tree of models and experiments (Section 12). The toolkit combines three canvases: **The Business Model Canvas** (Strategyzer), the **Value Proposition Canvas** used for **Customer Insights** (Strategyzer), and the **Lean Canvas** (Lean Canvas is adapted from The Business Model Canvas and is licensed under the Creative Commons Attribution-Share Alike 3.0 Un-ported License).

### Lean Canvas: The Boxes and the Order to Fill Them

**What it is.** A one-page business model for startups (source: blog.leanstack.com). Each box, with its prompt:

- **Problem:** list your customers' top 3 problems.
- **Existing Alternatives:** list how these problems are solved today.
- **Solution:** outline a possible solution for each problem.
- **Key Metrics:** list key numbers telling how your business is doing today.
- **Unique Value Proposition:** single, clear, compelling message that turns an unaware visitor into an interested prospect.
- **High-Level Concept:** list your X for Y analogy (e.g., YouTube = Flickr for videos).
- **Unfair Advantage:** something that can't be easily copied or bought.
- **Channels:** list your path to customers.
- **Customer Segments:** list your target customers and users.
- **Early Adopters:** list characteristics of your ideal customer.
- **Cost Structure:** list your fixed and variable costs.
- **Revenue Streams:** list your sources of revenue.

**How to fill it (numbered order):**

1. Customer Segments
2. Problem
3. Unique Value Proposition
4. Solution
5. (Channels is not numbered in the source image. Unverified: it is step 5 in the standard Lean Canvas order.)
6. Revenue Streams
7. Cost Structure
8. Key Metrics
9. Unfair Advantage

**Start with problem and customer segments.** Fill these two boxes first, as a pair, using four questions:

- Who is the customer?
- What are your early adopter's top 3 problems?
- How do they solve the problem today? (existing alternatives)
- How will you qualify/identify early adopters?

### Value Proposition Canvas: Two Ways to Start

**What it is.** The Value Proposition Canvas has a value map (products and services, gain creators, pain relievers) on the left and a customer profile (customer jobs, gains, pains) on the right. You can start from either side (source: Strategyzer, "Starting with the customer: value proposition design").

**Start from customer insights.** Begin on the customer side: collect jobs, pains and gains (sticky notes labeled Job, Pain, Gain) and feed them into the customer profile, then design the value map to fit.

**Start from an asset or opportunity.** Begin on the value map side: start from something you already have, a **tech**, a **capability** or a **patent**, and push it into the products and services, then look for customers whose jobs, pains and gains it fits.

### The Business Model Narrative

**What it is.** Part organizational story, part simple description of how you create value, part what makes you unique. Tie it explicitly to RTVN: in some simple way it should encompass the essential resources, the key transactions, and how value is created and captured.

**Why it works.** Jump ahead 50 years and ask how business models changed management: the most important thing may be the ability to use a business model to tell a straightforward, clear story of why an organization will succeed. Humans think in narratives and automatically create stories about everything, which makes a story a compelling way to communicate quickly to employees, partners, investors, customers or suppliers. Many entrepreneurs think about it only when building pitches; make it explicit and make sure it matches exactly what your underlying model does.

**How to do it.** Whichever tool you use (RTVN, Lean Canvas, Business Model Canvas), keep asking: what is the underlying story? It does not need to be perfect. Return to it constantly: "Is this actually what the business does, and do all the elements in this canvas tie back to it?"

### SHaRP Resources

**What it is.** A variation of the long-standing resource-based view of the firm (the VRIN framework), kept deliberately distinct from strategy. Organizations gain advantages and succeed because of the resources within them: human capital, financial capital, physical assets, capabilities, skills, knowledge. SHaRP gives a simple way to assess them:

- **Specific:** people, assets, or the way money is used, specific to that opportunity in that industry.
- **Hard to copy:** if anybody can access them, your advantage is limited in time or in geographic scope.
- **Rare:** once you access them, other organizations cannot as easily generate them.
- **Precious:** valuable in some specific way.

**How to use it.** The more of these characteristics a resource has, the more it contributes to the business model's success. A very specific individual adds a lot of value; if that person also has unique experience and capabilities that no one else has built, hard to copy, rare and precious, that person, asset, capability or skill is more likely to keep the business model viable long-term.

### The Customer Journey Map

**What it is.** A map, diagram or even a narrative of every step your customer takes: from first becoming aware of your innovation, product or company; through education, purchase and post-purchase support; until they become a long-term customer and even refer other customers to you. Not a new invention; at least a dozen templates exist online.

**Why it works.** It shows every interaction you have with the customer and gives a clear, testable process for everything the business model has to do to succeed. Surprisingly few entrepreneurs build one alongside their business model, though the two go hand in hand: once you know the journey, recognizing the critical places where you create and capture value becomes dramatically easier.

### Three Obstacles to a Great Business Model

1. **Unstated assumptions.** Assumptions stay in the back of your head, drive your thinking, and never get written down, so never get tested (Section 3).
2. **Not looking beyond the surface.** All the pieces seem to link up and you leave it there. Low-cost airlines (Southwest, Ryanair) obviously do not serve champagne. But Southwest spends more on training than most airlines, which does not seem to fit a low-cost model until you look deeper: mistakes in airlines (a bag on the wrong flight, a delayed flight) are extremely costly, so training is ultimately a low-cost activity because it returns more than it costs over the long haul.
3. **Missing the value needed for the customer relationship.** Where does the customer's need match the value you create? Use the customer journey map, or any tool linking customer needs to value creation, to make the connection explicit.

## 12. Business Model Innovation: Patterns, Freemium and Scalability

### Business Model Innovation Is Iteration

Business model innovation is not developing a brilliant idea and going with it. It is a lot of testing and iteration: tweaking the model to find the right fit, changing many things until the pieces come together. Before a business model becomes viable it may take years of experimentation and testing.

### Business Modeling Is an Experimentation Mindset

**What it is.** Business model design is not about sketching a plan on a piece of paper but a mindset of experimentation. In business modeling you manufacture experiments (business models and business model variations) that let the entrepreneur test the assumptions around the business quickly, cheaply and with minimum effort. Business model innovation is at the same time a mindset, a framework and a set of tools for entrepreneurs to build relevant businesses in today's marketplace.

**Why it works.** Business modeling does three things for an entrepreneur: it gives a better understanding of the businesses around you; it gives insight into how companies work at a holistic level, which makes you a better digital entrepreneur; and it lets you test the assumptions around the business you are trying to build, reducing the time and money spent on a project that was doomed to fail.

**How to do it.**

1. List the assumptions your business depends on.
2. Start testing from the riskiest assumptions (as practitioners like Ash Maurya highlight): those for which the business might not become sustainable over time. Monetization strategy and key customer understanding are among the riskiest and need to be tested quickly.
3. Build one or more business model variations and design experiments against each. The **Business Model Innovation tree** shows the structure: Business Model Innovation branches into Business Model One (Experiment A, Experiment B) and Business Model Two (Experiment C, Experiment D). (Compare "Cast a Wider Net: Multiple Model Variants" in Section 3.)
4. Use the results to move the product or service in line with the market.

### What Business Model Innovation Is, and Why It Creates Moats

**What it is.** Business model innovation is about increasing the success of an organization with existing products and technologies by crafting a compelling value proposition able to propel a new business model to scale up customers and create a lasting competitive advantage. An alternative phrasing: creating a compelling value proposition that allows an organization to scale up customers with a better operating model. It all starts by mastering the key customers.

**Why it works.** As technology becomes a commodity over time, creating a lasting advantage requires business model understanding, experimentation and execution. At its core, business model innovation is a subtle change. Because it is hard to dissect from the outside (in many cases it is detected only after an organization has achieved massive success), it is also hard to copy. In a world where technology has partly become a commodity, that difference is huge. What gives a competitive advantage is no longer technology alone, but technology paired with an innovative business model.

**Pitfall.** A business model is not static. Even though a business model creates a long-term competitive advantage, it requires continuous tweaking to make sure all its pieces come together. How Google's business model looks today will probably have evolved in a few years.

### Why Business Model Innovation Matters Now

**From competitor focus to customer focus.** For decades strategy looked at competition, with frameworks like Porter's Five Forces (Section 13). Business model innovation shifted the focus from competition to customers. Today business model innovation has become more important than technical innovation. Saying the focus has moved to customers does not mean you don't need to understand your competition. It means you start from customers and the problems they face, and only after that move to competition and the existing alternatives.

### Why Incumbents Miss the Opportunity

Every new business model starts in a **gap**: between **existing players** and a **new value proposition** sits an **opportunity**, a point of customer dissatisfaction the incumbents are not serving. An incumbent leaves the gap open for one of three reasons:

- It does not see the opportunity.
- It can't see it.
- It does not align with its business model.

**Example: DuckDuckGo versus Google.** Google tracks users. Some users think "I want privacy from Google!" That is where Google's value proposition fails ("I can't give you privacy"). DuckDuckGo built a business model based on privacy, with the value proposition "I can surf the web by protecting my privacy!" It makes money in two ways that don't need tracking:

- **Untracked advertising:** when a user types a search, DuckDuckGo shows an ad based only on that search term. If you type "car", DuckDuckGo shows a car ad. That doesn't involve tracking because it is based on the keyword and not the person.
- **Untracked affiliate marketing:** when a user visits Amazon and eBay through DuckDuckGo and subsequently makes a purchase, DuckDuckGo receives a small commission. This mechanism operates anonymously, and no personally identifiable information is exchanged between DuckDuckGo and Amazon or eBay.

Google cannot easily copy this because privacy does not align with a business model built on tracking-powered advertising.

### The Questions to Ask

There is no single path to business model innovation, but there are critical questions. To innovate a business model, think about how to tweak and redesign your value chain, cost structure, key partners and, in general, what can help you scale:

- How can I design a better value chain?
- Can I improve the existing cost structure?
- What is the distribution channel that can accelerate growth?
- Why is my company experiencing bottlenecks in certain areas?
- Is the organizational structure helping the company to grow as it should?

### Three Paths Toward Business Model Innovation

Sometimes you can design a business model from your previous experience in an industry; other times you have to figure it out along the way.

**Path 1: Engineer an innovative business model from scratch.**

*What it is.* Designing the business model deliberately before launch, using deep prior knowledge of the space. This is the work of a **Business Engineer**: a hybrid between a business administration and technology expert, a person with the business acumen and engineering abilities to understand a complex organization, devise solutions and work as a liaison between commercial and technical teams.

*Why it works.* As Reid Hoffman points out in his book Blitzscaling, business model innovation is a key ingredient of success, especially in digital, where countless companies offer innovative tools. Prior domain knowledge lets you design the model instead of stumbling onto it.

*Example.* Reid Hoffman: "This is what happened when I co-founded LinkedIn. The key business model innovations for LinkedIn, including the two-way nature of the relationships and filling professionals' need for a business-oriented online identity, didn't just happen organically." He used his understanding of the social networking world (he had founded a social network called SocialNet) to design LinkedIn's business model. LinkedIn was acquired in 2016 by Microsoft for $26.2 billion. The two-way nature is visible in LinkedIn's model: it serves **HR Managers** (LinkedIn Hiring) and **Professionals** (Learning & Development) on the same platform.

*Supporting data (LinkedIn profiles with the title "business engineer").* Top companies: IBM 41384, Accenture 33973, Microsoft 31266, Oracle 23065, Cisco 18572, Amazon 18398, Google 14344, Amazon Web Services 8613, Salesforce 7068, Facebook 5334. Top universities: University of Mumbai 38335, Stanford University 22114, University of California, Berkeley 21750, Massachusetts Institute of Technology 19595, Politecnico di Milano 16229, INSEAD 10884, Harvard Business School 10433, Politecnico di Torino 9140, Sapienza Università di Roma 5824. *Pitfall.* Designing a business model beforehand isn't always possible. In some cases you need to experiment, reiterate and find it.

**Path 2: Find an innovative business model along the way.**

*What it is.* Discovering the model through trial and error after the product exists.

*Example.* When Google was scaling up, it didn't figure it all out right away. Although it had incredible technology and product, it lagged in business model innovation. When it finally figured it out after a few trials and errors (Google was running out of investment money), it was a massive success. When it listed in 2004 and had to show its numbers, it already made over three billion in revenues, a 155x growth in about four years.

*How to do it.* Try and test many things before you can say you have a business model that scales sustainably and makes financial sense. If you find it, you have created a long-lasting competitive advantage.

**Path 3: Use business model innovation as a survival mechanism.**

*What it is.* An established leader experimenting with new models to protect itself from threats to its current one.

*Example.* Uber has been investing in self-driving cars since 2015. Why would a company dominating an entire space do this? First, if self-driving cars become mass-adopted, Uber would be out of business, so to thrive in the next era it needs to be on top of that game. Second, business model innovation: among its key partners, Uber has drivers across the world, and those drivers also pose a significant threat to Uber's success. Even if Uber pulls the plug on or spins off its self-driving business, the example shows a company that never stops experimenting. Business units like Uber Eats, Express Pool and Freight are all attempts to tweak an existing business model until it allows the company to become financially sustainable at massive scale.

### Is Business Model Innovation for Anyone?

In theory, yes. **Small-business example:** a small consulting business in a traditional industry where most competitors charge by the hour stops doing that and instead charges only a small retainer plus a success fee. This model might not be financially viable at the beginning, but it might wipe out competitors over time: with a larger customer base, the retainer becomes an essential revenue base and the success fees become the scalable part of the business.

**Pitfall.** In other cases business model innovation requires massive financial resources. A business that decides to dominate an existing industry by introducing an innovative business model growing at a reckless pace may attract investors looking for high returns, but it may also burn a lot of cash.

**Key takeaway.** Business model innovation is a popular topic and therefore surrounded by misconceptions. Business modeling has a meaning based on why you are using it; it is a dynamic concept, at the apex of its evolution, and can carry different meanings and interpretations.

### Case: A Freemium Textbook Startup (Lessons Learned)

Around 2008 a startup (PaperC GmbH) built a freemium model for digital textbooks: you paid only for what you saw. The model changed several times. What went wrong:

1. **Vanity metrics and the wrong incentives.** Investors looked at users and active users, not conversion rates, so the team was motivated to grow the free part. Later investors said "now we want to see revenues," and the team had to scramble to convert free users to paying. The problem was the wrong KPI incentives more than the model itself.
2. **A cumbersome pricing mechanism.** Pay-per-page required users to upload credits before buying, which was inconvenient. The team later wanted a Spotify-style freemium (free tier plus premium subscription).
3. **Fixing the model too early.** By then they had hundreds of contracts on the old model, so changing it was not easy. "We bet on the wrong horse and fixed the business model too early. We should have been much more flexible." This was 2008 to 2009, before lean startup thinking spread.

**General point on freemium.** Freemium can scale marketing because you gain many users quickly, but not every user or lead ever converts, and many freemium startups still run at net losses. It is a harder model than many believe. Example of what it actually takes: Slack had over half a million free users, yet about 40% of its 2019 revenue came from 575 customers, largely enterprises converted through a direct sales force. Freemium builds brand and can make you a standard, but you still need a sales force to find the commercially valuable users.

### Freemium: Does It Make Sense? (Decision Flowchart)

A freemium pattern can also be used as a growth tool (to the extent the company uses it to amplify its brand) and as a marketing funnel where enough non-paying customers can be converted into customers. Use this workflow to decide whether to implement it:

1. **Do we have extra resources to sustain a free offering?**
   - No → focus on mastering your key customers.
   - Yes → go to 2.
2. **Will the freemium offering cannibalize the existing key customers?**
   - Yes → focus on mastering your key customers.
   - No → go to 3.
3. **Is the freemium offering in line with my business model?**
   - Yes → define a solid freemium offering → measure impact on existing key customers → if not affected and getting viral growth → **Go!**
   - No → go to 4.
4. **Do we have alternative growth tools?**
   - Yes → develop alternative growth tools.
   - No → consider a free offering with complementary features.

### Business Model Patterns

**What it is.** A framework such as the four-W model is an "empty canvas" that you fill with an existing model or a vision. A business model pattern is on a different level: a recurring logic seen across very different business models, products and industries, always working for the same reason. When a logic is transferable, it is a pattern, and you can use it as a blueprint or recipe for your own model. More than 55 patterns have been identified.

**Example: razor and blade.** Named after Gillette, which has sold razors and blades for more than 100 years with the same logic: cheap razors, very expensive blades, lock-in because only official blades fit the razor, and high margins on the blades. The same logic describes printers and cartridges (developed since the 1980s), coffee capsules (Nespresso), and medical devices such as in diabetes care. Very different industries (medical, coffee, razors), same recurring logic.

**Innovation by combination.** A business model is typically a combination of several patterns. Analyses of famous business model innovators (such as Tesla or Uber) find at least three, four, five or six patterns combined in a very smart way. You do not need a wholly new idea; recombining patterns creates a new recipe. Getting the mix right can take several years, but a good mix of patterns is what makes them strong.

**A second framing.** A business model pattern is a blueprint for creating new ideas and achieving business model innovation as a means to create a lasting competitive advantage. The BMI Lab's Business Model Navigator identifies 60 business model patterns, from the research of Oliver Gassmann, Karolin Frankenberger and Michaela Csik. (The spoken source says "more than 55"; the slide source says 60. Unresolved which count is current.) There isn't a single way to define business model patterns, and in some cases a pattern can also be a growth tool or a revenue strategy (Freemium is the example above). Without getting bogged down in definitions and classifications, what matters is knowing how to use validated business systems and heuristics that help grow your business.

### Business Model Scalability: Two Dimensions

**What it is.** The venture capital question: how scalable is your business model? It can mean different things (expanding to new countries, more users, more profitably), so break it into two dimensions:

- **External scalability:** can the model scale to many users globally? Is this a global need? Are there limitations such as regulation?
- **Internal scalability:** how efficiently can you grow the customer base? Do you need a lot of investment and bound capital, or is it light (software: initial investment, then no further investment to grow)? This is largely about digitization: physical assets versus digital assets.

**How to use it.** Plot a business on both metrics:

- **Upper right (perfectly scalable):** digital platforms such as Facebook and Google. The world's most valuable companies are also the most scalable business models.
- **Lower left (least scalable):** 100- or 200-year-old models such as the village butcher or bakery: fixed to one location, physical, needing many people to scale, not digitized at all, and before franchising not scalable.

**Example.** Uber is less scalable than Google: even without owning cars, it needs many physical assets and many more people, and regulation differs city by city, limiting external scalability. (Inference in the source: this may change if heavy regulation arrives for digital and media platforms, which have scaled partly because regulation has been light.)

**Business model.** Amazon has a diversified business model. In 2018 it posted over $232 billion in revenues and over $10 billion in net profits. 5. Repeated many times over for more than two decades, this let Amazon expand to sell anything and create other businesses (AWS, Prime and physical stores) that run at high profitability compared to the online store.

**Business model: hidden revenue generation.** Users don't pay for the service. Google's revenue streams come from businesses bidding on keywords searched by users. Three sides, each with its own value proposition:

## 13. Disruption by Decoupling the Customer Value Chain

### The Common Pattern Behind "Doing It Differently"

Research on the economics of attention (the market for attention: suppliers of attention are people; buyers are companies that buy it from media for advertising and brand building) led to visiting fast-growing disruptors such as Facebook (in 2010), Uber, Airbnb and Netflix and asking each "how are you planning to disrupt this industry?" Every one answered "we're doing it very differently from the others." The more that was heard, the clearer it became they were doing virtually the same thing, the same strategy, applied in very different ways to their industries: a common recipe for digital disruption that works across every industry and country observed.

### The Customer Value Chain

**What it is.** A framework for all the steps or activities customers go through to acquire products and services. It can be mapped for any customer: an individual, a business, or a government.

**Example: buying a TV.**

1. Recognize you need a new TV.
2. Think of a few places to look for options.
3. Go to a major retailer (in the US, Best Buy).
4. Look at all options and screen a few ("a 50-inch TV under $1,000").
5. Get someone to help, figure out which you like (say a Samsung 50-inch).
6. Ask for it, pay for it, take it home, install it, use it.
7. A few years later, dispose of it: resell it or throw it away.

### Decoupling

**What it is.** Disruptive startups were not trying to steal customers from incumbents in the traditional sense. They chose **one activity** in the customer value chain that customers were not fully satisfied with, and did it much better, instead of trying to replace everything the incumbent does.

**Why it works.** You do not have to out-build the incumbent across the board. You specialize in the weak link, deliver better value for that activity, and capture business value away from the incumbent.

**Examples.**

- **Airbnb** did not build hotels to steal customers from Marriott or the Ritz Carlton. It improved matchmaking between people with homes to rent and people looking not just for rooms but for the experience of staying in someone's home. Matchmaking was the key activity it chose.
- **Birchbox** versus Sephora (the big beauty retailer): pay $10 a month and get four or five samples of beauty products at home, so you do not need to go to the store to sample and find new products. Afterwards you can buy the full-size item at Sephora, Amazon or anywhere. Birchbox did not take all of Sephora's activities, just one: sampling.
- **Drybar:** US hair salons cut, wash and dry hair; they cut well but do not dry hair very well. Drybar does just the drying, faster, more convenient to book and cheaper, with little technology, and now has hundreds of stores across the US.

### Disruption Is Business Model Innovation, Not Technology

We are infatuated with technology. Some giants did win through new technology (Apple, Google), but for the vast majority of startups considered "tech companies," what started their disruption (stealing large market share in a short period) was not a major technology.

**Example: early Uber.** When it launched (2008 per the source), you downloaded an app and texted or called to request a car to the airport. On the other end, a person received the text or call and phoned black-car companies (high-end limousines), just like a taxi dispatcher: "Can you go pick up this person?" They replied by text or email that a car was on its way. Probably the only innovation was seeing a little car on a map coming to you. Routing by algorithm came later. It grew because it was convenient and customers had assurance their car was coming, not because of technology nobody else had. It was fundamentally a better customer service and a new business model: you did not pay as the car left, and you avoided the hassle of hailing a cab.

**Pitfall.** If you start with technology first, you end up hunting for a place to use something complicated, instead of starting from a customer pain point in an activity.

### The Three Waves of Digital Disruption

Disruption does not come continuously. It comes in big discrete chunks: at one moment many markets get disrupted or the process starts, then the wave subsides, then another comes.

1. **Unbundling (about 1994 to 1999/2000): breaking the product.** Before, you bought a newspaper as a bundle (articles, restaurant reviews, classified ads, comic strips); you could not buy just one. A CD 20 years ago forced you to buy 15 tracks. Textbooks and other media were the same. The internet let companies unbundle: Google provided one article at a time, Craigslist just the classifieds, Yelp just restaurant reviews. Startups broke products into pieces and sold them or gave them away free.
2. **Disintermediation (about 1999/2000 to 2005): breaking the supply chain.** Companies that sold through intermediaries went direct. Travel agents packaged hotels, airlines and tourism activities; airlines and hotels started selling direct to consumers. Cut out the middleman.
3. **Decoupling (the last few years, from about 2006 and ongoing): breaking the customer value chain.** Not breaking the product or the supply chain, but breaking the customer's own chain of activities to deliver part of the value.

### What Drives Mass Switching: Cost in Three Currencies

**What it is.** Industries believe customers want different, specific things (quality cars, quality hotels, quality furniture). But what drives disruptive decisions, where customers en masse change purchase habits dramatically in a short time, is ultimately cost: getting what they want at the lowest cost possible. Cost is not just money; it is the entire cost of acquiring the product or service. Customers have three pockets of currency:

- **Monetary:** money.
- **Time:** the time to search for a hotel, or to receive a package ordered online.
- **Effort:** how effortful it is to compare and acquire the TV you want, or to get a car to your door to take you to the airport.

Decisions change toward whoever reduces these three costs the most. Airbnb reduced them; Uber reduced them; Amazon tries every day in every market to reduce them.

### The Declining Value of Brand Loyalty

Brands still matter and help reduce risk in some choices. What is new and a game changer is the reduction in the value of branding. People who liked Nestle chocolate always bought Nestle; people who liked BMW kept buying BMW. Now people try new brands they do not know or have not experienced. Why would anyone try Uber at first? There was no reason and real risk. Yet it grew very fast. Startups exploit the fact that people want better value (cheaper, faster, easier) and will experiment with new companies offering a better experience in buying, using, disposing of or repurchasing, giving up the safety net of large established brands like Nike, Coca Cola or IBM. Big businesses can no longer rest on decades of highly recognizable, high-equity brands; consumer-driven innovation is now a necessary condition to stay a leader. Dollar Shave Club disrupted Gillette's long-dominant razor-and-blade model in a very short time despite Gillette's strong brand.

### Beyond Porter's Five Forces

The five forces (published 1985, later a sixth force) explained competitive drivers in markets with many buyers and few suppliers and competitors, where competitors dictated profitability more than customers, so tracking and responding to competitors mattered more than consumers. Over the past 30 years many markets became saturated with suppliers (once only Gillette and Schick; now many razor brands). Tracking competitors and understanding industry forces is now only the first step. What makes you succeed is being customer-focused: understanding customers' priorities and delivering them so well that competitors matter less to your profitability than shifting consumer trends. Customers want specialized improvement in costs.

**The five forces in detail.** Porter's Five Forces Model helps organizations gain a better understanding of their industries and competition; the slide source dates its first publication to Professor Michael Porter's book "Competitive Strategy" in the 1980s (with the forces first set out in the 1979 HBR article quoted in Section 11). It breaks industries and markets down by analyzing them through five forces: competition in the industry (rivalry among existing competitors, at the center); potential of new entrants into the industry (threat of new entrants); power of suppliers (bargaining power of suppliers); power of customers (bargaining power of buyers); and threat of substitute products or services. Caveat on the shift to customers: it does not mean you don't need to understand your competition; you start from customers and the problems they face, and only after that move to competition and the existing alternatives.

**Customer centricity is not being nice.** It is not sending thank-you emails. "Customers don't want a relationship with companies. Customers want products and services that are cheaper, faster, more convenient and better than the alternative." Wherever you do not see a good product or service, can you make it better? That is the way to enter a market: decouple and do something small but much better.

### How to Start a Disruptive Company: The Five-Step Recipe

**Why specialize.** Otherwise you compete with big companies that have thousands of employees and billions of dollars. There is no way to win unless you specialize and see their weak points.

**How to do it.**

1. **Map the customer value chain** for a product or service you, as a consumer or B2B customer, are not fully satisfied with.
2. **Classify the activities:** those that create value, those that capture value, and those that erode value. Incumbents require customers to do a series of activities; some are good, some are not. The not-good ones are your beachhead, your entry gate into the market.
3. **Choose one and decouple it:** break the links around that activity so you can steal it from the incumbent.
4. **Increase the specialization forces:** reduce the monetary, time and effort costs of that activity for the customer, often by delivering it digitally, which generally lowers the customer's cost. You will start stealing customer activities from incumbents.
5. **Anticipate and preempt incumbents' responses.** Large companies that lose activities will respond; they have typical responses, so evaluate them and preempt them before they happen. That creates a sustainable business model.

### What Comes Next: Coupling

After decoupling, you grow by adjacent stealing: coupling new activities to your decoupled business model. Alibaba started in e-commerce, then moved into payments, then logistics, then search engines, then software and hardware. Amazon and Airbnb do the same. The next wave is coupling: companies starting in one (digital) industry and moving into very different industries by coupling activities in a smart way that benefits the customers they already serve.

## 14. Digital Transformation for Established Businesses

### Four Myths

1. **It is a technology problem,** solved by picking the right technology implementation.
2. **It is a project.** CEOs ask whether it is a three-year or four-year project and what budget to set aside. In reality it is evolving into a different way of operating as a business, not something run out of the project management office on a three-year timeline. It is a mindset shift.
3. **It is digitizing what you already do:** take the existing business, automate processes, add machinery. That may be part of it, but you miss much of the scope.
4. **It is impossible** (a view heard in parts of Silicon Valley): old companies are dinosaurs that will never change, and only digital natives (the Ubers, Googles and Netflixes) can succeed. Not true.

### Definition: A Question

**What it is.** "How does a business that started and grew to scale before the internet need to adapt in order to grow in the digital age? How does it need to evolve if it's going to reach its next stage of growth in the digital economy?"

**Why it differs from starting a startup.** A startup asks how to understand the market, customer needs, changing technologies and emerging possibilities, and how to envision a new offering. An existing enterprise has advantages (customers, brand reputation, talented employees, revenue) but also things that make change very hard: org charts, distribution partners and channels, a brand reputation to protect. The core thesis: digital transformation is not about technology; at its heart it is about strategy, leadership and new ways of thinking. The hardest part is learning to reimagine what your business is and what its future will be.

### Strategic Blind Spots

Organizations make decisions based on rules, principles and strategy drawn from decades of their own experience and what leaders learned in business school years ago, and do not even recognize that these guide their assumptions. Many of those rules changed dramatically in the digital era.

### The Five Domains of Strategy, Rethought

1. **Customers.** Old: customers as passive targets. Strategy and marketing were about identifying, segmenting, reaching, distributing to and messaging customers. New: customers as networks, with dynamic, reciprocal connections to each other and to you, playing active roles in shaping and even growing your business.
2. **Competition.** Old: competition defined by industry (the basis of the classic models), against companies that look like you, with a clear line between competitors and partners. New: industry definitions are blurry; you compete across and outside industries; you compete with your closest partners and partner with your fiercest competitors. Success is about the value you can assemble, often with an ecosystem, not only what you create yourself.
3. **Data.** Old: data used to operate and optimize the current business. New: capture and combine data from across the company, link it with outside data sets, bring in structured and unstructured data, and data becomes a strategic asset and a source of new value creation.
4. **Innovation.** Old: manage the risk of doing something new by planning, with detailed business plans for ventures no one had done before, "a popular form of business fiction writing." New: innovation demands much higher speed and managing risk as a process of experimentation: continuous experimentation and learning to discover what is feasible, workable, scalable and profitable.
5. **Value.** Old: your value proposition was defined by your industry. A newspaper's value was delivering news on paper and executing well each week with the smartest reporters and editors. New: ask "What business am I in? What value do I deliver to the world, and how can I best deliver it?" Shift from a defensive posture (how does digital change impact my existing model?) to a growth mindset (how can I use these changes to create new value that was not possible before?).

### From Mass Markets to Customer Networks

In the twentieth century customers were implicitly treated as mass markets, where businesses won through economies of scale: a repeatable model, a product sold to as many customers as possible, one consistent message. Up to the nineties, business schools taught Integrated Marketing Communications: always say the same thing at every touch point, or you confuse customers and dilute the message. That is the opposite of how marketing works today. Now it is about customers' interactions and dynamic behaviors: each customer or potential customer is a node in a connected environment where they learn and discover, purchase, influence others, form relationships with businesses, create feedback links, co-create, and spur innovation inside companies. Businesses still innovate, deliver products and manage brands, but in a reciprocal relationship, consciously influencing and shaping the network rather than pushing things out to it.

### Five Core Customer Behaviors

Research across hundreds of highly successful digital products, services and campaigns over about fifteen years found five behaviors that drive where customers bring their attention, energy and money:

1. **Access.** Experiences that give people what they want (service, product, information) simpler, quicker, easier, on demand or self-service. From the original BlackBerry to ride-sharing, often something people already had, made more accessible.
2. **Engage.** Content that is relevant and meaningful: interesting, informative, entertaining. Content (blogs, streaming media, video, social media, photos, now virtual reality) has been a massive driver of digital behavior from the very beginning.
3. **Customize.** People do not all want the same content, information or services. We are drawn to experiences with more choice, control over options, variation and personalization: from Netflix to Amazon to the filters on a Snapchat profile.
4. **Connect.** We are social animals. From email through Friendster and Myspace to Facebook, Instagram, WeChat and WhatsApp, tools to connect, communicate and share experiences are highly compelling.
5. **Collaborate.** The hardest and most complex to tap. People want more than connecting and expressing themselves; they are drawn to the chance to work together and contribute to a shared project or goal. It can be as involved as crowdsourcing, the Linux operating system or Wikipedia, or as simple as Waze: just by driving you feed a data engine, and many users tap the screen to flag cars on the roadside, a police speed trap or fog ahead. People believe in reciprocity and like being part of something bigger; design the right mechanisms and experience.

### The Platform Model Is Supercharged

No single business model works in every case, but the digital era has supercharged platform or multi-sided business models: of companies started since the birth of the World Wide Web, eight of the ten biggest have a platform business model at their core (Section 15).

### Principles of an Effective Transformation

1. **Holistic, not siloed:** look at digital's impact across the whole organization.
2. **Business-led, not IT-led:** start from business strategy and goals.
3. **Organized around experiments and strategic bets,** accepting you will not know at the start what will work, with much less emphasis on planning, best practices and detailed multi-year charts.
4. **Driven by a growth mindset,** not only cost-cutting and efficiency: how do we drive new sources of growth?
5. **Independent but aligned:** many independent projects rather than one central planning committee, with oversight that assesses priorities and puts resources behind the most strategically important initiatives.
6. **New structures,** run not just inside traditional business units but across them, bringing in outsiders.
7. **Works well with others:** not doing it all alone; partnering with an ecosystem of startups, partners, technology, innovation and strategy.
8. **A flexible management model** able to pursue both low- and high-uncertainty challenges and opportunities. This is critical, very different from traditional large-organization management, and likely one of the most lasting changes across industries.

## 15. Platform Business Models

### What a Platform Is

**What it is.** A business that facilitates an exchange of value between two or more distinct types of customers. Forms include marketplaces (Airbnb, Uber, eBay), transaction systems (PayPal, Paytm in India), advertising-based models (Google Search, Facebook) and innovation platforms (iPhone and iOS, Android). Each brings together different parties who play different roles and each contributes much of the value. The platform creates the experience, orchestrates, facilitates, makes it seamless and easy, and attracts people; but most of the service, content or value you get on Airbnb or eBay comes from another party through the platform, not from the business itself.

**Origin of the idea.** Around 2006 to 2007, looking at new business models at a web portal, the contrast was stark: the portal thought linearly (aggregate an audience, serve it ads), while Alibaba thought about its model fundamentally differently. Around 2008 data was not yet something businesses knew how to monetize. Around 2011 the thinking condensed into the shift "from pipelines to platforms": from traditional linear models (pipelines) to platforms. In 2010 to 2011, Uber, Airbnb, Facebook and Google looked like a Silicon Valley thing; today the pattern applies to everything, across industries and governments. There are tools built for this, such as a Platform Business Model Map, the first business model canvas designed specifically for multi-sided businesses, focused on the flow of value across participants: how you create, manage and grow a platform.

### Why Competition Changed: Resources and Data

**What it is.** In the industrial economy companies competed on supply-side resources they controlled through ownership or contract, even if outside the firm's boundaries. In a connected world, whether you own a resource is secondary. The primary advantage is whether you can understand the source and allocate it.

**Why it works.** Data helps you identify, understand and allocate resources, and in effect control market interactions around them. Traditionally you had to control the resource to control the interactions; now you can control interactions by managing data. Your competitive advantage is no longer limited to what you have in-house: it can be any resource around which you can gather data, aggregate and learn from it, and manage interactions. The biggest shift: competing through access to resources without owning them, through data.

**Network effects follow.** When you do not own the resources, you can benefit from network effects because the resources lie outside your firm. Encourage interactions, third parties bring their resources, you create a network of demand, more demand pulls in even more third parties. Hotels controlled the rooms they built; Airbnb does not control those resources the same way.

**Caveat.** Asset ownership is not going away: platforms still own the most critical assets. Amazon must own its warehousing system because that is what its ecosystem of third-party merchants needs. What changes is that you can also benefit from resources you do not control internally. The combination of digitized resources and network effects is what changes competition.

### A Strategic Framework: Incentives, Control Points, Scaling Factors

**Why.** When you compete with external resources and labor rather than only internal ones, you must understand participation and collaboration: what are the right incentives for external parties to participate?

**How to do it.**

1. **Start with incentives.** Identify the economic incentives for external producers and consumers to participate in your business model. This is not so different from the traditional question of how to procure the resources you compete on.
2. **Create a control point.** Once participants participate, what few factors let you establish control over the ecosystem? Amazon creates incentives for users (Prime) and provides tools to merchants, but what holds both sides together is the warehousing network: it enables two-day delivery through Prime and Fulfillment by Amazon, so third-party merchants no longer do their own fulfillment. The warehousing network is the control point.
3. **Scale both through scaling factors.** What mechanisms will make the platform scale? First, network effects. Second, learning effects (today, machine learning): the more data you capture, the more you learn about interactions and what is happening in the market.

### Pipeline Versus Platform: Three Differences

1. **Who you serve.** A pipeline serves its end consumers. A platform serves both sides: contributors or producers, and end consumers. Test: do external parties actually contribute to your business model? If not, there is no reason to call it a platform; a platform must have external participation and contribution.
2. **Where the assets sit.** Do you control assets internally, or manage interactions around assets, resources and users outside your system? A staffing company controls the temp staff it provides; a freelancing platform like Upwork does not control those resources but connects them to clients. You do not have to be asset-light, but do you benefit from assets outside your system?
3. **What you measure.** A pipeline focuses on product or service delivery: great product, constant shipping, internal metrics. A platform tracks how third parties deliver products and services on the platform and whether they are increasing their participation.

### Platform Metrics

**What it is.** Do not track just user base size or sign-ups. Platforms are associated with millions and billions of users, so people think sign-ups prove the platform. They do not.

**How to do it.**

1. **Track the rate of successful interactions,** and minimize failed ones. Example: a ride-hailing platform launches in a new city; a user opens the app and sees no car available. That is a failed market interaction you should be supporting. Uber translates this into driver metrics: drivers must keep a minimum acceptance rate and cannot reject more than 10% of ride requests, which serves the overall metric of successful interactions.
2. **Track scaling of participation over time.** Is the ecosystem scaling around you? Is each user's participation increasing? On Instagram, a new user who posts 20 photos in the first month and around 100 photos by the fifth month is increasing participation. Look for indicators that both producers and consumers are increasing participation.

### Network Effects Versus Viral Effects

**What it is.** A common misconception treats going viral as a network effect. They differ.

- **Virality:** the more people use your product, the more they spread the message about it externally. The more someone uses Gmail, the more others see emails from Gmail and may switch. Seeing Instagram photos on Facebook exposed people to Instagram.
- **Network effects:** the more people use the platform, the more value it creates for others using it. More Airbnb hosts means more choice for travelers; more YouTube videos means more choice for viewers.

"Network effects create value on the platform. Viral effects spread the word about the platform or the product externally." Embedding a YouTube video on Facebook is not a network effect but a viral effect: a growth tool that brings external users back to the platform, whereas a network effect increases value on the platform.

### Types of Network Effects

1. **Direct network effect:** the more users like you use something, the more value you get. The telephone: the more people use telephones, the more valuable each is.
2. **Cross-sided (two-sided) network effect:** more producers attract more consumers, and more consumers attract more producers. More Airbnb hosts means more value for travelers.
3. **Negative direct effect alongside a positive cross-side effect:** more hosts in one city means more competition among hosts for travelers, so the direct effect among hosts is negative while the cross-side effect of more travelers is positive. Think these factors through.
4. **Local network effects:** more users do not add value unless they are directly connected to you. On Facebook and WhatsApp you get value from the people you are directly connected to and the people they are connected to.
5. **Standards:** technical standards like DVD or CD: the more manufacturers adopt the standard, the more movie studios adopt it, and the more users benefit. Standards manifest network effects in complex B2B, industry-level ecosystems.

Much has been written about 10, 13 or 15 types of network effects. The single biggest distinction: one-sided versus two-sided, meaning whether value comes from users just like you, or from users who consume from you or whom you consume from.

**Strength and plateaus.** Ask what makes a network effect stronger and whether it stops increasing after a certain point. For a ride-hailing app, more drivers initially make a ride more likely, but once waiting time reaches a certain level, more drivers in a city do not improve your experience; the value levels off. Long-tail platforms like eBay keep creating more choice as more varied sellers join. Know at what point your network effect levels out, or whether it keeps growing.

### Negative Network Effects

**What it is.** The more the network grows, the more value for users goes down.

**Congestion versus noise.** In the traditional world this was congestion: more cars on the highway, more traffic jams; more people in a room, the less likely a decent conversation because it is crowded, everyone talks too loudly, and you cannot find the right person. Traditional networks were limited by scale (highway network, loads in a city, size of a room or conference hall). Online, the infrastructure's scale limits go away, which is why people talk mostly about positive network effects. But negative effects still appear online, not from scale limits but from limits in quality management or curation: the more users come on board, the harder it is to manage the quality of interactions.

**How to prevent it.** Set up curation mechanisms up front, and make sure quality control scales at the rate of the network. Otherwise quality dips, people see less value and start leaving. Distinguish congestion (scale limitations) from noise (curation limitations).

### The Chicken and Egg Dilemma

**What it is.** Network effects mean users create the value, so with no users there is no value. No producers, no consumers; no consumers, no producers. Who do you get first? A classic dilemma for every platform that relies on an ecosystem to create value.

**Two fundamental solutions.**

1. **Create value without the network.** Build value into the tools or software itself. OpenTable, now a network connecting restaurants with diners, launched as restaurant management software that restaurants found valuable even with no consumers on board.
2. **Launch in a high-activity market.** Facebook launched as a closed system inside Harvard: high activity, everyone knew each other, everyone came on and started interacting. Twitter launched at South by Southwest, where people tweeted about the conference and everybody saw the tweets. PayPal launched as a payment mechanism on eBay, where transactions were already happening and just needed a way to pay. Successful platforms always find high hubs of activity: other platforms (PayPal on eBay), physical locations (Facebook at Harvard) or events (Twitter at a conference), as a way to kickstart the network effect.

### Where to Start Building a Platform

**How to do it.**

1. **Start with a clear user pain point.** The basics of business do not change. Do not build a platform because it is a good thing to build, because that is where business is headed, or because platforms attract high valuations.
2. **Find the best way to solve it.** You may realize a platform model is the way, or it may be one of several.
3. **Identify the primary side.** Who are you primarily solving the problem for, consumers or producers? Facebook primarily solves a problem for users; brands, media companies and advertisers came on board later. OpenTable primarily solves restaurant and seating management for the restaurant; connecting restaurants to diners is an additional kicker.
4. **Solve uniquely well for the primary side first,** before optimizing the experience of the secondary side. You balance incentives on an ongoing basis, but initially you must know which side drives value creation: on Facebook, consumers drive engagement and data creation; on OpenTable, restaurants create the data about which seats are available.

### Open Questions on Platforms

Active research areas include antitrust and monopoly in platform markets; how global trade and national competition change as the world moves to platforms and ecosystems; and B2B ecosystems (logistics ecosystems, financial services ecosystems for the unbanked, reshaping how pharmaceutical companies run clinical trials), including competition, commoditization and new business models further up the supply chain.

## 16. Blockchain and Ecosystem Business Models

### Three Generations of Transaction Processing

1. **Mainframe transactions:** an era still running, with systems like Sabre handling many airline and hotel reservations.
2. **Web-based transactions:** for example building eBay's web auction system. Many people said credit card transactions over the internet would never work; today every bank, insurer and government transacts via web and mobile.
3. **Blockchain:** Bitcoin appeared around 2013 to 2014; in 2014 Ethereum introduced smart contracts, and by 2015 that produced the view that blockchain could be applied to any industry. That led to enterprise efforts built on open source projects (Hyperledger and Hyperledger Fabric, a blockchain built from the ground up for business).

The core idea is as basic as teamwork: a group working together produces a better outcome than any single member working alone. "It's this decentralization that brings trust to data."

### Blockchain in a Nutshell

Every blockchain has a shared ledger that is not managed by a single administrator but by a group through a consensus process. Once transactions are agreed, they are appended to the ledger, cryptographically secured with the prior block, making a chain that resists tampering and forms an audit log that becomes the center of trust.

### What Enterprise Blockchain Adds

1. **Accountability (permissioned).** Cryptocurrency participants were anonymous for good reason: emulating cash, a bearer instrument, with proof of work making it possible to trust participants without knowing who they are. Enterprises must pass regulations (HIPAA in healthcare, GDPR for privacy and identity), so members must be accountable and known to the network.
2. **Confidentiality and privacy.** Members are known, but not everyone should see every transaction. Like channels in Slack, the ecosystem can be subdivided by topic so transactions are not broadcast to everyone.
3. **Performance and scalability.** Enterprises have an insatiable appetite for performance; a thousand transactions per second or more is a base requirement.
4. **Finality.** Once the network arrives at an answer it cannot fork; a committed transaction is final. (Public networks have had forks.)
5. **Fault-tolerant security.** Participants differ: big or small IT budgets, great or weaker security architects. The network must keep running even if some actors are sloppy, run unpatched operating systems, or have a node fail.

### The Mindset Change: Blockchain Is a Team Sport

**What it is.** Businesses have spent hundreds of years responsible for themselves and are not structured to work in teams. Even in B2B you are responsible: if you lend money relying on a third party to vet a client who turns out to be a bad actor, you cannot tell the government "it was someone I trusted in my network." You go to jail, they don't.

**"The big trick in the business playbook is to design business processes that can be worked across a team."**

**Examples of win-win networks.**

- **Shared reference data.** Financial firms each pay intermediaries to distribute an exchange's end-of-day results (for example stock prices). Nobody's reference data is better than anyone else's; everyone spends a lot distributing the same data. Distributing it among themselves could be more efficient and save money.
- **Fraud sharing.** A bank hit by fraud may want to share it with other banks so the same bad actor cannot defraud them.

These involve teams sharing information they have not shared before, or would have paid intermediaries to distribute, adding friction.

### Playbook Changes

1. **A business model shared across companies.** Initially the benefit is cost savings. By 2019, industry by industry, two or three such networks had emerged where collaboration was no longer debated.
2. **Governance.** Once you have an idea that uses the power of the group over the individual company, how do you govern it? What are the rules, who are the referees, what mechanism runs the network, what are the obligations and rewards? "Governance becomes the next big important factor in your playbook."
3. **Dream big.** Even the world's biggest food companies lack full visibility across the supply chain. In a test it took Walmart seven days to trace a packaged mango from a store back to its farm; on a blockchain network with diverse organizations sharing data (Food Trust), a foodborne illness source can be pinpointed in 2.2 seconds.

### Scope: MVP and Minimum Viable Ecosystem (MVE)

**What it is.** For any transformative idea, first ask the scope: how big a bite for the first project (your MVP, the first bold step)? Then, uniquely for blockchain: who do you take that step with? That is your minimum viable ecosystem.

**How to size it.**

- One company is hardly an ecosystem (an ecosystem of one), though some companies span many countries and regulatory jurisdictions.
- One is too small; twenty may be too big and take too long to get off the ground.
- A decentralized network brings a better trust model but is often the harder place to start.
- Starting more centralized is often better and faster. The catch: you must convince company number five that members one to four do not have an unfair advantage, and prove it as you go.
- Start with a group of three or four: just enough decentralization to move, where everyone has control but no one is exclusively in control. That is the balance in new blockchain-based business models.

**Case: a procurement network.** The best ecosystems already exist, and your best partners are ones you already have. One large company buys from about 20,000 suppliers (some companies have 100,000), and neighboring companies buy from similar suppliers. Its chief procurement officer knew many peers and worked with about a dozen other chief procurement officers to create a blockchain network for the buyer-supplier relationship. It started with three companies (the initiator and two others, plus a business partner) with good relationships, common pain, and the inefficiencies of modern-day processes. Once the CPO asked the team to prove blockchain could address those inefficiencies, they were off to the races with three.

**Group dynamics.** Even in a room of a dozen banks looking to form a consortium, which looks decentralized, two or three leaders emerge immediately; you typically start with two or three whether they are alone or within a group of 12. Starting small means you must spend more time on why a fourth would ever join: what is their obligation and reward? If the first three take all the rewards, nobody would join that club. Keep the door open, and factor that in if you start centralized. If you start decentralized, work out how you will ever reach your MVP: answering to 12 lawyers makes the MVE very slow. Bigger MVEs are usually run by a few people with strong opinions. Results vary by industry and use case.

### Is Blockchain a Platform?

It is a different type of platform. The better networks look like platforms: programmable, with well-constructed APIs. People call blockchain web 3.0, "the web of value versus the web of information," but for web 3.0 to take root it must at least be web 2.0. A really good blockchain-based platform never even says the word "blockchain"; it is just a valuable network. How it was built may be an interesting dinner conversation, but when sharing its value it may never come up. The economics differ: today's platforms are single-company-centered (Google, Amazon, Facebook centralize most profits, control and the rules of the game), whereas blockchain-based platforms favor the ecosystem, with economics more balanced around a consortium or group than an individual company.

### How Blockchain Creates Trust

Trusting a person rests on reputation: a list of trustworthy things they have done, what others say. In a blockchain, trust is mathematical and algorithmic: you trust the data because it is very hard to tamper with. A database has a single administrator and supports update and delete. A blockchain has multiple administrators (multiple businesses, each with a copy of the ledger, each weighing in on consensus, each getting a copy once they agree), and it is append-only: once a block is appended it is cryptographically linked to the previous one. That audit log is the foundation of trust: everyone agreed, and no single company went in and altered the data. "The business trust is anchored in that algorithmic computational trust... from hard-to-manage or hard-to-quantify reputational thought into highly quantifiable, algorithmic, provable thought."

### New Economics: Tokens

Much of blockchain is about new economics. In Bitcoin you give something (energy) and get a piece of a token back. Tokenization has a role in enterprise blockchain too. Digital assets are key to digital transformation, but five digital applications today represent their assets in five different ways; a token lets you reason about a digital asset across applications. The Token Taxonomy Initiative (TTI), driven from Microsoft as one of the enterprise Ethereum efforts and joined by IBM, aims to classify tokens and create a taxonomy that could apply to any technology (Hyperledger Fabric, Ethereum or anything in between), to liberate the digital assets locked inside applications. Even so, with 10 networks you get a tower of Babel effect, with no easy way for one network to reason with another. Think about the economics and how to quantify them; tokenization is key.

**The bottom line.** The best blockchain ideas start as business ideas. "Blockchain doesn't change the art of business. It just restructures it a bit around the group." Teamwork gives the best results but has been hard to enforce in business. "Businesses do work together in B2B networks, but you can't be responsible for others' actions. But in blockchain you can: the network can be a responsible entity."

### Blockchain Economics 101: Where Value Is Captured

**Fat protocols.** According to Joel Monegro, a former analyst at USV (a venture capital firm), the blockchain implies value creation in its protocols. The web allowed value to be captured at the **applications layer** (Facebook, Twitter, Google, Uber, TikTok, Netflix and many others) on top of a thin protocol layer: the **web-based economy**. In a **blockchain economy**, value might be captured by the **protocols** at the base of the blockchain (for instance Bitcoin and Ethereum), with a thin applications layer on top.

**The counter-view.** According to blockchain investor Paivinen, due to ease of forking, incentives to compete, and improved interoperability and interchangeability, protocols might get thinner in a blockchain-based economy too. A forking, interoperable and interchangeable blockchain economy stacks governance protocol, processing protocol, file storage protocol and other protocols beneath the applications layer. The marginal value of scale might be lower than in a web-based economy, where massive scale created an economic advantage. The success of blockchain will depend on its commercial viability.

- **The good:** transparency, distributed ledgers, decentralized systems.
- **The bad:** security failures, fake transactions, no commercial application yet.
- **Potential applications:** publishing, ride-sharing, internet advertising, voting, banking.

"I am excited about the move to crypto based business models supporting decentralized apps for this very reason. I think it opens up the possibility that some very large new companies will be created that innovate largely on entirely new business models." (Fred Wilson, AVC, 2019.)

### Industries Blockchain Could Disrupt (CB Insights)

- **Banking:** as a digitized, secure and tamper-proof ledger, blockchain could serve the same function, injecting enhanced accuracy and information-sharing into the financial services ecosystem.
- **Hedge funds:** backed by names including First Round Capital and Union Square Ventures, Numerai is taking the hedge fund model, employing a bunch of traders and quants, and decentralizing it.
- **Messaging apps:** Telegram is still developing its blockchain-based platform called the TON (Telegram Open Network), which extends Telegram's services into payments, file storage and censorship-proof browsing for its 200M users.
- **Voting:** in the future, blockchain tools could serve as a foundational infrastructure for casting, tracking and counting votes, potentially eliminating the need for recounts by taking voter fraud and foul play off the table.

## 17. China's Digital Economy

### Six Megatrends

**What it is.** China is big, confusing and complicated. Six major long-term economic trends (30- and 40-year trends) drive the revenue and cost structures of companies there; understand them and much of the chaos becomes understandable:

1. **Urbanization.** When China reopened to the world around 1980, about 80% of the population lived on farms and 20% in cities, roughly the inverse of most developed countries. A steady movement into cities, still happening, creates a lot of economic drive.
2. **Manufacturing scale.**
3. **Money and capital.**
4. **Rising Chinese consumers,** important mainly in the last five to 10 years.
5. **Digital China** (the internet).
6. **Brainpower Behemoth:** more and more people with advanced degrees. China is not what it was in 1990; it has engineers, PhDs, artists and many advanced skills.

Most major companies are riding one or two of these trends. The model has held up for about four to five years and most trends should run at least another decade.

**Predictable versus unpredictable.** Urbanization, manufacturing and capital were easy to predict: a straight line from 1980 to today. Rising consumers were a late bloomer: 10 years ago people did not talk about Chinese consumers, who had income and savings but were not buying much. That changed dramatically in five to 10 years; China is now the world's largest market (or top two) for autos, gaming, offline gaming, cinema and movies, one industry after another. Digital China is the most unpredictable, because crazy things happen all the time. The digital story is mostly about consumers (Alibaba, Tencent, social media), who turn out to be almost entirely digital creatures. You cannot talk about Chinese consumers without talking digital anymore; everything happens on a smartphone.

### The World's Most Complicated Consumer Market

Everyone knows there are many Chinese consumers and they are getting richer. What is underappreciated is how complicated they are. About 30 million Chinese consumers live in caves; the far West has several hundred million who look like a different country; downtown Beijing has more billionaires than New York City. When you have a lot of anything you get a lot of complexity, plus a huge spread between very developed market behavior (Beijing, Shanghai) and behavior in the fields and mountains.

**How to understand them.** Go small: study micro-populations (Chinese moms, sports enthusiasts, inland consumers, dads). Break it up that way and you find people are very, very different.

### How the Internet Affects China

- **Faster adoption.** Smartphone penetration was predictable; the enthusiasm was not. People adopt things faster than in other countries, mobile apps take off like crazy, people spend more time online and contribute, post and add content more. Chinese users are some of the world's most enthusiastic netizens.
- **Online consumers are a network.** Unlike a shopper buying apples at a supermarket, online consumers are interconnected: one person shares what another shares. With 300 or 350 million Americans you get a certain number of connections; with a billion Chinese consumers it is exponentially larger. Digital China effectively operates as a network, very rapid and very vibrant. A new app can go from nothing to 100 million users in two or three months, not seen anywhere else.

### Mobile-First Evolution and the Super App

**US path:** people got online by PC at home, discovered Gmail, a couple of years later YouTube, and slowly adopted tools over time.

**China path:** most people joined around 2009 to 2010, on smartphones only, and got every tool at once: messaging, online video, online gaming. They jumped into the deep end of the pool and only know the internet on smartphones, which, unlike a PC, you carry all day: messaging, photos, video.

**How the super app formed.**

1. **Messaging, not email.** Send an email to someone in China and wait a week; they probably will not check it. It started with QQ (Tencent's instant messaging), which led to WeChat.
2. **Payments.** Alipay and WeChat Wallet were added.
3. **Mobile e-commerce.** With messaging and mobile payment in place, e-commerce happened on your phone wherever you were.

That is the basis of the super app: people live on their smartphones and it becomes the operating system for your life. None of the three steps happened in the US: people used email rather than messaging apps (at least then), credit cards worked (not awesome, but functional) so mobile payments did not take off, and e-commerce happened on the PC. The super app emerged first in China, then Southeast Asia (Grab, Gojek), with attempts in India; it is not clear it will emerge from the US at all. Facebook was copying WeChat: consolidating messengers (WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook Messenger), then adding mobile payment (Calibra), then e-commerce. Inference from the source: that copy is doubtful to work.

### Word of Mouth, Moved Online

For about 50 years China has run on word of mouth: news was mostly state-created broadcast and media, and corporate information was not believed either, so you asked a friend who is a good doctor, what is a good product, how good that washing machine is. QQ and WeChat moved all of this online. Social and chat are a big part of e-commerce: you chat with people about what to buy, and follow online influencers on video platforms. "I don't understand this product, I don't trust anyone, I don't trust the commercial, but I trust this one person I listen to on my channel." Influencers with millions of followers discuss which handbags and lipstick to buy, and discussion forums are vibrant. Thirty years ago, if you lived in Beijing you did not know people in Guizhou or Kunming; there were no phones. The country was very fragmented, with most people in villages and farms until 1980 to 1990. The internet stitched everyone together for the first time into an ongoing online conversation that never existed before.

### Government and the Internet

There is a system of controls on media and some conversation, partly around politically sensitive topics. There is another level: the government sees its role as fostering healthy conversation and discouraging things not considered good for society (no tattoos or smoking on TV; encouraging respect for family and elders; curbing excessive online gaming or near-naked content). It is less controlling than often assumed. Transparency and control of the online world increased dramatically in the last couple of years, and the tools advanced rapidly; many are run not by the government but by tech companies, media houses and filmmakers, who know what they can and cannot include.

### Companies to Watch

- **The big five or six by market cap:** Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance, Baidu, Didi, Meituan.
- **Telcos:** Huawei, ZTE, China Mobile, China Unicom. Hardware is becoming more software-like as networks, drones and refrigerators get smart, so equipment companies moving toward software matter.
- **Pure AI companies,** mostly not yet public: Megvii, SenseTime, Hikvision.

### Voice and AI Assistants

The big challenge is natural language processing: can a computer hear and understand you? It is hard; computer vision is far more commercialized. Chinese companies, especially the big search engine Baidu, have worked on voice for a long time because typing Chinese on a phone is hard (drawing characters is a pain, unlike typing English), so people switched to voice earlier. Reported accuracy for Chinese voice input is higher than for English for that reason. Alibaba (Genie), Xiaomi and Baidu all have AI assistants, the equivalents of Alexa and Google, with three or four companies competing. Whether they get past rudimentary understanding is unknown.

### Advice for Building a Digital Business in China

Most of the cool digital work in China is consumer-focused. The B2B and enterprise side is slow and less advanced than in the US or the West; the consumer side is where China is the frontier. The most important thing is to understand digital marketing and social media: it is very aggressive, and if you are not doing it, you cannot sell anything to anyone. Many Fortune 500 companies study their China teams' digital marketing and apply the lessons to the rest of the world.

### Content-Driven Commerce

- **KOLs (key opinion leaders).** Online influencers are essentially content creators: they film videos traveling through Paris looking for handbags, then sell their own products ("buy my handbag") and also contract with major brands. Brands use them largely as a replacement for celebrities or spokespeople because KOLs move more items. Content is a very powerful, authentic way to drive e-commerce, with depth of content and thinking.
- **Short-form video** (TikTok) started as a China phenomenon and is taking off in many countries, along with **live streaming** and **micro-gifting**.
- **Podcasting** is popular and easier to monetize in China than in the US, where there is not really an ad market for podcasts.
- **Micro-gifting** is very popular: people shower online influencers and e-sports players (e-sports is huge in China) with small gifts of money and things.

### Why Short Video Won

1. **Addictive format:** 15-second videos; a new video every 15 seconds is far more addictive.
2. **Reach:** perhaps 500 million people have smartphones and data plans good enough for long-form video platforms, but all one billion WeChat users can watch short video, even a farmer with a very basic phone and a cheap data plan, because short videos take little memory.
3. **Production, not just consumption:** anyone can film short videos on a phone, so it engages a much bigger share of the network as producers. That is why it does so well in poorer countries.
4. **Advertising:** every fourth or fifth video can be a full-screen 15-second video ad, far better than a banner ad after a search query. A 15-second car ad (Hyundai) covering your whole screen beats a banner headline. It works on many dimensions and is clever as a business.

## 18. Recommended Reading

- **The Lean Startup** (Eric Ries): a big-idea book with case studies that explains the context in which these methods apply.
- **Lean Analytics** (Alistair Croll and Benjamin Yoskovitz): goes into the metrics and what you need to measure and test your business.
- **Running Lean** and **Scaling Lean** (Ash Maurya): validation techniques, then traction, staged rollout and scaling; the Lean Canvas.
- **The Right It** (Alberto Savoia): pretotyping and getting the first step, the "big bang" of an idea, right.
- **The Lean Playbook** (Dan Olsen): a playbook for the full set of steps after the first.
- **Unlearn** (Barry O'Reilly): you cannot take on new principles if you are not willing to unlearn previous ones.
- **The Business Model Book** (Adam J. Bock and co-author): definitions, RTVN, SHaRP resources.
- **The Business Model Navigator** (Oliver Gassmann, Karolin Frankenberger, Michaela Csik): the four Ws and 55+ business model patterns.
- **The Corporate Startup:** for innovation from inside a corporation.
- Steve Blank's work on customer development.
- **Unlocking the Customer Value Chain** (Thales Teixeira): decoupling and coupling.
- **The Digital Transformation Playbook** and **The Network Is Your Customer** (David L. Rogers).
- **Platform Revolution** and **Platform Scale** (Sangeet Paul Choudary).
- **Blockchain for Business** (Jerry Cuomo and co-authors).
- **The One-Hour China Book** (Jeffrey Towson and Jonathan Woetzel).
- **The Goal** (Eliyahu Goldratt): Theory of Constraints and throughput thinking.
- **Blitzscaling** (Reid Hoffman): business model innovation as a key ingredient of hypergrowth; the Blitzscaling canvas (Section 10).
- **Competitive Strategy** (Michael Porter): the Five Forces (Section 13).

On thinking: practice 10x, first-principles, non-linear thinking (Google calls it moonshot thinking) as a learnable skill; read widely across philosophy, science and engineering to find patterns, since solutions to problems are everywhere and the hard part is piecing them together into something that makes sense.
